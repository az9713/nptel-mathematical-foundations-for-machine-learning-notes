6. Inner products, orthogonality, and orthonormal coordinates

Vector addition and scalar multiplication determine which representations can be combined. They do not specify how long a representation is or whether two directions are perpendicular. That geometry requires an additional choice. A model that compares a temperature change with an energy change, or two coordinates with different units, makes such a choice whenever it computes a distance.

Keep a simple running example in mind: two measured features form $x=(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb R^2$. Ordinary Euclidean geometry treats one unit of either coordinate equally. The weighted geometry

$$\langle x,y\rangle_G=x^\top Gy,\qquad G=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$$

treats a displacement in the first coordinate as twice as long as the same numerical displacement in the second. This is a modeling choice about scale, not a consequence of vector-space axioms. Lectures 15–17 introduce inner products, orthogonal complements, and orthonormal expansions; Mathematics for Machine Learning, §§3.2–3.6 provides a reference for the geometric definitions.

6.1 An inner product is a scalar-valued operation [lecture]

On $\mathbb R^n$, the usual dot product is $x^\top y=\sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i$. It takes two vectors and returns one real scalar. It is not a vector multiplication closed inside $\mathbb R^n$. Its self-product $x^\top x=\sum_i x_i^2$ is the squared Euclidean length, so the length is $\sqrt{x^\top x}$. Confusing a norm with its square changes both units and formulas.

More generally, an inner product on a real vector space $V$ is a map $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb R$ satisfying, for every $u,v,w\in V$ and $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$:

  • Linearity in one argument: $\langle u,\alpha v+\beta w\rangle=\alpha\langle u,v\rangle+\beta\langle u,w\rangle$.
  • Symmetry: $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle$.
  • Positive definiteness: $\langle v,v\rangle\geq0$, with equality exactly when $v=0$.

Symmetry and the first axiom imply linearity in the other argument. Thus a real inner product is bilinear. In particular, $\langle0,v\rangle=0$, as follows by writing $\langle0,v\rangle=\langle0+0,v\rangle=2\langle0,v\rangle$. The pair consisting of $V$ and the specified product is an inner product space.

These axioms apply to abstract vectors as well as coordinate columns. For real matrices of a fixed shape, the Frobenius product $\langle A,B\rangle_F=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}b_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)$ is an inner product. On real continuous functions over $[-1,1]$, the rule $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-1}^1f(t)g(t)\,dt$ is another. Bilinearity and symmetry follow from integration. If a continuous $f$ is nonzero somewhere, continuity gives a nontrivial interval on which $f^2$ is bounded below by a positive number, so its integral is positive. Continuity makes the definiteness argument precise.

6.2 A coordinate basis carries a Gram matrix

Let $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ be any basis of a real inner product space. Write $u=\sum_i c_ib_i$ and $v=\sum_j d_jb_j$. Bilinearity gives

$$\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{i,j}c_id_j\langle b_i,b_j\rangle=c^\top Gd, \qquad G_{ij}=\langle b_i,b_j\rangle.$$

The Gram matrix $G$ is symmetric. Moreover, $c^\top Gc=\|\sum_i c_ib_i\|^2>0$ for every nonzero coordinate vector $c$, because a basis cannot represent zero with nonzero coefficients. Thus $G$ is positive definite. Conversely, any real symmetric matrix with $c^\top Gc>0$ for every $c\ne0$ defines an inner product by $c^\top Gd$: symmetry, bilinearity, and positive definiteness follow directly.

For an arbitrary proposed matrix, positive quadratic values alone do not establish symmetry of the bilinear form; the skew-symmetric part disappears from $x^\top Gx$ but affects $x^\top Gy$. Both symmetry and positive definiteness are required. A diagonal matrix with positive entries works. A diagonal matrix with nonnegative entries and at least one zero entry yields a zero “length” for a nonzero coordinate direction and therefore only a seminorm, not an inner-product norm on the whole space.

The running product is ordinary Euclidean geometry after rescaling: with $L=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, $G=L^\top L$, so $\langle x,y\rangle_G=(Lx)^\top(Ly)$. The first coordinate has effectively been multiplied by two. For $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(1,-1)$, the ordinary dot product is zero, but $\langle u,v\rangle_G=3$, while $\|u\|_G=\|v\|_G=\sqrt5$. Their weighted cosine is $3/5$, rather than zero. Orthogonality depends on the stated geometry.

A change of coordinates should not accidentally change that geometry. If physical vectors have standard coordinates $x=Pc$, then $x^\top y=c^\top(P^\top P)d$. In an oblique basis, the coordinate product is therefore governed by $P^\top P$, not generally by $I$. Replacing it with $c^\top d$ changes the metric rather than merely renaming the same vectors.

6.3 Cauchy–Schwarz makes angle definitions possible [lecture]

Define $\|u\|=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}$. Positivity and absolute homogeneity follow immediately: $\|u\|\geq0$, only zero has zero norm, and $\|\alpha u\|=|\alpha|\|u\|$. The remaining norm axiom, the triangle inequality, requires a bound on cross terms.

For $v\ne0$, every real $t$ satisfies

$$0\leq\|u-tv\|^2=\|u\|^2-2t\langle u,v\rangle+t^2\|v\|^2.$$

Complete the square, or substitute the minimizing value $t_*=\langle u,v\rangle/\|v\|^2$. The resulting nonnegative remainder is

$$0\leq\|u\|^2-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle^2}{\|v\|^2}, \qquad |\langle u,v\rangle|\leq\|u\|\|v\|.$$

This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. If $v=0$ both sides are zero. Otherwise equality occurs exactly when $\|u-t_*v\|=0$, hence when $u$ is a scalar multiple of $v$. This includes opposite directions and zero vectors, not just vectors pointing the same way. For example, $u=-2v$ attains equality in the absolute-value inequality.

Expanding the squared norm of a sum now gives

$$\|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2 \leq(\|u\|+\|v\|)^2.$$

Taking nonnegative square roots proves $\|u+v\|\leq\|u\|+\|v\|$. The associated distance $d(u,v)=\|u-v\|$ is nonnegative, symmetric, zero exactly at equal points, and satisfies the triangle inequality by applying the norm inequality to $(u-w)+(w-v)$.

For nonzero real vectors, Cauchy–Schwarz puts $\langle u,v\rangle/(\|u\|\|v\|)$ in $[-1,1]$. We may therefore define the angle $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ through

$$\cos\theta=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\|v\|}.$$

In the Euclidean plane this agrees with trigonometry: substituting $u=\|u\|(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ and $v=\|v\|(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$ gives $u^\top v=\|u\|\|v\|\cos(\alpha-\beta)$. In an abstract real space, the inner product supplies the angle. Zero has no angle with another vector, because the denominator would vanish.

6.4 Orthogonality separates contributions [lecture]

Vectors $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal, written $u\perp v$, when $\langle u,v\rangle=0$. For nonzero vectors this means an angle of $\pi/2$. The algebraic definition also makes zero orthogonal to every vector, although its angle is undefined. If $u$ is orthogonal to every vector in $V$, choose $v=u$; then $\|u\|^2=0$, so $u=0$. This last conclusion relies on positive definiteness.

Orthogonality removes the cross term in a squared sum:

$$u\perp v\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2.$$

This is Pythagoras in an inner product space. For any finite pairwise orthogonal family, the same expansion gives $\|\sum_i u_i\|^2=\sum_i\|u_i\|^2$. It works for orthogonal matrices or functions just as it does for arrows.

A finite pairwise orthogonal family of nonzero vectors is independent. Indeed, if $\sum_i a_iu_i=0$, take the inner product with $u_j$ to obtain $a_j\|u_j\|^2=0$. Since $\|u_j\|^2>0$, every coefficient is zero. The nonzero condition cannot be dropped: a family containing zero is dependent even though zero is orthogonal to everything. Conversely, independent vectors can be oblique, such as $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$ in the usual plane.

6.5 An orthogonal complement uses every vector in a subspace [lecture]

For a subspace $W\subseteq V$, its orthogonal complement is

$$W^\perp=\{v\in V:\langle v,w\rangle=0\ \text{for every }w\in W\}.$$

Orthogonality to one selected vector generally is insufficient; orthogonality to a basis of $W$ is sufficient by linearity. The set contains zero. If $u,v\in W^\perp$, then for every $w\in W$, $\langle\alpha u+\beta v,w\rangle=0$, so it is a subspace. Moreover, $W\cap W^\perp=\{0\}$: a vector in the intersection is orthogonal to itself.

Suppose $V$ is finite dimensional and $W$ has a basis $w_1,\ldots,w_r$. Define a linear map $F:V\to\mathbb R^r$ by $F(v)=(\langle w_1,v\rangle,\ldots,\langle w_r,v\rangle)$. Its kernel is exactly $W^\perp$. Its restriction to $W$ has the Gram matrix of the $w_i$, which is positive definite and invertible; hence $F$ has rank $r$. Rank–nullity gives

$$\dim W^\perp=\dim V-\dim W.$$

The dimensions and zero intersection show $V=W\oplus W^\perp$. Every vector has a unique decomposition $v=w+z$, where $w\in W$ and $z\in W^\perp$. Uniqueness can also be checked directly: two decompositions would have $w-w'=z'-z$ in the intersection. The next chapter constructs $w$ as the best approximation to $v$ inside $W$.

These statements include the boundary cases $\{0\}^\perp=V$ and $V^\perp=\{0\}$. Applying the dimension formula twice also gives $(W^\perp)^\perp=W$ in finite dimensions. Infinite-dimensional spaces need additional closure and completeness hypotheses for the corresponding decomposition theorem; the finite-dimensional proof must not be transferred without them.

6.6 The four fundamental subspaces come in two ambient spaces [lecture]

Let $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ and use ordinary Euclidean products. Its kernel $\ker A$ and row space $\operatorname{range}(A^\top)$ lie in $\mathbb R^n$. Its column space $\operatorname{range}(A)$ and left kernel $\ker A^\top$ lie in $\mathbb R^m$. Spaces on opposite sides cannot be called orthogonal complements of one another unless a common ambient space has first been identified.

The key identity is $\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^\top y\rangle$. It proves a full equivalence:

$$x\perp\operatorname{range}(A^\top) \iff \langle x,A^\top y\rangle=0\ \forall y\in\mathbb R^m \iff \langle Ax,y\rangle=0\ \forall y \iff Ax=0.$$

The final step chooses $y=Ax$. Thus $(\operatorname{range}A^\top)^\perp=\ker A$, with both inclusions proved. Replacing $A$ by $A^\top$ yields $(\operatorname{range}A)^\perp=\ker A^\top$. The complement theorem consequently gives

$$\mathbb R^n=\operatorname{range}(A^\top)\oplus\ker A,\qquad \mathbb R^m=\operatorname{range}(A)\oplus\ker A^\top.$$

For $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, the kernel is the line through $z=(1,-1,1)$. Every row is perpendicular to $z$, and the two independent rows span the plane $z^\perp$. For an arbitrary input $x=(a,b,c)$, its kernel component is $\frac{a-b+c}{3}z$ and its row-space component is the difference. Multiplication by $A$ discards the former. This makes the “unobservable part” of an input an explicit orthogonal component.

The simple transpose is tied to Euclidean geometry. With domain product $x^\top Gz$ and codomain product $y^\top Hy$, the corresponding adjoint is $A^*=G^{-1}A^\top H$, because $\langle Ax,y\rangle_H=x^\top A^\top Hy=\langle x,A^*y\rangle_G$. Complement formulas use that adjoint under weighted geometry. Applying an unmodified transpose after changing the product can silently mix two geometries.

6.7 Orthonormal coordinates require only inner products [lecture]

A family $q_1,\ldots,q_r$ is orthonormal if $\langle q_i,q_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ equals one for $i=j$ and zero otherwise. Such vectors are nonzero and independent. They form a basis of their span; they form a basis of the whole $n$-dimensional space only if there are $n$ of them.

If $q_1,\ldots,q_n$ is an orthonormal basis and $v=\sum_i c_iq_i$, taking the inner product with $q_j$ isolates one coefficient:

$$\langle q_j,v\rangle=\sum_i c_i\langle q_j,q_i\rangle=c_j,\qquad v=\sum_{j=1}^n\langle q_j,v\rangle q_j.$$

The usual need to solve a coupled linear system disappears. For an orthogonal basis $u_j$ whose lengths are not one, the denominator remains: $c_j=\langle u_j,v\rangle/\|u_j\|^2$. Normalizing $q_j=u_j/\|u_j\|$ removes it.

For the ordinary product in $\mathbb R^2$, take $q_+=(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $q_-=(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. The vector $v=(3,1)$ has coordinates $c_+=2\sqrt2$ and $c_-=\sqrt2$. Reconstruction gives $(2,2)+(1,-1)=(3,1)$. Pythagoras gives the finite-dimensional Parseval identity:

$$\|v\|^2=\sum_{j=1}^n|\langle q_j,v\rangle|^2.$$

Here the equality reads $10=8+2$. With only an orthonormal subset, the sum of these squared coefficients is at most $\|v\|^2$: subtract their expansion from $v$, verify that the remainder is orthogonal to every selected vector, and apply Pythagoras. This is Bessel's inequality. Equality holds exactly when the vector lies in the selected span.

6.8 Fourier coefficients use the same orthogonality mechanism [lecture]

For real trigonometric polynomials on $[-\pi,\pi]$, choose $\langle f,g\rangle=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)g(t)\,dt$. Product-to-sum identities show that distinct sine and cosine frequencies are orthogonal: the integral of any nonconstant integer-frequency sine or cosine over a full period is zero. Under this normalization, $\|\cos nt\|=\|\sin nt\|=1$ for $n\geq1$, whereas $\|1\|=\sqrt2$. Thus the orthonormal family begins with $1/\sqrt2$, followed by $\cos t,\sin t,\cos2t,\sin2t,\ldots$.

For $f(t)=2+3\cos t-\sin2t$, its coordinates in that family are $2\sqrt2$, $3$, and $-1$ in the indicated directions. Its squared norm is therefore $8+9+1=18$. Equivalently, in the usual notation $f=a_0/2+\sum_{n\geq1}(a_n\cos nt+b_n\sin nt)$, one has $a_0=4$, $a_1=3$, and $b_2=-1$. The coefficient formulas are precisely inner products, with the constant normalization accounted for.

Finite sums require no convergence theorem. For arbitrary square-integrable functions, infinite Fourier expansion and Parseval equality additionally use completeness of the trigonometric system in the $L^2$ norm. Functions equal almost everywhere represent the same $L^2$ vector; a zero integral of $f^2$ means zero almost everywhere, not necessarily at every isolated point. Pointwise convergence is a separate question. The analogy with finite-dimensional coordinates is powerful when its topology and sense of equality are stated.

Complex vectors require one further adjustment. With the convention $\langle u,v\rangle=\overline u^\top v$, the product is conjugate-linear in the first argument, linear in the second, and conjugate symmetric. Then $\|u\|^2=\sum_i|u_i|^2$. The expression $u^\top u$ would give $1+i^2=0$ for the nonzero vector $(1,i)$, so it cannot define a complex norm. In complex Parseval identities, coefficients are squared in absolute value.

6.9 Check geometry, coordinates, and decomposition

The executable example compares two products and reconstructs both orthonormal coordinates and the row/kernel decomposition. Rounding is used only for presentation; the reconstruction checks retain floating-point precision.

import numpy as np

u = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
v = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
g = np.diag([4.0, 1.0])
print("ordinary and weighted products:", u @ v, u @ g @ v)
q = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -1.0]]) / np.sqrt(2.0)
target = np.array([3.0, 1.0])
coordinates = q.T @ target
print("orthonormal coordinates:", np.round(coordinates, 6))
print("squared norms:", round(target @ target, 6),
      round(coordinates @ coordinates, 6))
assert np.allclose(q @ coordinates, target)
a = np.array([[1.0, 1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0, 1.0]])
z = np.array([1.0, -1.0, 1.0])
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 4.0])
hidden = (x @ z) / (z @ z) * z
visible = x - hidden
print("kernel component:", hidden)
print("row-space component:", visible)
print("same measurement:", np.allclose(a @ visible, a @ x))
assert np.allclose(visible @ z, 0.0)

ordinary and weighted products: 0.0 3.0
orthonormal coordinates: [2.828427 1.414214]
squared norms: 10.0 10.0
kernel component: [ 1. -1.  1.]
row-space component: [0. 3. 3.]
same measurement: True

6.10 Six worked problems

Problem 1: Test a proposed product

On $\mathbb R^2$, decide whether $\langle x,y\rangle=x_1y_1+2x_1y_2+2x_2y_1+5x_2y_2$ is an inner product. Find a nonzero vector orthogonal to $(1,0)$.

Solution

The matrix is $G=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, which is symmetric. Its quadratic form is $x_1^2+4x_1x_2+5x_2^2=(x_1+2x_2)^2+x_2^2$, positive unless both coordinates vanish. Thus it is an inner product. Orthogonality to $(1,0)$ requires $\langle(1,0),y\rangle=y_1+2y_2=0$. Taking $y=(-2,1)$ works. This vector has ordinary dot product $-2$ with $(1,0)$, illustrating that the specified product determines the perpendicular directions.

Problem 2: Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz

For nonzero $v$, set $u=-3v$. Evaluate both sides of Cauchy–Schwarz and the real angle. Then determine whether $\|u+v\|=\|u\|+\|v\|$.

Solution

The inner product is $\langle u,v\rangle=-3\|v\|^2$, so its absolute value is $3\|v\|^2$. Also $\|u\|\|v\|=3\|v\|^2$, giving equality. The cosine is $-1$ and the angle is $\pi$. However $\|u+v\|=\|-2v\|=2\|v\|$, whereas $\|u\|+\|v\|=4\|v\|$. Thus equality in the absolute Cauchy–Schwarz bound does not force equality in the triangle inequality; the latter requires nonnegative alignment for nonzero real vectors.

Problem 3: Find an orthogonal complement

In ordinary $\mathbb R^3$, let $W=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0),(0,1,1)\}$. Find $W^\perp$ and decompose $x=(2,0,1)$ into its components in $W$ and $W^\perp$.

Solution

For $z=(a,b,c)$, the equations are $a+b=0$ and $b+c=0$. Hence $W^\perp=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1,1)\}$. Write its component as $tz$ with this generator. Orthogonality of $x-tz$ to $z$ requires $t=(x^\top z)/(z^\top z)=3/3=1$. The two components are $(1,-1,1)$ and $(1,1,0)$. Their sum is $(2,0,1)$, and their dot product is zero. Their dimensions are one and two, matching the full dimension three.

Problem 4: Orthogonal does not mean normalized

Use the orthogonal basis $u_1=(2,2)$ and $u_2=(3,-3)$ to represent $x=(4,2)$. Explain why the two dot products alone are not the coefficients.

Solution

The squared lengths are $8$ and $18$, and their dot product is zero. Thus $c_1=(u_1^\top x)/8=12/8=3/2$ and $c_2=(u_2^\top x)/18=6/18=1/3$. Reconstruction gives $(3,3)+(1,-1)=(4,2)$. The raw products $12$ and $6$ include the lengths of the selected basis vectors. Dividing by their squared lengths compensates for that scale. Pythagoras in this basis reads $\|x\|^2=c_1^2\|u_1\|^2+c_2^2\|u_2\|^2=18+2=20$.

Problem 5: Left-kernel consistency

For $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, find $\ker A^\top$. Use it to characterize which $b\in\mathbb R^3$ can equal $Ax$.

Solution

The equations $A^\top y=0$ are $y_1+y_3=0$ and $y_2+y_3=0$, so $\ker A^\top=\operatorname{span}\{(-1,-1,1)\}$. Its orthogonal complement is the column space of $A$. Therefore $b$ is attainable exactly when $-b_1-b_2+b_3=0$. Directly, $Ax=(x_1,x_2,x_1+x_2)$ gives the same condition and the unique solution $x=(b_1,b_2)$. The one-dimensional left kernel detects the single compatibility condition in the three output coordinates.

Problem 6: A finite Fourier energy calculation

With product $\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}fg$, take $f(t)=1+2\sin t+3\cos2t$. Find its orthonormal coefficients, total squared norm, and the squared norm omitted by retaining only the constant and first sine.

Solution

The normalized constant is $1/\sqrt2$, so its coefficient is $\sqrt2$. The other nonzero coefficients are $2$ for $\sin t$ and $3$ for $\cos2t$. Orthogonality gives $\|f\|^2=2+4+9=15$. The retained function is $1+2\sin t$ with squared norm $6$, and the residual is $3\cos2t$ with squared norm $9$. The residual is orthogonal to the retained span, so the energies add exactly. This is a finite identity and makes no assertion about convergence of an infinite series.

Inner products turn representations into geometric objects, and orthonormal bases make their coordinates independently measurable. The next chapter uses that geometry to construct the closest point in a subspace and to build orthonormal bases from arbitrary independent inputs.
