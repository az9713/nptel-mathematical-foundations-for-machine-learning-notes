18. Sample geometry and covariance matrices

A table of observations has two geometries. Each row is a point in feature space, useful for comparing observations. Each column is a vector in observation space, useful for comparing features. Centering, variance, and correlation connect these views. Covariance packages all the centered feature inner products into one matrix, allowing the spectral theorem from linear algebra to organize statistical variation.

The algebra below is valid for a fixed data table. Statistical claims about estimating a population covariance require an additional sampling model, which will be stated separately.

Rows are observations; columns are feature profiles Lecture 48, 05:28

Let $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ have $n$ observations as rows and $d$ features as columns. Denote row $i$ by $x_i^T$, where $x_i\in\mathbb R^d$ is a column vector, and denote column $j$ by $c_j\in\mathbb R^n$. Entry $X_{ij}$ is feature $j$ measured on observation $i$.

In feature space, the rows are $n$ points in $\mathbb R^d$. Their centroid is the sample mean vector $\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i$. In observation space, the columns are $d$ vectors in $\mathbb R^n$. Their dot products compare how features vary across matched observations. These spaces have different dimensions and different objects; moving between them should not silently transpose the meaning of a variable.

Use the following original table as a running example. Its third feature is the sum of the first two:

$$X=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&0&2\\3&2&5\\4&4&8\end{bmatrix},\qquad \bar x=\begin{bmatrix}5/2\\2\\9/2\end{bmatrix}.$$

There are four points in three-dimensional feature space, or three profiles in four-dimensional observation space. The column relationship is exact. A covariance computation should expose its associated null direction rather than fail while attempting an unnecessary matrix inverse.

Centering is an orthogonal projection in observation space Lecture 48, 19:16

Let $\mathbf1\in\mathbb R^n$ be the all-ones vector. Projecting column $c_j$ onto its span gives

$$P c_j=\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^T}{n}c_j=\bar x_j\mathbf1,\qquad P=\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^T}{n}.$$

The scalar mean $\bar x_j$ is the coefficient along $\mathbf1$, while $\bar x_j\mathbf1$ is the projected vector. Along the unit vector $\mathbf1/\sqrt n$, the signed coordinate is $\sqrt n\bar x_j$; the projection length is $\sqrt n|\bar x_j|$. Those three related quantities must not be called the same length.

The residual, or deviation vector, is $d_j=c_j-\bar x_j\mathbf1$. Its entries sum to zero, so $\mathbf1^Td_j=0$: the constant component and deviations are orthogonal. Pythagoras gives $\|c_j\|^2=n\bar x_j^2+\|d_j\|^2$. The data split into a location component and a fluctuation component.

Define the centering matrix $H=I_n-P$. Since $P^T=P$, $P^2=P$, we have $H^T=H$ and $H^2=H$. Also $H\mathbf1=0$, and $Hv=v$ for every vector whose entries sum to zero. Thus $H$ is the orthogonal projection onto the $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace $\mathbf1^\perp$. Applied to the entire table,

$$D=HX=X-\mathbf1\bar x^T.$$

Every column of $D$ is a deviation vector, and every row is an observation translated by the centroid. In our example the columns are $(-3/2,-1/2,1/2,3/2)^T$, $(0,-2,0,2)^T$, and their sum. In software one usually subtracts column means directly; forming a dense $n\times n$ centering matrix is unnecessary for a large dataset. The matrix notation explains the operation, rather than prescribing its most expensive implementation.

Sample variance and covariance are scaled inner products Lecture 48, 29:47

For $n\geq2$, define the usual sample covariance matrix as

$$S=\frac{1}{n-1}D^TD,\qquad S_{jk}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_{ij}-\bar x_j)(X_{ik}-\bar x_k).$$

The diagonal $S_{jj}$ is the sample variance $s_j^2$, and $s_j=\|d_j\|/\sqrt{n-1}$. The off-diagonal entry is the sample covariance of two feature profiles. The lectures often use denominator $n$ for empirical moments; that convention is also meaningful, but scales the matrix by $(n-1)/n$. These notes use $n-1$ for sample covariance unless explicitly labeling the empirical-distribution convention. Neither convention changes the correlation coefficient if used consistently in numerator and denominator.

For nonconstant columns,

$$r_{jk}=\frac{S_{jk}}{\sqrt{S_{jj}S_{kk}}}=\frac{d_j^Td_k}{\|d_j\|\|d_k\|}.$$

Sample correlation is exactly the cosine of the angle between centered column profiles. It lies in $[-1,1]$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. A constant feature has zero deviation vector, so its correlations are undefined. Zero dot product between uncentered columns does not imply zero centered covariance. Even zero sample correlation is a property of the observed table and does not establish population independence.

The running example gives

$$S=\begin{bmatrix}5/3&4/3&3\\4/3&8/3&4\\3&4&7\end{bmatrix}.$$

For instance the first two deviation vectors have dot product four, so their sample covariance is $4/3$. Their correlation is $4/\sqrt{5\cdot8}=2/\sqrt{10}$, a positive association without perfect alignment. The third feature is determined by both of them, explaining why three positive diagonal variances will not make the matrix invertible.

The distinction between the empirical factor $1/n$ and the corrected factor $1/(n-1)$ is noted explicitly in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, §6.4.2, printed p. 192. To justify “unbiased,” suppose the row vectors are iid with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$. The identity

$$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)^T=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)(x_i-\mu)^T-n(\bar x-\mu)(\bar x-\mu)^T$$

has expectation $n\Sigma-n(\Sigma/n)=(n-1)\Sigma$. Therefore $E[S]=\Sigma$. This proof concerns a random sample before realization; it is not an assertion that any particular $S$ equals the population matrix. Dependence or a different sample design changes the expectation calculation. The empirical law assigning mass $1/n$ to each realized row instead has covariance $D^TD/n$ exactly by definition.

A covariance matrix is the variance of every linear combination at once Lecture 49, 01:57

Let $Z=(Z_1,\ldots,Z_d)^T$ be a random column vector with finite coordinate second moments. Its mean vector is $\mu=E[Z]$, evaluated coordinatewise, and its covariance is

$$\Sigma=E[(Z-\mu)(Z-\mu)^T]=E[ZZ^T]-\mu\mu^T.$$

Entry $(j,k)$ is $\operatorname{Cov}(Z_j,Z_k)$; the diagonal contains coordinate variances. For a deterministic column $a\in\mathbb R^d$, linearity gives $E[a^TZ]=a^T\mu$. Center and square the scalar combination to obtain

$$\operatorname{Var}(a^TZ)=a^T\Sigma a=\sum_{j,k}a_ja_k\operatorname{Cov}(Z_j,Z_k).$$

Choosing every coefficient equal to one recovers the sum of all covariance entries as the variance of the coordinate sum. Diagonal contributions are variances; symmetric off-diagonal contributions appear twice. This is why simply adding coordinate variances requires zero cross covariances.

Lecture 49, 18:40. Symmetry follows from interchanging scalar factors in each covariance. Moreover $a^T\Sigma a$ is a variance, so it is nonnegative for every $a$: covariance matrices are positive semidefinite. The sample formula gives the same property directly: $a^TSa=\|Da\|^2/(n-1)\geq0$. Consequently every eigenvalue is nonnegative, because an eigenvector $q$ satisfies $q^T\Sigma q=\lambda\|q\|^2$.

Positive semidefinite does not mean positive definite. A zero eigenvalue identifies a nonzero direction with zero variance, hence an affine constraint $a^TZ=a^T\mu$ almost surely. In a fixed sample it identifies $Da=0$, a linear dependence of centered columns. Since $\operatorname{rank}(H)=n-1$,

$$\operatorname{rank}(S)=\operatorname{rank}(D)\leq\min(d,n-1).$$

Thus when $d\geq n$, the usual sample covariance is necessarily singular, even if the population covariance is positive definite. Singularity need not indicate a computational defect. In our table $a=(1,1,-1)^T$ is a null vector because the third feature is the sum of the first two.

Covariance transforms by congruence Lecture 49, 28:36

If $Y=AZ+b$ with a deterministic $m\times d$ matrix $A$ and a fixed $m$-vector $b$, then $E[Y]=A\mu+b$. Centering gives $Y-E[Y]=A(Z-\mu)$, so

$$\operatorname{Cov}(Y)=A\Sigma A^T.$$

No restriction $m\leq d$ is needed; the output can have fewer, equal, or more coordinates. The dimensions of both sides are $m\times m$. The translation changes the mean but not the covariance. This congruence formula is inspected in §6.4.4, printed p. 194.

For row-oriented data, transforming each observation by $y_i=Ax_i+b$ gives the data table $Y=XA^T+\mathbf1b^T$. Its centered table is $DA^T$, and its sample covariance is $ASA^T$. Writing $AX$ instead would generally have incompatible dimensions and the wrong meaning. This transpose is an operational consequence of recording observations as rows while mathematical observations remain column vectors.

Orthogonal diagonalization decorrelates coordinates Lecture 49, 32:39

A real symmetric covariance has an orthonormal eigenbasis: $\Sigma=Q\Lambda Q^T$, with $Q^TQ=I$ and nonnegative diagonal $\Lambda$. Repeated eigenvalues do not prevent choosing such a basis; distinct eigenvalues are not required for the spectral theorem. Individual eigenvectors within a repeated eigenspace are not unique.

Transform the centered population vector to principal coordinates $T=Q^T(Z-\mu)$. Its covariance is $Q^T\Sigma Q=\Lambda$, so its coordinates are uncorrelated and coordinate $j$ has variance $\lambda_j$. This step is both an orthogonal coordinate change and the covariance congruence calculation. A general similarity $V^{-1}\Sigma V$ is not the covariance transformation of $V^{-1}Z$ unless the transpose relation is also satisfied; arbitrary diagonalization notation must not replace the congruence rule.

A matrix whose columns are eigenvectors is traditionally called a modal matrix. For real symmetric matrices we choose it orthogonal, making inverse equal transpose. Decorrelation yields independence when the transformed vector is jointly Gaussian; it does not remove nonlinear dependence for a general law.

If every eigenvalue is positive, the whitening transform $W=\Lambda^{-1/2}Q^T(Z-\mu)$ gives covariance $I$. With zero eigenvalues, division by their square roots is undefined. One may retain only positive-variance eigenvectors and whiten in that lower-dimensional subspace. Adding a ridge $\alpha I$ stabilizes inverses, but whitening with $(\Sigma+\alpha I)^{-1/2}$ produces covariance with eigenvalues $\lambda_j/(\lambda_j+\alpha)$, not exactly identity. These are different chosen operations.

In a sample, PCA uses eigenvectors of $S$ or right singular vectors of $D$. If $D=U\Gamma V^T$, then $S=V\Gamma^T\Gamma V^T/(n-1)$; its nonzero eigenvalues are squared singular values divided by $n-1$. This links the earlier SVD chapters to statistical geometry. Maximizing variance or retaining large eigenvalues is an unsupervised criterion, not a proof of predictive relevance. The covariance also depends on feature units, so an apparently dominant direction can reflect a scale choice rather than a scientific priority.

Executable check: singular covariance can be the correct answer

The program verifies centering as a projection, computes the sample matrix, identifies the known null constraint, and whitens only its positive-variance subspace. It uses a tolerance for numerical eigenvalues. Tiny negative values near zero from roundoff would require a tolerance diagnosis, rather than a claim that exact covariance can have a genuinely negative eigenvalue.

import numpy as np
X = np.array([[1., 2., 3.], [2., 0., 2.], [3., 2., 5.], [4., 4., 8.]])
n, d = X.shape
H = np.eye(n)-np.ones((n, n))/n
D = H @ X
S = D.T @ D/(n-1)
values, Q = np.linalg.eigh(S)
assert np.allclose(H@H, H) and np.allclose(H.T, H)
assert np.allclose(D.mean(axis=0), 0)
assert np.allclose(S @ np.array([1., 1., -1.]), 0)
assert values[0] > -1e-12
positive = values > 1e-10
whitened = D @ Q[:, positive] @ np.diag(1/np.sqrt(values[positive]))
assert np.allclose(whitened.T@whitened/(n-1), np.eye(positive.sum()))
print("sample mean:", X.mean(axis=0).tolist())
print("sample covariance:")
print(np.array2string(S, precision=6, suppress_small=True))
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(values, 6).tolist())
print("centered rank, whitened dimension:",
      np.linalg.matrix_rank(D), whitened.shape[1])

Six worked problems

1. Recover the projection and its residual

For a feature column $c=(2,4,9)^T$, compute its constant projection, deviation vector, and sample variance. Verify orthogonality.

Solution

The mean is five, so the projection is $(5,5,5)^T$ and deviations are $(-3,-1,4)^T$. Their dot product is $5(-3-1+4)=0$. Squared deviation length is $9+1+16=26$, giving sample variance $26/(3-1)=13$. The empirical-distribution variance would be $26/3$. The original squared norm $4+16+81=101$ equals projection squared norm $75$ plus residual squared norm $26$.

2. Pairwise plausible correlations can be jointly impossible

Can a correlation matrix be $R=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.9&0.9\\0.9&1&-0.9\\0.9&-0.9&1\end{bmatrix}$?

Solution

Although every individual correlation is within $[-1,1]$, take $a=(-1,1,1)^T$. Direct multiplication gives $Ra=-0.8a$, so $a^TRa=-0.8\|a\|^2=-2.4$. This would be a negative variance of a standardized-variable combination, impossible. A valid correlation matrix must be positive semidefinite as a whole; pairwise bounds alone are insufficient.

3. High dimension forces sample singularity

A dataset has eight observations and twelve features. What is the largest possible sample covariance rank, and can adding more features make it invertible?

Solution

Centering puts every column into $\mathbf1^\perp\subset\mathbb R^8$, whose dimension is seven. Therefore rank is at most seven, leaving at least five zero eigenvalues in the $12\times12$ covariance. More features cannot exceed that observation-space rank. Additional independent observations, dimension reduction, or a deliberate regularization convention address different aspects of the problem; adding arbitrary columns does not create missing sample directions.

4. Transform the running table

For the running example, define two new features $y_1=x_1+x_2-x_3$ and $y_2=2x_1$. Compute their sample covariance without rebuilding the data table.

Solution

Use $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&-1\\2&0&0\end{bmatrix}$. The first row is a null direction of $S$, so the first transformed variance and both cross entries are zero. The second variance is $4S_{11}=20/3$. Thus $ASA^T=\operatorname{diag}(0,20/3)$. The new first feature is identically zero on every row. Its Pearson correlations are undefined, even though its covariances are zero.

5. Rotation and repeated eigenvalues

If a two-dimensional population has covariance $4I_2$, which orthogonal directions diagonalize it? Does that prove rotational symmetry of the entire distribution?

Solution

Every orthogonal $Q$ gives $Q^T(4I)Q=4I$, so no preferred eigenvector orientation is determined by covariance. It does not prove full distributional rotational symmetry. A law assigning equal mass to $(2\sqrt2,0)$, $(-2\sqrt2,0)$, $(0,2\sqrt2)$, $(0,-2\sqrt2)$ has this covariance but support on just four points. Rotating by an arbitrary angle changes its law. The covariance encodes second moments, not the entire geometry of probability mass.

6. Whitening a zero-variance direction

A covariance has eigenvalues nine, one, and zero. Compare retaining the first two principal coordinates and whitening them, with ridge transformation using $\alpha=1$ in all three coordinates.

Solution

In principal coordinates the covariance is $\operatorname{diag}(9,1,0)$. Dividing the first two coordinates by three and one gives a two-dimensional identity covariance. There is no third random fluctuation to rescale. Ridge inverse-square-root scaling instead gives the three-dimensional covariance $\operatorname{diag}(9/10,1/2,0)$. It is well-defined as an operator but neither full identity nor a creation of missing variation. Choosing a tolerance or ridge is a modeling and numerical decision that should be stated.

Sources and limits

Lectures 48–49 supply the two data geometries, mean projection, deviation-vector interpretation of spread and correlation, population covariance, positive semidefiniteness, affine transformation, and diagonalization. Inspected reference sections are Mathematics for Machine Learning, §§6.4.1–6.4.4, printed pp. 190–194. The centering operator, rank and denominator proofs, singular example, whitening check, and exercises are original teaching developments. A sample covariance matrix describes observed second-order structure; it does not by itself establish independence, causation, a normal population, or reliable out-of-sample geometry.
