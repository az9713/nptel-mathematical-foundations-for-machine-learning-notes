17. Concentration inequalities and the central limit theorem

A measured average latency does not directly answer how often a service misses a deadline. Nor does one observed average reveal how much another sample's average would change. This chapter separates three mathematical tools: bounds on a single variable's tail, finite-sample concentration of an average, and the limiting shape of its standardized fluctuations. Their assumptions and conclusions differ.

A probability bound is a guarantee under a stated model, not an estimate claimed to be close to the true probability. An approximation may be more accurate in a particular example while offering less protection when its assumptions are uncertain.

Tail events and threshold conventions Lecture 45, 05:23

If $F_X(t)=P(X\leq t)$, then $P(X\gt t)=1-F_X(t)$, while $P(X\geq t)=1-F_X(t^-)$, where $F_X(t^-)=P(X\lt t)$. The distinction matters at atoms. For a continuous law the two probabilities agree, but a discrete deadline or count threshold can include a nonzero point mass.

As a real threshold increases without bound, the tail probability tends to zero for every real-valued random variable. This follows from continuity of probability applied to decreasing tail events with empty intersection. It does not say how rapidly the tail decreases, nor does it imply that a mean or variance exists. Moments supply additional information from which quantitative bounds follow.

Markov's inequality: charge every exceedance a minimum cost Lecture 45, 28:55

Let $X\geq0$ almost surely and $E[X]=m\lt\infty$. For a threshold $a\gt0$, the pointwise inequality $X\geq a1_{\{X\geq a\}}$ yields, on taking expectations,

$$P(X\geq a)\leq\frac{m}{a}.$$

This proof uses neither a mass function nor a density. Every outcome beyond the threshold contributes at least $a$ to the nonnegative budget $E[X]$. Negative contributions elsewhere would invalidate that budget argument, which is why nonnegativity is essential. A known lower bound $X\geq\ell$ allows applying Markov to $X-\ell$ instead, for a threshold greater than $\ell$.

For a nonnegative latency with mean five milliseconds, the probability of exceeding or reaching twenty milliseconds is at most $5/20=1/4$. No normality or independence assumption is involved: this is a statement about a single law. It may be conservative. If the actual law is exponential with mean five, its probability of at least twenty is $e^{-4}$, much smaller. The inequality deliberately covers many other laws consistent with the same mean.

The bound is sharp when only nonnegativity and mean are known. For $0\leq m\leq a$, put mass $m/a$ at $a$ and the remaining mass at zero. The mean is $m$ and the threshold probability equals $m/a$. This is a scaled Bernoulli variable, rather than a Bernoulli variable on $\{0,1\}$ unless $a=1$.

If $m/a\gt1$, the inequality is still true but uninformative. Combine it with the universal probability bound to write $P(X\geq a)\leq\min(1,m/a)$. A right-hand side larger than one is not an invalid probability calculation; it is a weak upper bound. The same caution applies to Chebyshev's bound below.

Chebyshev's inequality is Markov applied to a squared deviation Lecture 46, 07:26

Let $X$ have finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. For any $a\gt0$, the events $|X-\mu|\geq a$ and $(X-\mu)^2\geq a^2$ are identical. Apply Markov to the nonnegative squared deviation:

$$P(|X-\mu|\geq a)\leq\frac{E[(X-\mu)^2]}{a^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}.$$

This argument permits positive and negative values of $X$ because the transformed variable is nonnegative. If $\sigma\gt0$ and $k\gt0$, set $a=k\sigma$ to obtain $P(|X-\mu|\geq k\sigma)\leq1/k^2$. Therefore the probability of being strictly inside $k$ standard deviations is at least $1-1/k^2$, useful when $k\gt1$. At $k=1$ it supplies only the trivial lower bound zero. If variance is zero, $X=\mu$ almost surely and every positive absolute threshold has tail probability zero.

A three-standard-deviation event has probability at most $1/9$ under this finite-variance statement. A normal law gives a much smaller probability, but Chebyshev does not assume a bell-shaped law. It cannot reproduce normal-specific tail probabilities from the first two moments alone.

Chebyshev's bound can also be attained. For $a\geq\sigma\gt0$, assign total mass $\sigma^2/a^2$ equally to $\mu-a$ and $\mu+a$, and put the rest at $\mu$. The mean and variance are exactly $\mu,\sigma^2$ and the outside probability equals the bound. Concentration around the mean requires a hypothesis; it cannot be asserted merely because a distribution plot looks smooth.

Markov and Chebyshev are not competing estimates of automatically identical events. Markov on a nonnegative latency bounds a one-sided deadline event; Chebyshev bounds a two-sided event around its mean. If mean is five and variance is four, the event $X\geq9$ is contained in $|X-5|\geq4$, giving a bound $1/4$. Markov gives $5/9$ for that deadline. In other parameter regimes Markov may be more useful. Compare available valid bounds on the particular event, and take their minimum.

Sampling distributions are laws of statistics before observing the data Lecture 46, 28:06

A population may mean a finite list of units or an idealized probability law for repeated measurements. A sample is generated by a selection mechanism. In the iid model, $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are mutually independent variables with the same law as $X$. A realized dataset $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ is one outcome of that random vector. It need not be a subset without repetitions: iid draws from a finite list correspond to sampling with replacement.

The sample mean $\bar X_n=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i$ is itself a random variable, because a new sample can give a new value. Its sampling distribution describes those possible values and their probabilities. The observed average $\bar x_n$ is a number, not the whole sampling distribution.

For iid observations with finite mean and variance, linearity and the vanishing cross covariances give the exact identities

$$E[\bar X_n]=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$

They hold for every positive integer $n$, not only as $n\to\infty$. Unbiasedness means the first identity; it does not say an individual observed mean equals $\mu$. The standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$ of the sample mean is its standard error when the population variance is known. Multiplying sample size by four halves this uncertainty under the iid model.

The lectures enumerate all equal-sized subsets of a small population and average their means. Under uniform sampling of subsets, symmetry also gives an unbiased mean, but the observations within a subset are dependent. Their variance includes a finite-population correction, derived in Problem 5. That example establishes an expectation identity under its selection mechanism, not the iid central limit theorem.

A finite-sample bound proves a weak law of large numbers

Apply Chebyshev to the sample mean. For every fixed $\epsilon\gt0$,

$$P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq\epsilon)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\longrightarrow0.$$

This is convergence in probability, written $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu$, a finite-variance iid version of the weak law of large numbers. The event is about a fixed error tolerance. The theorem states that its probability shrinks; it does not promise monotonic improvement along every realized sample sequence, and does not say every future sample average is inside the tolerance.

The same inequality supplies a conservative design calculation. To guarantee the displayed failure probability at most $\delta\in(0,1)$, it suffices to take $n\geq\sigma^2/(\delta\epsilon^2)$, rounding up. This is a sufficient bound given the true variance and sampling assumptions, not a necessary sample size and not a guarantee from an uncertain variance estimate plugged into the formula.

The central limit theorem concerns standardized fluctuations Lecture 47, 23:37

For iid real observations with mean $\mu$ and variance $0\lt\sigma^2\lt\infty$, define

$$Z_n=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^nX_i-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}.$$

The classical central limit theorem states that $Z_n$ converges in distribution to a standard normal variable. Explicitly, for every real $z$, $P(Z_n\leq z)\to\Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. The theorem and characteristic-function route below are checked against Scott Sheffield's MIT 18.175 Lecture 15, slides 10–11.

The centering and scaling matter. The unscaled mean converges in probability to a point; it does not approach a fixed normal law of positive variance. For suitable finite $n$, the theorem motivates approximating its distribution by $N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$. The raw sum instead has mean $n\mu$ and variance $n\sigma^2$. A count, its sum, and its average cannot be plotted on the same numerical axis without conversion.

Here is a bounded proof sketch, invoking a convergence theorem rather than claiming an elementary complete proof. For $W=(X-\mu)/\sigma$, $E[W]=0$ and $E[W^2]=1$. Its characteristic function $\phi_W(t)=E[e^{itW}]$ always exists since the integrand has modulus one. The exponential expansion, with its second-order remainder controlled by an integrable multiple of $W^2$, gives $\phi_W(t)=1-t^2/2+o(t^2)$. Independence implies $\phi_{Z_n}(t)=\phi_W(t/\sqrt n)^n\to e^{-t^2/2}$. This limiting function is the standard normal characteristic function. Lévy's continuity theorem converts this convergence into convergence in distribution. The substantial invoked result is that last theorem, not the manipulation of finite moments.

The weak law concerns errors on a fixed scale; the CLT magnifies those errors by $\sqrt n$ to reveal their limiting shape. Neither is a statement that the original observations become normal. Independent normal observations have an exactly normal mean for every $n$; nonnormal observations need not have a normal mean at any finite $n$.

There is no universal sample-size cutoff

For independent Bernoulli observations, $n\bar X_n$ is exactly binomial and the sampling mean remains on a finite grid. A continuous approximation assigns zero probability to individual points, so its useful comparison is between interval or CDF probabilities, not equality of point masses. A continuity correction can improve some lattice approximations, but does not make them exact.

“Thirty observations is enough” is not a theorem. For Bernoulli success probability $0.001$ and $n=30$, the probability of no successes is $(0.999)^{30}$, greater than $0.97$. Most sample means are still exactly zero. The hypotheses of the classical CLT hold, but this sample size is far from a useful symmetric approximation. Finite variance alone supplies no universal finite-$n$ accuracy threshold or rate for every law.

Dependence can eliminate averaging entirely: if $X_i=V$ for every $i$, then $\bar X_n=V$ and variance remains $\operatorname{Var}(V)$. Identical distributions alone are insufficient. Infinite variance also falls outside the theorem stated here. There are other limit theorems for other hypotheses, but a large dataset cannot supply missing assumptions.

Executable check: exact binomial CDFs against a normal approximation

This deterministic program uses the exact binomial formula for $p=0.3$, with floating-point arithmetic, and compares CDFs at the standardized grid points. The reported maximum is over those points, not a certified supremum over every real threshold. No continuity correction or Monte Carlo sampling is used. It checks exact mean and variance identities numerically while displaying improving shape approximation in this particular family.

from math import comb, erf, sqrt
p = 0.3
for n in (5, 20, 80):
    masses = [comb(n, k)*p**k*(1-p)**(n-k) for k in range(n+1)]
    mean = sum(k*q for k, q in enumerate(masses))
    variance = sum((k-mean)**2*q for k, q in enumerate(masses))
    assert abs(sum(masses)-1) < 1e-12
    assert abs(mean-n*p) < 1e-10
    assert abs(variance-n*p*(1-p)) < 1e-10
    cumulative, gap = 0.0, 0.0
    for k, q in enumerate(masses):
        cumulative += q
        z = (k-n*p)/sqrt(n*p*(1-p))
        normal_cdf = (1+erf(z/sqrt(2)))/2
        gap = max(gap, abs(cumulative-normal_cdf))
    print(f"n={n:2d}: variance of mean={p*(1-p)/n:.6f}, "
          f"grid CDF gap={gap:.6f}")

n=20: variance of mean=0.010500, grid CDF gap=0.108010

Six worked problems

1. Diagnose a missing nonnegativity assumption

A proposed proof claims $P(X\geq2)\leq E[X]/2$ for every integrable real $X$. Disprove it and give a valid replacement.

Solution

Take $X=2$ or $-2$ with equal probability. The left side is $1/2$, while the proposed right side is zero. Negative contributions canceled the mean. A valid Markov application is $P(|X|\geq2)\leq E[|X|]/2=1$, or, since mean is zero and variance four, Chebyshev gives the same two-sided bound. Both are weak but correct.

2. Sharpness and the boundary of an event

Construct a nonnegative law with mean three attaining Markov's bound at threshold twelve. Would it attain the same bound for a strict exceedance?

Solution

Put probability $1/4$ at twelve and $3/4$ at zero. Then $E[X]=3$ and $P(X\geq12)=1/4=3/12$. But $P(X\gt12)=0$. For strict exceedance, putting the upper mass at $12+\eta$ and assigning it probability $3/(12+\eta)$ approaches $1/4$ as $\eta\downarrow0$. The supremum need not be attained for a strict event.

3. Design a conservative sample size

Under an iid model with known variance at most nine, choose a sufficient sample size for $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq0.2)\leq0.05$.

Solution

Chebyshev gives at most $9/[n(0.2)^2]=225/n$. Requiring $225/n\leq0.05$ gives $n\geq4500$. This conservative guarantee uses only a variance bound. A smaller normal-based approximation may be useful with suitable information, but cannot replace this guarantee solely by invoking a numerical sample-size rule.

4. A shared fluctuation leaves an uncertainty floor

Let $X_i=H+\epsilon_i$, where $H,\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\ldots$ are independent, mean zero, with variances $\tau^2,s^2,s^2,\ldots$. Find the variance of the sample mean.

Solution

The mean is $H+n^{-1}\sum_i\epsilon_i$. Independence gives variance $\tau^2+s^2/n$. The common term does not average away. Each $X_i$ has variance $\tau^2+s^2$, but inserting that into the iid formula would incorrectly predict $(\tau^2+s^2)/n$. The pairwise covariance $\tau^2$ accounts for the difference.

5. Sampling without replacement

A population has $N\gt1$ fixed values, mean $\mu$, and variance $v=N^{-1}\sum_j(x_j-\mu)^2$. Draw an ordered sample of $n\leq N$ distinct units uniformly. Derive the sample-mean variance.

Solution

Each draw has variance $v$. For two different positions, average the products of centered values over all distinct ordered units: $E[(X_i-\mu)(X_k-\mu)]=[\sum_{a\ne b}(x_a-\mu)(x_b-\mu)]/[N(N-1)]$. The numerator is $(\sum_a(x_a-\mu))^2-\sum_a(x_a-\mu)^2=-Nv$, so covariance is $-v/(N-1)$. Expanding the variance of the mean gives $v/n\,[1-(n-1)/(N-1)]=(v/n)(N-n)/(N-1)$. At $n=N$ the sample mean is exactly the population mean. This is a different design from independent sampling with replacement.

6. A sharper one-sided bound from the same moments

For mean-zero $Z$ with variance $\sigma^2\gt0$, derive a bound for $P(Z\geq a)$, $a\gt0$, by applying Markov to $(Z+t)^2$ with $t\geq0$.

Solution

On $Z\geq a$, $(Z+t)^2\geq(a+t)^2$, hence $P(Z\geq a)\leq(\sigma^2+t^2)/(a+t)^2$. Differentiate this ratio; the derivative has the sign of $at-\sigma^2$, so the minimizing nonnegative choice is $t=\sigma^2/a$. Substitution gives $\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+a^2)$. For mean five, variance four, and threshold nine, this gives $1/5$, improving the two-sided Chebyshev bound $1/4$. The original derivation shows why optimizing a valid transformed-variable bound can use directional information.
