2. Fields, vector spaces, and admissible representations

A category encoder represents three document types by $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, and $(0,0,1)$. An algorithm averages the first two observations and obtains $(1/2,1/2,0)$. The computation is valid real-vector arithmetic, but its result is no longer an observed category. This distinction between a space that permits operations and a subset that represents admissible observations is the reason to define the algebra before designing the algorithm.

Lectures 2–4 supply the sequence: numerical representations, scalar fields, coordinate-vector operations, abstract vector spaces, and subspaces. Examples, proofs, code, and exercises below are original teaching constructions.

2.1 A list of measurements needs an interpretation Lecture 2 · 03:24

A numerical coordinate vector is an ordered list of numbers with a specified interpretation for every position. Let $d$ be a positive integer and let $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)^\top$ denote a column with $d$ real entries. The superscript $\top$ indicates transpose. The collection of all such columns is $\mathbb R^d$. Order matters: an input schema that exchanges word count and document age describes a different representation even when the list has the same length.

If there are $n$ observations, placing one transposed observation vector in each row produces a data matrix $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$. Its entry $X_{ij}$ is measurement $j$ of observation $i$. A row lies in $\mathbb R^d$, whereas a feature column lies in $\mathbb R^n$. A linear combination of rows combines observations; a linear combination of columns combines measured features across the entire sample. The arithmetic is similar, but the objects being combined differ.

Our category encoder uses $d=3$, and its admissible observations form the set $E=\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}$ of the three displayed vectors. The ambient space is $\mathbb R^3$. An ambient space is the larger mathematical collection in which representations reside. The set of possible observations need not equal that space. This lets an algorithm use subtraction to measure differences even when a negative coordinate is not itself a valid observed count or category.

Coordinates alone also do not determine which physical combinations make sense. Adding a length to a temperature is not licensed by storing both as floating-point numbers. Componentwise addition combines the same kind of measurement in the same position; comparing mixed coordinates by a distance requires a deliberate scaling convention. The vector-space structure establishes permissible abstract operations, while the application supplies their meaning.

2.2 Scalar arithmetic is part of the model Lecture 2 · 10:38

A field is a set $F$ equipped with two binary operations, addition and multiplication. A binary operation takes two elements of a specified set and returns another element of that set. The operations satisfy the following laws for all $a,b,c\in F$. Addition and multiplication are associative and commutative. There are distinct identity elements $0_F$ and $1_F$. Every $a$ has an additive inverse $-a$, and every nonzero $a$ has a multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$. Multiplication distributes over addition.

Associativity means $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$ and $(ab)c=a(bc)$. Commutativity means $a+b=b+a$ and $ab=ba$. The identities satisfy $a+0_F=a$ and $a1_F=a$. Inverses satisfy $a+(-a)=0_F$ and, for $a\ne0_F$, $aa^{-1}=1_F$. Distributivity is $a(b+c)=ab+ac$. Closure, identities, and inverses alone are not a complete field definition: the other laws are needed to rearrange and cancel expressions.

The real numbers $\mathbb R$, rational numbers $\mathbb Q$, and complex numbers $\mathbb C$ are fields under their usual arithmetic. The integers $\mathbb Z$ are not. An integer inverse of $2$ would be an integer $b$ with $2b=1$, and no such integer exists. Both $1$ and $-1$ do have integer multiplicative inverses; the failure is that every nonzero integer must have one. A single failed element disproves the field claim.

Two elementary consequences explain why inverses matter. First, $a0_F=a(0_F+0_F)=a0_F+a0_F$; adding the negative of $a0_F$ to both sides gives $a0_F=0_F$. Second, if $ab=0_F$ and $a\ne0_F$, multiply by $a^{-1}$ to obtain $b=0_F$. A field therefore has no pair of nonzero elements with zero product. These facts use the axioms rather than treating familiar real-number answers as universal arithmetic conventions.

Inverses are unique. If both $b$ and $c$ are inverses of nonzero $a$, then $b=b(ac)=(ba)c=c$. The middle equality uses associativity and the two inverse identities. This small proof identifies the permission behind cancellation in later linear algebra: division by a nonzero scalar is defined in the chosen field.

2.3 Why prime modular arithmetic works Lecture 2 · 25:50 Lecture 3 · 02:15

Fix an integer $m\ge2$. Two integers are congruent modulo $m$ when their difference is divisible by $m$. We write $a\equiv b\pmod m$. Each congruence class has one representative in $\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}$, and $\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$ denotes the set of classes. To add or multiply representatives, perform the integer operation and retain the remainder after division by $m$.

These operations do not depend on the chosen representatives. If $a'=a+rm$ and $b'=b+sm$ for integers $r,s$, then $a'+b'-(a+b)=(r+s)m$. Also, $a'b'-ab=m(rb+sa+rsm)$. Both differences are multiples of $m$. Integer associativity, commutativity, and distributivity therefore descend to congruence classes. The remaining question for a field is whether every nonzero class has an inverse.

Modulo $5$, the inverses of $1,2,3,4$ are respectively $1,3,2,4$. For example, $2\cdot3=6\equiv1\pmod5$, while $4\cdot4=16\equiv1\pmod5$. The additive inverse of $a$ is the class of $-a$; for example, $2+3\equiv0\pmod5$. The same pair can happen to be both an additive-inverse pair and a multiplicative-inverse pair, but the defining equations differ.

Modulo $6$, the nonzero classes $2$ and $3$ have product $0$. If $2$ had an inverse $b$, multiplying $2\cdot3=0$ by $b$ would give $3=0$, a contradiction between distinct congruence classes. The class $4$ also lacks an inverse. Addition still has inverses, and multiplication is still closed. Those properties do not rescue the missing multiplicative inverses.

Prime-modulus criterion, proved here. The usual addition and multiplication make $\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$ a field exactly when $m$ is prime.

If $m=rs$ is composite, with $1\lt r,s\lt m$, the classes of $r$ and $s$ are nonzero while their product is zero, so no field is possible. Conversely, let $m=p$ be prime and $a$ a nonzero class. If $ak\equiv a\ell\pmod p$, then $p$ divides $a(k-\ell)$. Since $p$ is prime and does not divide $a$, it divides $k-\ell$. Multiplication by $a$ therefore maps distinct classes to distinct classes. An injective map from a finite set to itself is surjective: it has as many distinct outputs as the set has elements. Hence one of $a\cdot0,\ldots,a\cdot(p-1)$ equals $1$, supplying an inverse.

The proof uses the elementary prime-divisibility fact that a prime dividing a product divides one factor. A computational characterization for general moduli is that $a$ is invertible exactly when its greatest common divisor with $m$ is $1$. Necessity follows because a common divisor of $a$ and $m$ would divide $ab-km=1$ if an inverse existed. For sufficiency, Bézout's integer identity gives $u,v$ with $ua+vm=1$ when the greatest common divisor is $1$; reducing the identity modulo $m$ gives the inverse $u$. Bézout's identity is invoked as an integer-arithmetic result, rather than claimed as proved by a finite table.

Two-element arithmetic and binary representations

The field $\mathbb F_2=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ has just $0$ and $1$. Its addition gives $1+1=0$ and has the truth table of exclusive-or. Its multiplication has the truth table of logical and. This statement concerns two specified operations. It does not identify every operation on stored binary integers with field arithmetic: integer addition includes carries, whereas componentwise addition in $\mathbb F_2^d$ does not.

The bit strings $11$ and $01$ represent integers $3$ and $1$, whose sum is $100$. Treated as vectors in $\mathbb F_2^2$, their sum is $10$. The same symbols encode distinct objects. Physical high and low voltages implement distinguishable states; assigning the names $0$ and $1$ and choosing field operations are further representational decisions. The algebra does not determine the voltage values of a circuit.

2.4 A vector space is a set with specified operations Lecture 3 · 19:05

Let $F$ be a field. A vector space over $F$ is a nonempty set $V$ equipped with vector addition $V\times V\to V$ and scalar multiplication $F\times V\to V$. Vector addition is associative and commutative, has an identity $0_V$, and supplies an additive inverse for every vector. For all $u,v\in V$ and $a,b\in F$, the remaining laws are

$$a(u+v)=au+av,\qquad (a+b)u=au+bu,\qquad a(bu)=(ab)u,\qquad 1_Fu=u.$$

The zero scalar $0_F$ and zero vector $0_V$ belong to different sets. Their notation is often shortened to $0$ when context identifies the type. From $0_Fu=(0_F+0_F)u=0_Fu+0_Fu$, vector cancellation gives $0_Fu=0_V$. Similarly, $a0_V=0_V$, and $(-1_F)u=-u$. These relations follow from the laws. A dot product, matrix product, or componentwise product is additional structure, not part of the vector-space definition.

For $V=F^d$, coordinate addition and scaling are defined by $(u+v)_j=u_j+v_j$ and $(au)_j=au_j$. On the left, the operations take vectors as inputs; on the right, they use field arithmetic. Every axiom follows coordinate by coordinate from the corresponding scalar law. For instance, the $j$th coordinate of $a(u+v)$ is $a(u_j+v_j)=au_j+av_j$, equal to the $j$th coordinate of $au+av$.

The field is part of the specification. In $\mathbb R^2$, $(1,1)+(1,1)=(2,2)$. In $\mathbb F_2^2$, the same coordinate symbols add to $(0,0)$. Complex numbers can be treated as a vector space over $\mathbb C$ or over $\mathbb R$; the permitted scalar coefficients differ. A library's “vector” type does not by itself establish which field or operations a theorem assumes.

The reference definition in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, Section 2.4.2, printed pages 37–38, uses the real field and separates vector addition from multiplication by scalars. Our modular examples extend that distinction to expose which properties belong to the coefficients. Each proof below uses its explicitly stated field.

2.5 Vectors need not be arrows Lecture 4 · 01:53

A field is a vector space over itself: vector addition is field addition, and scalar multiplication is field multiplication. The set $\mathbb R^d$ is another example. Neither observation implies that a vector must have a visible spatial direction. A polynomial, matrix, or function can be a vector when appropriate operations satisfy the same laws.

Let $\mathcal P_r$ consist of real polynomials of degree at most a fixed nonnegative integer $r$, together with the zero polynomial. Addition adds functions, and scaling multiplies coefficients. Adding two such polynomials cannot create a higher power, and scaling cannot create one either. The zero polynomial is the additive identity; negating coefficients gives inverses. The remaining laws hold pointwise by real arithmetic.

For example, with $r=2$, $f(t)=2+t^2$ and $g(t)=-2+3t-t^2$ have sum $3t$, which remains in $\mathcal P_2$. This is why “degree at most two” matters. Polynomials of degree exactly two are not closed: leading coefficients can cancel, and the zero polynomial is absent. The degree of the zero polynomial need not be assigned an ordinary nonnegative value; including it explicitly avoids a false closure argument.

For fixed positive integers $a,b$, matrices $\mathbb R^{a\times b}$ form a vector space under entrywise addition and scaling. Fixed shape is essential. The union of all square matrix sizes is not a vector space under ordinary matrix addition, since a $2\times2$ matrix and a $3\times3$ matrix cannot be added by that operation. Matrix multiplication is separate; its possible noncommutativity does not invalidate the vector-space laws.

The symmetric real $a\times a$ matrices form a smaller vector space. A matrix is symmetric when $A^\top=A$. For symmetric $A,B$ and real $\alpha,\beta$, $(\alpha A+\beta B)^\top=\alpha A^\top+\beta B^\top=\alpha A+\beta B$. Their combinations therefore stay symmetric. Products need not: $(AB)^\top=BA$, which may differ from $AB$. Closure must be checked for the operations in the definition, not every operation available on the objects.

Continuous real-valued functions on a fixed interval also form a real vector space under pointwise addition and scaling. Real sequences $(x_1,x_2,\ldots)$ form an infinite-coordinate vector space with coordinatewise operations. These examples require no infinite sums: adding two functions or sequences is defined pointwise. Convergence, lengths, and finite bases require additional questions, not changes to the algebraic definition.

2.6 Subspaces retain the same operations Lecture 4 · 10:37

A subspace $U$ of $V$ is a subset that is itself a vector space over the same field using the operations inherited from $V$. It is enough to check that $U$ is nonempty and $\alpha u+\beta v\in U$ for every $u,v\in U$ and every pair of scalars $\alpha,\beta$. This subspace test packages closure under addition and scaling into one statement.

To prove sufficiency, choose $u\in U$ and use zero coefficients to obtain $0_V\in U$. Setting one coefficient to zero gives scaling closure; setting both to one gives addition closure. A coefficient of $-1$ supplies additive inverses. The other laws already hold in $V$ and therefore hold for elements of $U$. Necessity follows from the vector-space laws in $U$. Nonemptiness matters: a statement quantified over pairs of elements alone would be vacuously true for the empty set.

For a fixed real $k$, let $L_k=\{(t,kt):t\in\mathbb R\}$. Combining $(s,ks)$ and $(t,kt)$ with coefficients $\alpha,\beta$ gives $(\alpha s+\beta t,k(\alpha s+\beta t))$, again in $L_k$. Thus every such line through the origin is a subspace of $\mathbb R^2$. The vertical axis $\{(0,t):t\in\mathbb R\}$ is also a subspace, although no finite slope $k$ represents it. A geometric parameterization must account for its omitted case.

The sets $\{0_V\}$ and $V$ are the two trivial subspaces. A plane through the origin in $\mathbb R^3$ can be written as $U=\{x:a^\top x=0\}$ for fixed nonzero $a$. For $u,v\in U$, distributivity gives $a^\top(\alpha u+\beta v)=\alpha a^\top u+\beta a^\top v=0$. Closure follows without a drawing. More generally, solutions of homogeneous linear equations form a subspace by the same argument.

An intersection of subspaces is a subspace. Each contains zero, and a linear combination of elements belonging to every constituent subspace remains in every one. A union generally fails. In $\mathbb R^2$, the horizontal and vertical axes are subspaces, but $(1,0)+(0,1)=(1,1)$ belongs to neither axis. The smallest subspace containing both is the whole plane, since any $(a,b)$ equals $a(1,0)+b(0,1)$.

The reference Section 2.4.3, printed page 39, states the inherited-operation test and homogeneous-system examples. The line, plane, and union arguments above expose which part of the definition each construction uses. They prepare the next chapter's question: how many generating vectors are necessary?

2.7 Affine and convex closure answer different questions Lecture 4 · 25:34

The category encoder lives in a vector space but its observed vectors do not form a subspace. They exclude zero and do not permit arbitrary scaling. Their probability mixtures form the probability simplex $\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb R^3:x_j\ge0,\ \sum_jx_j=1\}$. If $x,y\in\Delta$ and $0\le t\le1$, then $tx+(1-t)y$ has nonnegative coordinates summing to one. A set closed under such combinations is convex. Convex closure is weaker than vector-space closure.

Dropping nonnegativity gives $H=\{x:\sum_jx_j=1\}$, an affine set. An affine set is a translate $x_0+U$ of a subspace $U$. Here take $x_0=e_1$ and $U=\{u:\sum_ju_j=0\}$. For each $x\in H$, $x-e_1\in U$, and adding any zero-sum vector to $e_1$ gives a unit-sum vector. Affine combinations have coefficients summing to one, with negative coefficients permitted; the simplex excludes those combinations that create negative coordinates.

Subspaces do not generally partition a vector space: any two share zero and need not be disjoint. Translates of one fixed subspace, called cosets, do partition the ambient space. If $x+U$ and $y+U$ share a point, $x+u=y+v$ for some $u,v\in U$. Then $x-y=v-u\in U$, and adding an element of $U$ shows the two cosets are identical. Distinct cosets are consequently disjoint. This is the precise version of dividing a space using a subspace.

For the encoder, an arithmetic mean can represent a category mixture even when it is not a single category. A hard-label task needs a separate decision rule mapping that mixture to an admissible label. The real-vector operation is well defined; its interpretation requires an application rule. The algebra captures closure and permitted combinations. It leaves semantics, measurement uncertainty, and the objective of prediction unspecified.

2.8 Exhaustive finite checks

The following standard-library program accepts integer moduli at least two and returns one inverse or None for each residue. Invalid moduli raise ValueError. It exhaustively searches a finite set and has no file or network effects. The greatest-common-divisor assertion compares the search with a separate characterization. The finite sweep illustrates the theorem; it is not a proof for all moduli.

from math import gcd


def inverses(modulus):
    if not isinstance(modulus, int) or modulus < 2:
        raise ValueError("modulus must be an integer at least 2")
    return [next((b for b in range(modulus)
                  if (a * b) % modulus == 1), None)
            for a in range(modulus)]


for modulus in range(2, 13):
    found = inverses(modulus)
    assert all((found[a] is not None) == (gcd(a, modulus) == 1)
               for a in range(modulus))
assert inverses(5) == [None, 1, 3, 2, 4]
assert inverses(6) == [None, 1, None, None, None, 5]
print("modulo 5 inverses:", inverses(5))
print("modulo 6 inverses:", inverses(6))
print("fields among moduli 2..12:",
      [n for n in range(2, 13) if None not in inverses(n)[1:]])

modulo 5 inverses: [None, 1, 3, 2, 4]
modulo 6 inverses: [None, 1, None, None, None, 5]
fields among moduli 2..12: [2, 3, 5, 7, 11]

2.9 Problems and worked solutions

Problem 1 · A complete field test

Find every invertible class modulo $8$. Explain why closure under addition and multiplication cannot establish that this is a field. Then identify all inverses modulo $7$.

Solution

Modulo $8$, the invertible classes are $1,3,5,7$, since their squares $1,9,25,49$ leave remainder one. The classes $2,4,6$ have a common divisor greater than one with $8$ and lack inverses. Every product $2b$ is even, so it cannot differ from $1$ by a multiple of $8$. Closure holds for all residues under the modular operations, but the inverse requirement fails. Modulo $7$, the inverses of $1,2,3,4,5,6$ are $1,4,5,2,3,6$, as the products $1,8,15,8,15,36$ are each congruent to one. The prime-modulus theorem explains why every nonzero class works.

Problem 2 · The operations determine the zero

On positive real numbers, define $u\oplus v=uv$ and $a\odot u=u^a$ for real $a$. Show this is a real vector space and identify its zero vector and additive inverse.

Solution

The bijection $L(u)=\log u$ onto $\mathbb R$ satisfies $L(u\oplus v)=L(u)+L(v)$ and $L(a\odot u)=aL(u)$. Each proposed operation therefore corresponds exactly to the ordinary real vector operation; applying $L$ to each axiom proves equality, since $L$ is injective. The zero vector is $1$, since $u\oplus1=u$. The additive inverse is $1/u$, since $u\oplus(1/u)=1$. Scalar zero sends every vector to $u^0=1$, agreeing with this vector-space zero. Under ordinary addition and ordinary scalar multiplication, the positive reals would not be a real vector space. The set alone is insufficient.

Problem 3 · Matrix closure

Show symmetric $2\times2$ real matrices form a subspace. Find two whose product is not symmetric and explain why there is no contradiction.

Solution

Each matrix has the form $\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{bmatrix}$. Every real linear combination preserves its equal off-diagonal entries, and the zero matrix has this form, so the subspace test applies. Take $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$. Their product is $AB=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$, which is not symmetric. Matrix multiplication is not a required vector-space operation; its closure failure does not contradict closure under addition and scaling.

Problem 4 · Intersections and unions

For $U=\{(x,y,z):z=0\}$ and $W=\{(x,y,z):x=y\}$, describe their intersection, prove it is a subspace, and show their union is not.

Solution

Both constraints hold exactly for $(t,t,0)$, so $U\cap W=\{t(1,1,0):t\in\mathbb R\}$. Combining two such vectors gives $(\alpha s+\beta t)(1,1,0)$, still in the set, and zero belongs. For the union, $u=(1,0,0)\in U$ and $w=(0,0,1)\in W$, but their sum $(1,0,1)$ satisfies neither constraint. The intersection retains every restriction for both summands; the union lets its summands satisfy different restrictions and therefore need not preserve either under addition.

Problem 5 · Category averages and admissibility

Average $e_1,e_1,e_2,e_3$. Does the result belong to $E$, $\Delta$, and $H$? Give an affine combination in $H$ outside $\Delta$, and explain what an algorithm needs to output a single category.

Solution

The mean is $(1/2,1/4,1/4)$. It is not one-hot, so it lies outside $E$. Its nonnegative entries sum to one, placing it in $\Delta$ and $H$. The affine combination $2e_1-e_2=(2,-1,0)$ has coefficient sum one and coordinate sum one, so it lies in $H$, but its negative coordinate excludes it from $\Delta$. A category output needs a rule, such as selecting a largest coordinate with a stated tie convention. Interpreting that rule as minimizing an expected classification cost needs probability and loss assumptions; vector geometry does not supply them.

Problem 6 · Computational diagnosis

Use the inverse search at modulus $9$ to test the claim that every odd modulus defines a field. Locate the failed classes, give an algebraic counterexample, and explain the scope of the numerical test.

Solution

The classes $3$ and $6$ lack inverses modulo $9$. Their products are divisible by three and cannot be congruent to one. The relation $3\cdot3=9\equiv0\pmod9$ gives nonzero zero divisors. This one odd composite modulus disproves the universal statement. Exhaustive search certifies the inverse table for $9$, but finitely many checked moduli cannot prove a statement about every integer. The prime-modulus proof provides the general criterion and predicts the failure without a search.
