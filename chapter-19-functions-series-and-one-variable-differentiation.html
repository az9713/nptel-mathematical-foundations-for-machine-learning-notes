19. Functions, series, and one-variable differentiation

A fitted model has a parameter that can change, a loss that can be evaluated, and a rule that proposes the next parameter value. A derivative tells that rule how the loss responds locally. It does not tell it how far a finite step remains reliable. A scalar parameter will connect these questions to rates of change, polynomial approximations, and computational derivative checks.

Lecture spine: Lectures 50–51. The examples, proofs, numerical experiments, and problems are original teaching constructions. Timestamps locate related lecture topics.

19.1 A function includes its domain and codomain L50 · 09:40

A function $f:D\to C$ assigns exactly one output $f(x)\in C$ to every input $x$ in its domain $D$. The codomain $C$ is the declared output set. Its image $f(D)=\{f(x):x\in D\}$ consists of the values actually attained. For $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ defined by $f(x)=x^2$, the image is $[0,\infty)$, although the codomain is all real numbers.

This distinction affects inversion. Squaring on all real numbers has two inputs for each positive output, so it has no single-valued inverse there. Restricting its domain and codomain to $[0,\infty)$ produces the nonnegative square-root inverse. Multiple parameters producing the same model prediction create an analogous identifiability question.

Let $\theta\in\mathbb R$ be a scalar parameter and $J(\theta)=(\theta-2)^2$ a constructed training loss. An optimization procedure chooses successive values of $\theta$. Calculus describes the resulting changes in $J$; it does not establish whether the training target represents future observations. The numerical objective and the statistical task remain different objects.

19.2 A derivative defines a local linear approximation L50 · 12:41

For a real function on an open interval containing $x$, its derivative is the finite two-sided limit

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$

The increment $h$ approaches zero from both signs. At a domain boundary, a one-sided derivative must be specified separately. The quotient gives the slope between two points; the limit gives the tangent slope when it exists.

Equivalently, $f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)h+r(h)$, where $r(h)/|h|\to0$. The derivative captures the first-order change while leaving an error small relative to the increment. This proves that differentiability implies continuity: both correction terms tend to zero. The converse fails. The continuous function $|x|$ has quotient $|h|/h$ at zero, with right limit $1$ and left limit $-1$.

For the running loss, direct expansion gives $J(\theta+h)-J(\theta)=2(\theta-2)h+h^2$. Therefore $J'(\theta)=2(\theta-2)$ and the remainder is exactly $h^2$. At zero a small positive increment decreases the loss, but the exact change $-4h+h^2$ becomes positive when $h\gt4$. The derivative supplies a local approximation, not an unrestricted finite-step guarantee.

Units help interpret the same calculation. If an input measures time and an output measures distance, its derivative measures distance per time. For a loss as a function of a parameter, the units are loss per parameter unit. Rescaling the parameter can change the numerical derivative without changing the represented model. A learning rate must be interpreted with that parameterization.

19.3 Higher derivatives reveal changing sensitivity L50 · 18:08

The second derivative $f''$ differentiates the first derivative; the $k$th derivative is written $f^{(k)}$. For a position function, the first three derivatives give velocity, acceleration, and jerk. For a loss, they describe slope and changes in slope.

An interior point with $f'(x_0)=0$ is stationary. If $f''$ is continuous and positive at $x_0$, it remains positive nearby. Thus $f'$ increases through zero, and $f$ decreases before $x_0$ and increases after it. This proves a strict local minimum. Reversing the curvature sign gives a strict local maximum. A zero second derivative is inconclusive: $x^4$, $-x^4$, and $x^3$ all have first and second derivatives zero at the origin, but give a minimum, a maximum, and neither.

These are local claims. They neither compare distant minima nor characterize constrained boundaries. For $J$ restricted to $[0,1]$, the minimum occurs at $1$ although $J'(1)=-2$. The available directions differ from the unconstrained directions.

19.4 Taylor coefficients need an approximation bound L50 · 21:42

The degree-$m$ Taylor polynomial centered at $a$ is

$$T_m(a+h)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}h^k,$$

where $f^{(0)}=f$, $0!=1$, and $k!$ multiplies the positive integers through $k$. Differentiating $h^k$ exactly $k$ times gives $k!$ at zero, while all other surviving powers vanish there. This explains the coefficient formula: the polynomial matches the function's derivatives at the center. It does not yet bound the approximation away from that center.

If $f$ has $m+1$ continuous derivatives between $a$ and $a+h$, Taylor's theorem supplies an intermediate point $\xi$ with remainder $f^{(m+1)}(\xi)h^{m+1}/(m+1)!$. Therefore a bound $|f^{(m+1)}|\le M$ throughout that interval gives absolute error at most $M|h|^{m+1}/(m+1)!$. This is a theorem under smoothness assumptions, not an empirical fit. The coefficient and analyticity conventions are also specified in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §5.1.1, printed pages 142–145.

Every derivative of $e^x$ equals $e^x$. At zero its quadratic Taylor polynomial is $1+x+x^2/2$. At $x=1/2$ it gives $1.625$. Since $e^t\le e^{1/2}$ for $0\le t\le1/2$, the absolute error is bounded by $e^{1/2}(1/2)^3/6$. The executed calculation gives actual error $0.02372127$ and bound $0.03434836$. The conservative bound uses the largest possible derivative on the interval rather than assuming the unknown intermediate point.

19.5 An infinite series requires convergence to the function L50 · 29:09

The Taylor series uses the same coefficients in an infinite sum. At center zero it is called a Maclaurin series. Claiming a representation means that the finite sums converge to the function. A function that equals its Taylor series near each point is analytic. Having derivatives of every order alone does not imply this property.

For the exponential, the remainder bound tends to zero on every fixed bounded interval. The ratio of successive bounds is $|h|/(m+2)$, eventually smaller than any fixed positive number below one. Thus $e^x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k/k!$ for every real $x$.

The geometric series exposes a boundary. Multiplying its finite sum by $1-x$ cancels intermediate terms and gives $\sum_{k=0}^{m}x^k=(1-x^{m+1})/(1-x)$ for $x\ne1$. The remainder tends to zero for $|x|\lt1$, yielding $1/(1-x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k$ there. At $x=2$ the function exists but the series diverges. A correct coefficient pattern cannot justify substitution beyond its convergence domain.

Let $i^2=-1$ and let $t$ be real. Absolute convergence of the exponential series permits separating its even and odd terms, giving

$$e^{it}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^kt^{2k}}{(2k)!} +i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^kt^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} =\cos t+i\sin t.$$

The cosine and sine series can also be obtained from the cycles of their derivatives at zero. This connects exponentials with planar rotations. It does not replace the domain and differentiability conditions for arbitrary complex functions.

19.6 Differentiation preserves sums but not products L51 · 08:48

For differentiable functions $f,g$ and real constants $\alpha,\beta$, linearity of limits gives $(\alpha f+\beta g)'=\alpha f'+\beta g'$. Differentiation is a linear operator on a suitable space of functions. Multiplication follows a different rule.

Insert the local models $f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)h+o(|h|)$ and its counterpart for $g$ into their product. The coefficient of $h$ is $f'g+fg'$; products of two small increments vanish after division by $|h|$. Therefore $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$. Taking $f(x)=g(x)=x$ gives derivative $2x$, whereas the product of their derivatives is $1$. This counterexample separates a linear operator from a multiplicative one.

When $g(x)\ne0$, continuity keeps the denominator nonzero nearby. Differentiating $g(1/g)=1$ yields $(1/g)'=-g'/g^2$. Applying the product rule then gives $(f/g)'=(f'g-fg')/g^2$. The nonzero denominator belongs to the theorem's assumptions. These formulas agree with §5.1.2, printed page 145.

19.7 The chain rule follows the order of composition L51 · 19:28

The composition $f\circ g$ applies $g$ first, then $f$. If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ at $g(x)$, the first map produces increment $g'(x)h+o(|h|)$. Applying the outer local model yields first-order change $f'(g(x))g'(x)h$. Consequently $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)$. The outer derivative is evaluated at the inner output.

For $f(u)=\cos u$ and $g(x)=x^2$, the composition is $\cos(x^2)$ with derivative $-2x\sin(x^2)$. In the reverse order it is $\cos^2x$, with derivative $-2\cos x\sin x$. Changing map order changes the mathematical object. For $z=\sin\sqrt{x+1}$ on $x\gt-1$, three local derivatives give $dz/dx=\cos\sqrt{x+1}/[2\sqrt{x+1}]$.

A formal cancellation cannot replace differentiability assumptions at an intermediate point. Composing $x^2$ with the nonnegative square root gives $|x|$, which is not differentiable at zero. Away from zero the local rules apply. Multivariable chain rules and backpropagation require the same attention to evaluation points and domains.

19.8 Finite differences balance two errors L51 · 30:20

With a bounded second derivative, a forward difference has truncation error proportional to $h$. A central difference cancels even Taylor terms and has truncation error proportional to $h^2$ under a third-derivative bound. In floating-point arithmetic, subtracting nearly equal function values introduces rounding whose effect is amplified by division by $h$.

This standard-library program has no file or network effects. It checks the derivative of the exponential at zero against its exact value $1$, then verifies a cubic central-difference identity. The displayed errors are actual output; their last digits can vary with the floating-point library.

from math import exp, factorial, isclose

x = 0.5
approx = sum(x**k / factorial(k) for k in range(3))
bound = exp(x) * x**3 / factorial(3)
assert abs(exp(x) - approx) <= bound
print(f"quadratic={approx:.8f} exact={exp(x):.8f}")
print(f"error={exp(x)-approx:.8f} bound={bound:.8f}")
for h in [1e-2, 1e-4, 1e-6, 1e-8, 1e-10]:
    forward = (exp(h) - 1) / h
    central = (exp(h) - exp(-h)) / (2*h)
    print(f"h={h:.0e} forward={abs(forward-1):.3e} "
          f"central={abs(central-1):.3e}")
assert isclose((2.1**3 - 1.9**3) / 0.2, 12.01)

error=0.02372127 bound=0.03434836

The central error improves and then grows as the step becomes smaller. That implementation observation does not contradict a limit defined using exact real arithmetic. An optimizer's numerical derivatives require checks across step sizes, not faith in the smallest available increment.

19.9 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Exact difference errors

For $f(x)=x^3$, derive forward and central difference errors at a general $x$.

Solution

Expansion gives $(x+h)^3-x^3=3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3$. The forward quotient is $3x^2+3xh+h^2$, with error $3xh+h^2$. The difference $(x+h)^3-(x-h)^3$ is $6x^2h+2h^3$, so the central quotient is $3x^2+h^2$, with error $h^2$. At $x=2,h=0.1$ it equals $12.01$, as checked by the program. The first-order term cancels by symmetry; it also vanishes accidentally in the forward method at $x=0$.

Problem 2 · Composition order

Let $f(u)=e^u$ and $g(x)=x^2+1$. Derive both composite derivatives and compare the functions at zero.

Solution

The first composition is $e^{x^2+1}$, with derivative $2xe^{x^2+1}$. The reversed composition is $e^{2x}+1$, with derivative $2e^{2x}$. At zero their values are $e$ and $2$, and their derivatives are zero and two. A product of local derivatives must retain both map order and evaluation points.

Problem 3 · Uniform approximation

Find an order $m$ guaranteeing error below $10^{-3}$ when approximating $1/(1-x)$ by its first $m+1$ geometric terms for every $|x|\le1/2$.

Solution

The exact remainder is $x^{m+1}/(1-x)$. Its magnitude is at most $2(1/2)^{m+1}=2^{-m}$. Thus $m=10$ suffices since $2^{-10}=1/1024\lt10^{-3}$. The bound applies uniformly to the interval. A pointwise convergence statement alone would not supply a single order working for all inputs.

Problem 4 · Smooth without analytic

Set $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}$ elsewhere. Explain why its Taylor series at zero fails to represent it at any nonzero input.

Solution

Repeated differentiation away from zero gives a polynomial in $1/x$ times $e^{-1/x^2}$. For every fixed power $r$, $|x|^{-r}e^{-1/x^2}\to0$: substituting $t=1/x^2$ gives a power of $t$ times $e^{-t}$, which tends to zero. This same bound makes every successive difference quotient at zero vanish, proving inductively that all derivatives extend continuously there with value zero. All Taylor coefficients vanish. Yet $f(x)\gt0$ for nonzero $x$, so the zero series cannot represent it on any neighborhood.

Problem 5 · Choose a finite-difference scale

If each function evaluation has absolute error at most $\varepsilon$, combine a forward-difference rounding bound with a second-derivative bound $M$.

Solution

The two evaluation errors can differ by at most $2\varepsilon$, giving quotient error at most $2\varepsilon/|h|$. Taylor's theorem bounds truncation by $M|h|/2$. The combined bound is $M|h|/2+2\varepsilon/|h|$. For positive $M$ and $\varepsilon$, minimizing it gives $|h|=2\sqrt{\varepsilon/M}$. If $\varepsilon=0$ and $M\gt0$, the bound instead decreases as $|h|\to0$ and has no minimizing nonzero step. This identifies a useful scale within the assumed error model, not a universal optimum for every floating-point function evaluation.

Problem 6 · Constrained stationarity

Compare stationary and “no feasible improvement” conditions for $J(\theta)=(\theta-2)^2$ on $\mathbb R$ and on $[0,1]$.

Solution

On $\mathbb R$, the derivative vanishes only at $2$, where the square reaches zero. On $[0,1]$ it is negative everywhere, and the constrained minimum is $1$. A feasible small increment $h$ from that endpoint is nonpositive. Its exact loss change is $-2h+h^2\ge0$. There is no feasible local improvement even though the ordinary derivative is $-2$. A zero-gradient stopping condition alone is inappropriate at that constrained boundary.
