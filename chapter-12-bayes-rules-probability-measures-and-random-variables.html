12. Bayes rules, probability measures, and random variables

A fault detector raises an alarm on 90% of faulty devices and only 5% of ordinary devices. An alarm sounds. Is the device probably faulty? The answer depends on how often faults occur before the alarm is observed. Reversing a conditional probability requires the base rate, a model of every route to the observation, and a precise statement of what the observation means.

Recording spine: Lectures 32–34. The detector example and finite measurable-space constructions are original. A hypothesis in Bayes' rule need not be a physical cause. The mathematical update alone does not establish a causal explanation.

12.1 A partition accounts for every route to an observation Lecture 32 · 01:51

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$ be a probability space. A finite or countable partition $H_1,H_2,\ldots$ consists of disjoint events whose union is $\Omega$. Think of the $H_j$ as mutually exclusive operating regimes. For an observed event $B$, its pieces $B\cap H_j$ are also disjoint and have union $B$. Countable additivity therefore gives $P(B)=\sum_jP(B\cap H_j)$.

When every included partition event has positive probability, use $P(B\cap H_j)=P(B\mid H_j)P(H_j)$ to obtain the law of total probability:

$$P(B)=\sum_jP(B\mid H_j)P(H_j).$$

Zero-probability partition cells contribute zero through the intersection formulation and need not receive a ratio-defined conditional probability. The weights $P(H_j)$ sum to one, so this is a weighted average of the regime-specific observation probabilities. Adding those conditional probabilities without their regime weights would describe a different experiment.

Let $F$ mean fault and $N=F^c$ ordinary operation. Suppose $P(F)=0.02$, $P(N)=0.98$, $P(B\mid F)=0.9$, and $P(B\mid N)=0.05$, where $B$ is an alarm. Then

$$P(B)=0.9(0.02)+0.05(0.98)=0.018+0.049=0.067.$$

Most alarms in this model arise from ordinary devices because ordinary devices are much more common. If there were additional regimes, they would need their own disjoint cells or an explicit catch-all cell. A list of possible causes that can happen simultaneously is not automatically a partition. One can instead partition by all joint combinations, or specify a rule that assigns each outcome to exactly one category.

12.2 Reverse the conditional with Bayes' rule Lecture 32 · 22:07

For $P(B)\gt0$, conditional probability gives $P(H_k\mid B)=P(H_k\cap B)/P(B)$. Substituting the product rule in the numerator and total probability in the denominator yields

$$P(H_k\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid H_k)P(H_k)}{\sum_jP(B\mid H_j)P(H_j)}.$$

The prior $P(H_k)$ represents the hypothesis probability before observing $B$. The likelihood $P(B\mid H_k)$ describes how compatible the observation is with that hypothesis. The evidence $P(B)$ normalizes the products so the posterior probabilities sum to one. With the data fixed, the likelihood is viewed as a function of the hypothesis; it is not generally a probability distribution over hypotheses.

For the detector, the posterior fault probability is $0.018/0.067=18/67$, approximately $0.268657$. It is larger than the prior $0.02$ but well below one half. The conditional probability $0.9$ was a sensitivity, not the answer to the inverse question. In a modeled population of 1,000 devices, 20 are faulty and contribute 18 expected alarms; 980 ordinary devices contribute 49 expected alarms. The same ratio is $18/(18+49)$.

For two hypotheses, divide the posterior formulas to obtain an odds update:

$$\frac{P(F\mid B)}{P(N\mid B)} =\frac{P(B\mid F)}{P(B\mid N)}\frac{P(F)}{P(N)}.$$

The alarm likelihood ratio is $0.9/0.05=18$. Prior odds are $1/49$, so posterior odds are $18/49$, corresponding to probability $18/67$. The observation multiplies odds by eighteen; it does not multiply the prior probability by eighteen and stop. The denominator matters because all posterior probabilities must still sum to one.

Bayesian classification uses these posterior comparisons across labels. A decision may also involve unequal mistake costs, as in Chapter 1. The largest posterior label minimizes zero-one classification loss, but a different loss can require a different threshold. Neither the update nor the threshold proves that the supplied likelihoods and priors fit real devices.

12.3 A sigma algebra specifies which questions are observable Lecture 33 · 15:39

A measurable space is a pair $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$ in which $\mathcal F$ is a sigma algebra: it contains $\Omega$, contains the complement of every member, and contains the union of every countable sequence of members. It consequently contains the empty set. De Morgan's identity gives closure under countable intersections, because $\bigcap_jE_j=(\bigcup_jE_j^c)^c$. Differences satisfy $E\setminus F=E\cap F^c$, and symmetric differences are unions of two differences, so those are measurable too.

Consider four raw outcomes $\Omega=\{a,b,c,d\}$. Suppose a device can distinguish only the blocks $C_1=\{a,b\}$, $C_2=\{c\}$, and $C_3=\{d\}$. The measurable events are every union of these blocks. There are eight: the empty set, the three individual blocks, the three unions of two blocks, and $\Omega$. The singleton $\{a\}$ is absent because the observation cannot distinguish it from $b$.

The sigma algebra generated by a collection of sets is the smallest sigma algebra containing them. It exists because the full power set is one candidate and the intersection of all candidates is again a sigma algebra. In the finite example, unions of the three blocks form precisely that generated collection. The trivial sigma algebra $\{\varnothing,\Omega\}$ permits no nontrivial distinction, while the power set permits every distinction.

On the real line, the Borel sigma algebra is generated by open intervals. It includes the intervals and sets obtained from them by countable complement and union operations. For most elementary real-valued models, it provides the target event collection. The role of sigma algebras is closure of meaningful questions; it is not merely a requirement to place every imaginable subset in a basket.

12.4 Probability is normalized measure Lecture 33 · 28:05

A measure $\mu$ assigns each event in $\mathcal F$ a value in $[0,\infty]$, with $\mu(\varnothing)=0$ and countable additivity over disjoint events. Infinite measure is allowed; for example, the entire real line has infinite ordinary length. A measure space is the triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)$. A probability measure is a measure with total mass one, so all its event values are finite and at most one.

If a measure has finite positive total mass, normalization $P(E)=\mu(E)/\mu(\Omega)$ produces a probability measure. This does not work by dividing by an infinite total length. A uniform distribution on a finite interval is normalized length, but there is no uniform probability with constant positive density over the entire real line.

Countable additivity controls limiting events. Suppose $E_1\subseteq E_2\subseteq\cdots$, and set $E=\bigcup_nE_n$. Define disjoint increments $D_1=E_1$ and $D_n=E_n\setminus E_{n-1}$. Then $P(E)=\sum_nP(D_n)$ and $P(E_k)=\sum_{n\le k}P(D_n)$. Taking the limit of partial sums proves continuity from below: $P(E_k)\to P(E)$. For decreasing events, complementing proves continuity from above in a probability space.

This is why countability is part of the axiom. Under uniform probability on $[0,1]$, the increasing intervals $[0,1-1/n]$ have probabilities approaching one. The union misses only the endpoint one, a null event. The model consistently handles infinitely many nested questions without treating uncountable unions as ordinary sums of point masses.

12.5 A random variable transfers the probability model Lecture 34 · 18:43

A real random variable is a measurable function $X:\Omega\to\mathbb R$. Measurability means that $X^{-1}(( -\infty,t])=\{\omega:X(\omega)\le t\}$ belongs to $\mathcal F$ for every real $t$. These threshold events generate the Borel sigma algebra, so the condition suffices to make every Borel numerical question about $X$ an event.

The function is fixed; uncertainty comes from its input outcome. Once $\omega$ is realized, $x=X(\omega)$ is a numerical value. The distribution, or law, of $X$ is the induced measure $P_X$ on real events:

$$P_X(S)=P(X\in S)=P(\{\omega:X(\omega)\in S\}).$$

Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong distinguish the outcome, mapping, and induced law in Section 6.1.2, printed pages 175–177. The preimage notation does not require an inverse function: many raw outcomes may map to the same number. A random variable may describe a count, a waiting time, an indicator, a loss, or a reward; a reward interpretation is optional.

For three independent fair binary reports, $\Omega=\{0,1\}^3$ has eight equally weighted outcomes. Let $X$ count the ones. There is one sequence giving zero, three giving one, three giving two, and one giving three. Thus $P_X$ assigns masses $1/8,3/8,3/8,1/8$ to $0,1,2,3$. The count forgets order. The event $\{X=2\}$ is the preimage $\{011,101,110\}$, so its probability is obtained by adding those three atom weights.

The range $X(\Omega)$ is a set of possible mapped values, whereas the distribution records their probabilities. Range alone cannot determine whether the law is continuous, discrete, or mixed. A function can attain extra values only on null outcomes without changing its law. Chapter 13 classifies the distributions using probability mass and density properties. For an arrival count, the outcome should encode an arrival configuration over a time interval; the random variable counts it. The set of individual vehicles alone is not a sample space for all possible multi-vehicle configurations.

12.6 Verify an update and a transferred distribution

The standard-library program uses exact fractions to verify Bayes' rule and enumerates all eight three-bit outcomes. It accepts only the fixed finite constructions and has no external effects. The count-law assertion independently checks the preimage accounting; replacing ordered sequences by four equally weighted count values would fail it.

from collections import Counter
from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product

prior = [F(2, 100), F(98, 100)]
likelihood = [F(9, 10), F(5, 100)]
joint = [p * q for p, q in zip(prior, likelihood)]
evidence = sum(joint)
posterior = [p / evidence for p in joint]
assert evidence == F(67, 1000)
assert posterior[0] == F(18, 67)
assert sum(posterior) == 1
counts = Counter(sum(bits) for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=3))
law = [F(counts[k], 8) for k in range(4)]
assert law == [F(1, 8), F(3, 8), F(3, 8), F(1, 8)]
print("alarm probability:", evidence)
print("fault probability given alarm:", posterior[0])
print("count distribution:", ", ".join(map(str, law)))

alarm probability: 67/1000
fault probability given alarm: 18/67
count distribution: 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8

12.7 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · A changed base rate

Keep detector sensitivity $0.9$ and false-alarm probability $0.05$, but change the fault prior to $0.1$. Find the posterior fault probability after an alarm.

Solution

The faulty-and-alarm mass is $0.1(0.9)=0.09$, and ordinary-and-alarm mass is $0.9(0.05)=0.045$. Their sum is $0.135$, so posterior fault probability is $0.09/0.135=2/3$. The detector's conditional performance has not changed; the population mix changed. Quoting sensitivity alone cannot determine the probability a flagged item is faulty.

Problem 2 · Overlapping explanations

Two proposed failure explanations can both occur. Why is $P(B\mid H_1)P(H_1)+P(B\mid H_2)P(H_2)$ not necessarily $P(B)$? Construct a valid partition from the two explanations.

Solution

The sum counts $B\cap H_1\cap H_2$ twice and omits alarms when neither explanation holds. Partition instead into $H_1\cap H_2$, $H_1\cap H_2^c$, $H_1^c\cap H_2$, and $H_1^c\cap H_2^c$, dropping empty cells. They are disjoint and exhaustive, so total probability over those cells is valid. Whether those cells represent actual causes remains a separate scientific question.

Problem 3 · Measurability on a coarse instrument

For the chapter's blocks $\{a,b\},\{c\},\{d\}$, determine whether the function $X(a)=0$, $X(b)=1$, $X(c)=2$, $X(d)=3$ is measurable. Give a measurable repair.

Solution

The threshold preimage $\{X\le1/2\}=\{a\}$ is not a union of whole blocks, so it is not measurable. Set $X(a)=X(b)=0$, while retaining values two and three for $c,d$. Every threshold preimage now is a union of blocks. More generally, on this finite sigma algebra a real measurable function must be constant within each indistinguishable block.

Problem 4 · Generate the events

On a six-outcome space, let $E$ be the odd outcomes. List the sigma algebra generated by $E$ and explain why it is not the full power set.

Solution

The collection is $\{\varnothing,E,E^c,\Omega\}$. It contains the generator and is closed under complement and countable unions because any union of its four members is again a member. Every sigma algebra containing $E$ must contain all four, so it is minimal. It cannot distinguish two odd outcomes from one another, while the full power set contains their separate singletons and has $2^6=64$ members.

Problem 5 · Same law, different functions

For three fair bits let $X$ count ones and $Y=3-X$. Show that $X$ and $Y$ have the same law but are not the same function and are not independent.

Solution

The masses $1/8,3/8,3/8,1/8$ are symmetric around $3/2$, so replacing $k$ by $3-k$ leaves the law unchanged. At outcome $000$, however, $X=0$ and $Y=3$, so the functions differ. Their sum is always three. In particular, $P(X=0,Y=0)=0$ but $P(X=0)P(Y=0)=1/64$, proving dependence. A marginal law does not specify joint behavior.

Problem 6 · Normalization requires finite mass

Can assigning the same positive probability to every nonnegative integer define a probability law? Can ordinary length on all real numbers be normalized to a uniform probability?

Solution

If each integer has positive mass $c$, the countable sum diverges and cannot equal one. If each has zero mass, countable additivity makes total mass zero. For the real line, equal positive density gives infinite integral, while zero density gives zero integral. A uniform law can be defined on a finite interval by dividing length by its finite positive total, but this normalization cannot divide infinite length into a probability law.
