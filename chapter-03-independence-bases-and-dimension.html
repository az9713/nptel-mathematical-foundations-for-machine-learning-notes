3. Independence, bases, and dimension

A document model stores three measured features, but its third feature is exactly the sum of the first two. Adding the third column enlarges the parameter list without enlarging the set of possible training predictions. Some fitted coefficients become ambiguous. To distinguish the size of a representation from the independent information in its linear combinations, we need three separate questions: what can be generated, whether its coefficients are unique, and how many generators are necessary.

Lectures 5–6 supply the progression from linear, affine, and convex combinations to dependence, span, bases, unique coordinates, and dimension. The feature-column model, proofs, numerical sensitivity study, and problems below are original constructions.

3.1 Combining observations is a modeling choice Lecture 5 · 01:52

Let $V$ be a real vector space and let $v_1,\ldots,v_k\in V$ be a finite list. A linear combination is a vector $x=\sum_{j=1}^k a_jv_j$ with coefficients $a_j\in\mathbb R$. Closure under addition and scalar multiplication guarantees that $x\in V$. The number $k$ counts vectors being combined; it need not equal the number of coordinates in any particular representation.

The standard coordinate vector $e_j\in\mathbb R^d$ has a one in position $j$ and zeros elsewhere. Any $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)^\top$ satisfies $x=\sum_{j=1}^dx_je_j$: coordinate $i$ of the sum receives the sole nonzero contribution $x_i$. This establishes that coordinate expansion is itself a linear-combination statement. It does not make standard coordinate directions the only useful generating vectors.

Taking every coefficient equal to one gives a sum. Taking every coefficient equal to $1/k$ gives the arithmetic mean. A combination is affine when $\sum_j a_j=1$, and convex when, additionally, every coefficient is nonnegative. An arithmetic mean is convex. An affine combination may extrapolate outside the points being combined because negative coefficients are permitted.

For original constructed measurement vectors $u=(2,0)$ and $v=(0,2)$, the mean is $(1,1)$. The affine combination $2u-v=(4,-2)$ uses coefficients summing to one but lies beyond their line segment. The linear combination $u+v=(2,2)$ need not lie on their affine line at all. These are different allowable sets of outputs, not competing names for the same operation.

Affine combinations have an additional invariance. If every input is shifted by the same vector $b$, then $\sum_j a_j(v_j+b)=\sum_j a_jv_j+b$ when the coefficients sum to one. Thus averaging locations commutes with changing the origin. An unrestricted linear combination instead acquires $(\sum_j a_j)b$, so its behavior depends on the coefficient sum. The restriction has a concrete geometric meaning.

3.2 Zero combinations expose redundancy Lecture 5 · 23:27

The list $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is linearly independent when $\sum_j a_jv_j=0$ forces every $a_j=0$. It is linearly dependent when some coefficients, not all zero, give the zero vector. “Not all zero” allows individual coefficients to vanish. Requiring every coefficient to be nonzero would miss ordinary dependence involving only part of a list.

For our feature model, observations are rows and measured features are columns. Use the three feature columns in $\mathbb R^3$

$$b_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad b_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad c=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\2\end{bmatrix}=b_1+b_2.$$

The relation $b_1+b_2-c=0$ proves that the three-column list is dependent. The first two columns are independent: if $\alpha b_1+\beta b_2=0$, its first coordinate is $\alpha=0$ and its second is $\beta=0$. Three distinct directions are not enough to establish independence. The direction of their sum differs from each summand while still adding no new generating ability.

Dependence is equivalent to the ability to remove at least one vector without shrinking the span. Suppose $\sum_j a_jv_j=0$ and $a_r\ne0$. Division by $a_r$ gives $v_r=-\sum_{j\ne r}(a_j/a_r)v_j$. Any appearance of $v_r$ in another combination can therefore be replaced by the other vectors. Conversely, an expression for one vector in terms of the others can be moved to one side to give a nontrivial zero combination. The field's division rule is what makes this implication possible.

Several useful tests now have short proofs. A list containing the zero vector is dependent: assign it coefficient one and all others coefficient zero. A one-vector list is independent exactly when that vector is nonzero, since a nonzero coefficient could otherwise be divided out. Every sublist of an independent list is independent: extend a proposed relation by zeros. Every larger list containing a dependent list remains dependent for the same reason.

For two nonzero vectors, dependence means that one is a scalar multiple of the other. The multiple may be negative: $(1,0)$ and $(-2,0)$ are dependent despite pointing in opposite directions. If a zero vector is present, dependence is automatic and the geometric notion of direction is unavailable. These qualifications matter when a diagram is used as a test.

3.3 Span describes every output a linear model can produce Lecture 6 · 15:16

The span of a finite list is the set of all its linear combinations:

$$\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k) =\left\{\sum_{j=1}^k a_jv_j:a_1,\ldots,a_k\in\mathbb R\right\}.$$

It is a subspace even when the generating list is dependent. The zero coefficients supply zero. If $x=\sum_j a_jv_j$ and $y=\sum_j d_jv_j$, then $\alpha x+\beta y=\sum_j(\alpha a_j+\beta d_j)v_j$ is another listed combination. This proves closure using the subspace test. Every subspace containing all $v_j$ must contain their linear combinations; the span is consequently the smallest subspace containing them, where “smallest” refers to inclusion.

For $b_1,b_2$, any combination is $(a_1,a_2,a_1+a_2)^\top$. Conversely, every vector satisfying $x_3=x_1+x_2$ has this form, by choosing $a_1=x_1$ and $a_2=x_2$. Their span is therefore the plane $U=\{x\in\mathbb R^3:x_3=x_1+x_2\}$. The ambient space has three coordinates, but this plane needs only two freely chosen values. Adding $c$ does not change it, since $c$ is already a combination of the first two generators.

Let $A=[b_1\ b_2\ c]$ be the data matrix and let $w=(w_1,w_2,w_3)^\top$ contain feature weights. Its training prediction vector is $Aw=(w_1+w_3)b_1+(w_2+w_3)b_2$. Thus every prediction lies in $U$, and every vector in $U$ can be produced. A target such as $(0,0,1)^\top$ lies outside $U$ and cannot be fitted exactly by any weight choice. Optimizer sophistication cannot enlarge this fixed prediction class.

The reference Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, Sections 2.5–2.6.1, printed pages 40–45, separates linear independence from the generating-set property. Keeping them separate prevents two common mistakes: assuming that independent vectors span the entire ambient space, or assuming that a spanning list supplies unique coefficients.

3.4 A basis supplies existence and uniqueness Lecture 6 · 25:15

A basis of a vector space $U$ is a linearly independent list that spans $U$. When coordinates are used, its order is part of the specification. Spanning ensures that every vector has a representation, while independence ensures that the coefficients are unique. Neither property implies the other without an appropriate dimension hypothesis.

To prove uniqueness, suppose $x=\sum_j a_jb_j=\sum_j d_jb_j$ in an independent list. Subtracting gives $\sum_j(a_j-d_j)b_j=0$. Independence forces $a_j-d_j=0$ for every $j$, so the two coefficient lists coincide. Conversely, a nontrivial zero relation could be added to any coefficient list to produce another representation of the same vector. Unique representation and independence are therefore two descriptions of the same algebraic obstruction, once representability has been established.

In our plane, $\mathcal B=(b_1,b_2)$ is a basis. The vector $y=(2,3,5)^\top$ has coordinate vector $[y]_{\mathcal B}=(2,3)^\top$. The brackets mean coefficients relative to the ordered basis, not the original three measurements. Reversing the basis order reverses the coefficients. Choosing another basis changes the coefficients while leaving $y$ itself unchanged.

The three-column list $(b_1,b_2,c)$ spans the same plane but is not a basis. Every weight vector

$$w(t)=\begin{bmatrix}2-t\\3-t\\t\end{bmatrix},\qquad t\in\mathbb R,$$

produces $y$, because its two effective coefficients are $2$ and $3$. The parameter ambiguity is exactly the zero relation $(-1,-1,1)^\top$. If an additional rule chooses the smallest Euclidean coefficient length, minimize $(2-t)^2+(3-t)^2+t^2=3(t-5/3)^2+14/3$. The unique selection is $(1/3,4/3,5/3)^\top$. That choice comes from the extra norm criterion, not from the training predictions alone.

A basis is also a minimal spanning list. Removing a basis vector cannot preserve spanning, since an expression for it in the remainder would violate independence. Conversely, if a finite spanning list is dependent, the earlier removal argument produces a smaller spanning list. Repeating that removal ends with an independent spanning list. This procedure constructs a basis from any finite generating list without assuming beforehand which vectors are essential.

3.5 Why every basis has the same length Lecture 6 · 36:27

For dimension to be meaningful, the number of basis vectors must be independent of the chosen basis. The key fact is an exchange argument: if $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are independent and $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ span the same space, then $k\le r$.

Because the $u$ list spans, $v_1$ is a combination of its elements. At least one coefficient is nonzero, since $v_1\ne0$. Solve for the corresponding $u$ vector and replace it by $v_1$; the new list still spans. Next express $v_2$ in this new spanning list. Some coefficient on a remaining original $u$ must be nonzero. Otherwise $v_2$ would be a combination of $v_1$, contradicting independence. Solve for that $u$ and replace it by $v_2$.

Continue in the same way. At step $j$, independence of $v_1,\ldots,v_j$ guarantees a remaining original generator with a nonzero coefficient in the expression for $v_j$. There are only $r$ original positions available. If $k\gt r$, after $r$ replacements the next independent vector would have to lie in the span of its predecessors, a contradiction. Thus $k\le r$. The proof identifies precisely why both independence and spanning are needed.

Apply the result to two bases of lengths $k$ and $r$. Each is independent and each spans, so it gives both $k\le r$ and $r\le k$. Therefore $k=r$. Their common length is the dimension, written $\dim U$. The zero space has dimension zero, with the empty list as its basis: its only vector is the empty linear combination, interpreted as zero.

The standard list $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ spans $\mathbb R^d$ and is independent because the coordinates of $\sum_j a_je_j$ are exactly the coefficients. Hence $\dim\mathbb R^d=d$. Any independent list in $\mathbb R^d$ has at most $d$ members, by exchange with the standard spanning list. Our plane has dimension two although its vectors have three entries. Dimension counts independent generating directions, not the length of the stored representation.

An independent list can be extended to a basis in a finite-dimensional space. If it does not span, select a vector outside its span and append it. A relation with a nonzero coefficient on the new vector would express that vector through the old list, which is impossible; a relation with coefficient zero would contradict old independence. Each addition preserves independence. The dimension bound prevents indefinite extension, so the process ends with a basis.

There are many bases. In a nonzero real space, replacing the first basis vector by $tb_1$ for any nonzero real $t$ preserves independence and spanning. Distinct choices give infinitely many ordered bases. This statement depends on the field and on positive dimension: a finite-field space has only finitely many vectors, while the zero space has the empty basis. The reference Section 2.6.1, printed pages 45–46, states the finite-dimensional basis and dimension properties; the exchange proof here supplies their mechanism.

A square-coordinate test Lecture 6 · 13:01

For $d$ vectors in $\mathbb R^d$, place them in the columns of a square matrix $M$. Their independence is equivalent to $\det M\ne0$, where the determinant is the alternating multilinear scalar function of the columns normalized to equal one on the identity matrix. These properties give $\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}=ad-bc$ in two dimensions. They also give zero when one column is a combination of the others: expand in that column, and every term has a repeated column, whose alternating determinant vanishes.

For the converse, an independent $d$-column list is a basis by the dimension result. The matrix therefore has an inverse $N$ whose columns give the coordinates of the standard vectors. The determinant product identity $\det(MN)=\det M\det N$, obtained by multilinearly expanding the product's columns and collecting its alternating terms, gives $\det M\det N=\det I=1$. Thus $\det M$ cannot be zero. For example, the pair $(1,0)^\top,(1,\varepsilon)^\top$ has determinant $\varepsilon$, identifying exactly the value where independence fails. A rectangular generating matrix has no determinant of this form; one must examine its independent columns instead.

3.6 A basis determines a transformation only with linearity Lecture 6 · 27:07

The motivation for selecting a basis is often to understand an entire transformation by its action on a few vectors. This works exactly when the transformation preserves the relevant combinations. A map $T:U\to W$ between real vector spaces is linear if $T(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha T(x)+\beta T(y)$ for every pair of vectors and real coefficients. Repeated application gives

$$T\left(\sum_j a_jb_j\right)=\sum_j a_jT(b_j).$$

Once the basis coefficients of $x$ are known, the right side determines $T(x)$ from the basis images. For our plane, specify $T(b_1)=(1,2)^\top$ and $T(b_2)=(-1,1)^\top$. The unique linear map with these images sends $y=2b_1+3b_2$ to $2(1,2)+3(-1,1)=(-1,7)^\top$. Existence follows by defining the map on basis coefficients; uniqueness follows from the displayed preservation rule.

For an arbitrary nonlinear map, basis values are insufficient. The maps $T_0(x_1,x_2)=0$ and $T_1(x_1,x_2)=x_1x_2$ agree on the standard basis $(1,0),(0,1)$ but disagree at $(1,1)$. Thus a basis is not a statistical sampling plan for an unrestricted function. The exact inference rests on a structural law, rather than on representativeness of the selected inputs. The next chapter develops the linearity assumption and its matrix representation in detail.

3.7 Unique coordinates need not be stable

Exact independence is an algebraic yes-or-no property. Numerical recovery also asks how much a coefficient changes when the observed vector changes slightly. Consider $b_1=(1,0)^\top$ and $b_2=(1,\varepsilon)^\top$ with nonzero real $\varepsilon$. If $\alpha b_1+\beta b_2=0$, the second coordinate gives $\beta\varepsilon=0$, hence $\beta=0$ and then $\alpha=0$. The pair is independent for every nonzero $\varepsilon$.

Nevertheless, representing the small vector $(0,\delta)^\top$ requires $\beta=\delta/\varepsilon$ and $\alpha=-\delta/\varepsilon$. A measurement perturbation of size $|\delta|$ has coordinate length $\sqrt2|\delta/\varepsilon|$. As $|\varepsilon|$ decreases, the amplification grows. At $\varepsilon=0$ uniqueness fails entirely; before that limit, exact uniqueness can coexist with serious sensitivity. A numerical rank test therefore needs a scale-dependent tolerance, not an unsupported inference from distinct-looking columns.

Linear independence is also different from statistical independence. The first concerns exact equations among vectors. The second concerns probabilities of jointly occurring events. Let a random variable $Z$ take $-1,0,1$ with equal probability, and let $Q=Z^2$. The observation columns $(-1,0,1)^\top$ and $(1,0,1)^\top$ are linearly independent: their first and third rows force both coefficients to vanish. Yet $Q$ is determined by $Z$ and is not statistically independent of it. For example, knowing $Z=0$ makes $Q=0$ certain, whereas without that knowledge its probability is $1/3$. The probability chapters formalize this distinction.

3.8 Coordinates, redundancy, and sensitivity in code

This fixed-data NumPy example computes basis coordinates, checks a zero relation, and compares a redundant minimum-norm fit with its independently derived coefficients. The final loop varies a nearly dependent basis while holding the observation perturbation fixed. It has no file or network effects. The solver is used as a numerical reference; later chapters derive least squares and its minimum-norm rule.

import numpy as np

B = np.array([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [1., 1.]])
A = np.column_stack((B, B[:, 0] + B[:, 1]))
y = np.array([2., 3., 5.])
coordinates = np.linalg.solve(B[:2], y[:2])
assert np.allclose(B @ coordinates, y)
assert np.array_equal(A @ np.array([-1., -1., 1.]), np.zeros(3))
minimum_norm = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]
assert np.allclose(minimum_norm, [1 / 3, 4 / 3, 5 / 3])
print("basis coordinates:", coordinates)
print("redundant minimum-norm weights:", np.round(minimum_norm, 6))
for epsilon in [1e-2, 1e-4, 1e-6]:
    C = np.array([[1., 1.], [0., epsilon]])
    perturbed = np.linalg.solve(C, [0., 1e-6])
    assert np.allclose(C @ perturbed, [0., 1e-6])
    print(f"epsilon={epsilon:.0e}, coordinates={perturbed}")

3.9 Problems and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Spanning without uniqueness

Describe all coefficients representing $(a,b,a+b)^\top$ using $(b_1,b_2,c)$. Which target vectors have no such representation?

Solution

The coordinate equations are $w_1+w_3=a$ and $w_2+w_3=b$, while the third follows from their sum. Choose any real $t=w_3$; all solutions are $(a-t,b-t,t)^\top$. The free parameter witnesses dependence. A vector $(a,b,d)^\top$ is representable exactly when $d=a+b$, since every generated vector satisfies that equation and the choices $(a,b,0)$ generate every vector satisfying it. Thus lack of uniqueness and lack of existence are distinct questions with different conditions.

Problem 2 · Polynomial coordinates depend on the field

Show that $(1+t,1-t,t^2)$ is a basis for real polynomials of degree at most two. Express $a+bt+ct^2$ in that basis. What fails over $\mathbb F_2$?

Solution

A combination $\alpha(1+t)+\beta(1-t)+\gamma t^2$ has coefficients $(\alpha+\beta,\alpha-\beta,\gamma)$. To match $(a,b,c)$, solve $\alpha=(a+b)/2$, $\beta=(a-b)/2$, and $\gamma=c$. This supplies a unique representation for every real polynomial, proving both spanning and independence. Over $\mathbb F_2$, subtraction equals addition and $2=0$, so $1-t=1+t$. The first two proposed basis vectors coincide and their sum is zero with nonzero coefficients. Division by two is unavailable, and the list is dependent. The field is a hypothesis of the basis claim.

Problem 3 · Dimension through replacement

In a space spanned by three vectors, can four vectors be independent? Explain the obstruction using the exchange argument rather than a diagram.

Solution

Assume four independent vectors $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$ exist. Replace one generator by $v_1$ using a nonzero coefficient in its spanning expression. Express $v_2$ in that new spanning list; independence forces a nonzero coefficient on an original generator, which can be replaced. Repeat for $v_3$. The resulting three-vector list consists entirely of $v_1,v_2,v_3$ and still spans the space. It must therefore express $v_4$ as their combination, contradicting independence. The contradiction uses spanning to guarantee each expression and independence to guarantee an eligible replacement.

Problem 4 · Basis values do not identify a nonlinear map

Construct infinitely many maps $\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ agreeing on both standard basis vectors and at zero but differing elsewhere. Why would imposing linearity remove the ambiguity?

Solution

For each real $\lambda$, define $f_\lambda(x_1,x_2)=\lambda x_1x_2$. Every map vanishes at zero and at $(1,0),(0,1)$. At $(1,1)$ it equals $\lambda$, so the maps disagree as $\lambda$ varies. A linear map with zero values on both standard basis vectors must satisfy $T(x_1,x_2)=x_1T(1,0)+x_2T(0,1)=0$ for every input. Its values are then fixed by preservation of combinations. The conclusion is structural, not an empirical extrapolation from three observed input-output pairs.

Problem 5 · Sensitivity sweep

For the nearly dependent basis, derive the coordinate change caused by perturbing $(0,0)$ to $(0,\delta)$, and determine when its length exceeds one. Use $\delta=10^{-6}$ to interpret the code output.

Solution

Solving $\alpha+\beta=0$ and $\varepsilon\beta=\delta$ gives $(-\delta/\varepsilon,\delta/\varepsilon)$. Its Euclidean length is $\sqrt2|\delta/\varepsilon|$, exceeding one exactly when $|\varepsilon|\lt\sqrt2|\delta|$. With $\delta=10^{-6}$, the three coordinate changes are $(-10^{-4},10^{-4})$, $(-10^{-2},10^{-2})$, and $(-1,1)$. The last has length $\sqrt2$ despite a measurement perturbation of only $10^{-6}$. Each basis remains algebraically independent. The sweep exposes numerical sensitivity that a yes-or-no independence test would not report.

Problem 6 · Feature design and predictions

An engineer removes $c$ as redundant and keeps $b_1,b_2$. Does this change the possible training predictions? Does it establish that the third feature can always be discarded at deployment?

Solution

The training prediction space is unchanged because $c=b_1+b_2$ on the observed rows. Every old prediction $w_1b_1+w_2b_2+w_3c$ equals $(w_1+w_3)b_1+(w_2+w_3)b_2$, and every reduced-model prediction is obtained in the old model with $w_3=0$. This proves equality on those rows. To guarantee the same reduction for every old coefficient vector at deployment, the feature identity must hold for all admissible future inputs. If a future row has values $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$, the old score minus the score from the reduced coefficients is exactly $w_3(x_3-x_1-x_2)$. Thus the sample coefficient transformation alone does not guarantee deployment equivalence when the identity fails. A particular predictor with $w_3=0$ is an exception because it already ignores the third feature. The design decision therefore needs a domain constraint or an evaluation on the intended future population.
