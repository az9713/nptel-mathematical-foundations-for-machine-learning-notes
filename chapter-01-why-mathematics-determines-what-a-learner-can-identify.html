1. Why mathematics determines what a learner can identify

A document assistant retrieves a page because its numerical representation resembles a query. It then estimates whether the page answers the question and decides whether another search is worth its cost. Three different errors can occur: the representation can discard the relevant distinction, the probability estimate can be wrong, or the decision rule can optimize the wrong consequence. A program can execute perfectly while making any of these errors. Mathematics makes their causes distinguishable.

Recording spine: the introduction video and Lecture 1. Timestamp links locate related lecture topics. The document-assistant case, calculations, code, and exercises are original teaching constructions. This chapter develops the motivation mathematically; later chapters establish the underlying theory in detail.

1.1 Four tasks require different mathematical objects Lecture 1 · 07:29

Let an input be an object we wish to analyze, such as a document. A feature map is a specified rule that replaces an input with numerical measurements. Write $\phi:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^d$, where $\mathcal X$ is the input set, $d$ is the number of measurements, and $\mathbb R^d$ is the set of real coordinate vectors of length $d$. A predictor maps those measurements to an output. A training procedure uses observations to choose the predictor's parameters. These are three distinct objects: representation, fitted function, and procedure that fits it.

Regression estimates a numerical output, such as a page's reading time. Classification selects a label from a specified set, such as relevant or irrelevant. Dimensionality reduction replaces a representation by fewer coordinates while trying to preserve a chosen property. Density estimation estimates how a probability distribution allocates mass across possible observations. Similar data formats can support all four tasks; the desired output and criterion determine the problem.

For example, representing a document by counts of two terms produces a vector $(x_1,x_2)$. Predicting reading time from this vector is regression. Predicting whether it contains a required explanation is classification. Replacing the two counts by their sum is a one-dimensional representation. Modeling the frequencies of count pairs is a distributional problem. The sum preserves total count but discards which word occurred. No optimizer can recover distinctions that the feature map has erased.

The official course syllabus identifies linear algebra, probability and statistics, and multivariable calculus as the mathematical core. The four-task organization also appears in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong's introductory discussion, printed pages 13–16. Here it serves a concrete diagnostic: identify which object is wrong before changing the training procedure.

1.2 Rows describe observations; columns describe measured variables Lecture 1 · 10:38

Suppose $n$ documents each have $d$ numerical features. Let $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ be the data matrix, with entry $X_{ij}$ equal to feature $j$ of document $i$. The index $i$ runs over observations and $j$ over features. A row is a vector in $\mathbb R^d$; a column is a vector in $\mathbb R^n$. Confusing these two spaces changes the question being asked.

Let $w\in\mathbb R^d$ contain feature weights. The score vector $s=Xw\in\mathbb R^n$ has one number per document. Its $i$th entry is $s_i=\sum_{j=1}^dX_{ij}w_j$. Equivalently, $s$ is a linear combination of the feature columns. This equality is the bridge from individual predictions to the geometry of the entire dataset.

Consider the constructed matrix and two candidate weight vectors

$$X=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\\3&6\end{bmatrix},\qquad w=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \widetilde w=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Both give the score vector $(1,2,3)^\top$, where the superscript $\top$ transposes a row into a column. Indeed their difference is $(-2,1)^\top$, and $X(-2,1)^\top=0$. Thus the training scores cannot distinguish these parameters. The ambiguity arises from the representation: the second feature is exactly twice the first on every observed row.

At a new feature vector $(1,3)$, the first predictor gives $1$ while the second gives $2$. Equal training predictions therefore do not establish equivalent behavior on every possible input. If the relation $x_2=2x_1$ holds for every feasible input, the two predictors are equivalent on that restricted domain. Identifiability depends on the feasible inputs, not just on the parameter list.

A null direction of $X$ is a nonzero vector $v$ with $Xv=0$. Adding any multiple of $v$ to the parameters leaves the observed scores unchanged. Later chapters connect such directions to linear independence, rank, least squares, and singular values. This calculation already establishes why adding a new optimizer cannot resolve an exact informational ambiguity without an additional criterion.

1.3 A similarity score encodes a chosen geometry Lecture 1 · 12:49

For two real vectors $a,b\in\mathbb R^d$, their dot product is $a^\top b=\sum_j a_jb_j$. Their Euclidean lengths are $\|a\|_2=\sqrt{a^\top a}$ and $\|b\|_2=\sqrt{b^\top b}$. For nonzero vectors, cosine similarity is the normalized dot product

$$\operatorname{cosim}(a,b)=\frac{a^\top b}{\|a\|_2\|b\|_2}.$$

Normalization removes positive overall scaling. If $b=ca$ with $c\gt0$, the numerator is $c\|a\|_2^2$ and the denominator is the same quantity, giving similarity $1$. With $c\lt0$, the denominator uses $|c|$ and the result is $-1$. The zero vector has no cosine similarity under this definition because its length is zero; software must choose an explicit policy for it.

Take a query vector $q=(1,0)$ and document vectors $a=(2,0)$ and $b=(1,1)$. Their dot products with $q$ are $2$ and $1$, while the cosine similarities are $1$ and $1/\sqrt2$. Replacing $b$ by $(100,100)$ changes its dot product to $100$ but leaves its cosine unchanged. A dot-product ranking can therefore reward magnitude; an angular ranking deliberately discards that magnitude.

Neither score knows whether a passage answers a question. Two vectors can be orthogonal in the chosen coordinates while the underlying documents share a causal relationship or discuss the same event using different words. Orthogonality means a zero inner product in a specified geometry. It does not mean the real objects have no meaningful relationship. Chapters on inner products and projection develop the exact algebraic statement.

1.4 Probabilities and decisions answer different questions Lecture 1 · 16:44

Let $Y\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether a document actually contains the required explanation. For observed features $x$, write $p(x)=P(Y=1\mid x)$ for the conditional probability under a specified model. The vertical bar means that the features are treated as known. Estimating this probability and choosing an action from it are separate operations.

Suppose accepting an irrelevant document incurs cost $c_{\mathrm{FP}}\gt0$, while rejecting a relevant one costs $c_{\mathrm{FN}}\gt0$. Correct decisions cost zero. Accepting has conditional expected cost $c_{\mathrm{FP}}(1-p)$; rejecting has cost $c_{\mathrm{FN}}p$. The cheaper action is acceptance precisely when

$$c_{\mathrm{FP}}(1-p)\lt c_{\mathrm{FN}}p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\gt\frac{c_{\mathrm{FP}}}{c_{\mathrm{FP}}+c_{\mathrm{FN}}}.$$

For costs $4$ and $1$, the threshold is $4/5$. At probability $3/4$, acceptance costs $1$ on average, while rejection costs $3/4$. A threshold of $1/2$ would choose a more expensive action. The arithmetic is conditional on the probability estimate and cost model; it does not establish that either accurately describes deployment.

Correlation requires another distinction. Two measured variables may move together because one causes the other, because a common factor influences both, or because the sample was selected in a particular way. Observed co-variation alone cannot decide among these explanations. The probability chapters establish covariance as a measure of linear co-variation and construct dependent variables with zero covariance. Causal interpretation requires assumptions beyond the joint probability table.

1.5 Training chooses parameters under a stated objective Lecture 1 · 20:33

An objective function assigns a numerical score to each permitted parameter choice. Minimization selects choices with smaller objective. Let a scalar parameter $\theta\in\mathbb R$ control a deliberately simple predictor, and define its training objective by $J(\theta)=(\theta-2)^2$. Its derivative, the local rate of change, is $J'(\theta)=2(\theta-2)$. A gradient-descent step with positive step size $\eta$ is

$$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta J'(\theta_k),\qquad \theta_{k+1}-2=(1-2\eta)(\theta_k-2).$$

The integer $k$ counts updates. The second equality follows by substituting the derivative and collecting the terms in $\theta_k-2$. Repeated substitution gives error $(1-2\eta)^k(\theta_0-2)$. It tends to zero from every starting point exactly when $|1-2\eta|\lt1$, or $0\lt\eta\lt1$. At $\eta=1$, a nonzero error alternates sign without shrinking. Above $1$, its magnitude increases. Following the negative derivative does not by itself guarantee a successful finite step.

A constraint changes the admissible choices. If $\theta$ must lie between $0$ and $1$, the unconstrained minimizer $2$ is unavailable. On this interval, increasing $\theta$ decreases $(\theta-2)^2$, so the constrained minimizer is the boundary point $1$. A vanishing unconstrained derivative is therefore not a necessary condition for every constrained minimum. The optimization chapters make the role of feasible directions precise.

These are exact statements about a specified objective. They do not establish low error on future documents. A training criterion may approximate the desired task badly, and future observations may differ from the training population. Numerical optimization, statistical estimation, and the usefulness of the selected decision remain separate claims.

1.6 An executable diagnostic Lecture 1 · 24:50

The following standard-library program has no file or network effects. It checks the constructed parameter ambiguity and compares a convergent gradient step with the oscillatory boundary case. It uses finite toy inputs and does not train a document model. The assertions compare outputs with independently derived values, so changing a sign or normalization can produce a detectable failure.

from math import isclose

rows = [(1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 6)]
weights = [(1, 0), (-1, 1)]
scores = [[sum(x*w for x, w in zip(row, weight))
           for row in rows] for weight in weights]
assert scores[0] == scores[1] == [1, 2, 3]


def iterate(rate, steps):
    theta = 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        theta -= rate * 2 * (theta - 2)
    return theta


assert isclose(iterate(0.25, 8), 2 - 2 / 256)
assert iterate(1.0, 8) == 0.0
print("identical training scores:", scores[0])
print("stable iterate:", iterate(0.25, 8))
print("boundary iterate:", iterate(1.0, 8))

identical training scores: [1, 2, 3]
stable iterate: 1.9921875
boundary iterate: 0.0

1.7 Exercises and worked solutions

The opening assistant can now be analyzed in terms of representation, probabilities, objectives, and decisions. The examples expose their interactions without claiming that a toy calculation certifies a working application. The following problems test where those distinctions matter.

Problem 1 · Identifiability

Find every pair $(w_1,w_2)$ that produces scores $(1,2,3)^\top$ for the chapter's data matrix. What extra criterion selects the smallest Euclidean coefficient length?

Solution

Every row is a multiple of $(1,2)$, so the required relation is $w_1+2w_2=1$. Thus all solutions are $(1-2t,t)$ for $t\in\mathbb R$. Their squared lengths are $(1-2t)^2+t^2=5t^2-4t+1=5(t-2/5)^2+1/5$. The smallest length occurs uniquely at $t=2/5$, giving $(1/5,2/5)$. This extra norm criterion selects among identical training predictions; the observations alone do not select it.

Problem 2 · Lost information

A feature map sends a two-word count vector $(a,b)$ to $a+b$. Show why no downstream deterministic predictor can always decide whether the first count exceeds the second.

Solution

The inputs $(2,0)$ and $(0,2)$ both map to $2$, but the desired answers are different. Any deterministic function of the transformed feature receives the same input for both cases and returns the same output. It must therefore fail on at least one. Increasing the number of optimizer steps cannot repair a non-injective representation that merges differently labeled inputs.

Problem 3 · Similarity and scaling

For $q=(1,0)$, compare the two ranking rules on $a=(2,0)$ and $b=(100,100)$. What information does normalization discard?

Solution

The dot products are $2$ and $100$, so the raw dot product ranks $b$ first. The cosines are $2/(1\cdot2)=1$ and $100/\sqrt{20000}=1/\sqrt2$, so the angular rule ranks $a$ first. Multiplying either document by a positive constant preserves its direction while changing its total magnitude. Cosine normalization discards that magnitude, which is desirable only if magnitude is irrelevant to the intended comparison.

Problem 4 · Decision costs

Determine the optimal decision when $p=0.6$, the false-positive cost is $1$, and the false-negative cost is $9$. Repeat when the costs are exchanged.

Solution

In the first case, acceptance costs $1(0.4)=0.4$ and rejection costs $9(0.6)=5.4$, so accept. With exchanged costs, acceptance costs $9(0.4)=3.6$ and rejection costs $1(0.6)=0.6$, so reject. The conditional probability is identical; only the decision consequences changed. The thresholds are $0.1$ and $0.9$, respectively.

Problem 5 · A stable algorithm can optimize the wrong task

Construct a training sample and a deployment population on which a constant least-squares predictor fits training perfectly but has nonzero deployment error.

Solution

Let every training response equal $0$. The constant predictor $f(x)=0$ has zero training squared error and is a minimizer over constant predictors. Let every deployment response equal $1$. Its deployment squared error is exactly $1$ on every observation. Optimization has solved the specified training problem; the population changed. No numerical convergence argument can rule out this constructed change in the response distribution.

Problem 6 · Step size and a constraint

For $J(\theta)=(\theta-2)^2$ on $[0,1]$, start at $0$ and use step size $1/4$. After each unconstrained step, replace values outside the interval by the nearest endpoint. Determine the first three iterates and explain why the final point need not have derivative zero.

Solution

The first unconstrained step gives $0-(1/4)(-4)=1$, which is feasible. The next unconstrained proposal is $1-(1/4)(-2)=1.5$ and is replaced by $1$. The third iteration repeats the same operation, so the iterates are $1,1,1$. At this point the derivative is $-2$. It points toward a decrease outside the permitted interval, but every feasible displacement from the upper endpoint goes left and increases the objective. The boundary constrains improvement even though the unconstrained derivative is nonzero.
