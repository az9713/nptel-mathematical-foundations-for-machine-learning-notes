24. Perceptrons, multilayer networks, and backpropagation

A threshold unit draws one separating hyperplane. A network composes units to build a richer function. Backpropagation then answers a different question: how does changing each parameter change a specified loss? Representation, differentiation, and successful optimization are three distinct achievements. A network can represent the desired answer while a particular training run fails to find it.

Lecture spine: Lectures 62–64. Biological descriptions motivate a deliberately simplified mathematical model. The constructions and proofs below are original. The notation uses column feature vectors and column gradients; a batch matrix has observations in rows.

24.1 A neuron model becomes an affine decision rule L62 · 02:25

Let $x\in\mathbb R^d$ be an input, $w\in\mathbb R^d$ weights, and $b\in\mathbb R$ a bias. An artificial unit first computes the preactivation $z=w^\top x+b$ and then the activation $a=\phi(z)$. This abstracts weighted aggregation and response; it does not reproduce a biological neuron's detailed dynamics. For the threshold activation $\phi(z)=\mathbf1\{z\ge0\}$, the output is binary. When $w\ne0$, the set $w^\top x+b=0$ is a hyperplane of dimension $d-1$, and the positive and negative outputs occupy its two half-spaces.

The bias moves the boundary away from the origin. It can be absorbed algebraically by defining $\widetilde x=(x^\top,1)^\top$ and $\widetilde w=(w^\top,b)^\top$. Then $z=\widetilde w^\top\widetilde x$. This bookkeeping changes the parameter dimension and feature norm, which matters in a convergence bound. It does not create an additional measured feature.

For Boolean inputs $x_1,x_2\in\{0,1\}$, choosing $w=(1,1)^\top$, $b=-3/2$ implements AND; $b=-1/2$ implements OR. Choosing a single weight $-1$ and bias $1/2$ implements NOT. These examples explain the lecture's threshold-logic bridge without suggesting that every Boolean pattern is linearly separable.

24.2 Why one separator fails, and composition succeeds L62 · 12:25

XOR has target one at $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, and zero at $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Suppose a threshold unit represented it. The zero at the origin requires $b\lt 0$. The two positive points require $w_1+b\ge0$ and $w_2+b\ge0$, so $w_1+w_2+2b\ge0$. But the negative point $(1,1)$ requires $w_1+w_2+b\lt 0$. Adding $b\lt 0$ gives $w_1+w_2+2b\lt 0$, a contradiction. Moving or rotating one line cannot solve this geometry.

Nonlinear hidden features change the problem. Define $\operatorname{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z)$ and set

$$h_1=\operatorname{ReLU}(x_1+x_2),\qquad h_2=\operatorname{ReLU}(x_1+x_2-1),\qquad \widehat y=h_1-2h_2.$$

At input sums $s=0,1,2$, the output is respectively $0,1,0$. Thus a two-unit hidden layer with a linear output exactly represents XOR on these four inputs. It makes no claim to represent a unique Boolean extension off that finite set. For instance, at $(1/4,1/4)$ its output is $1/2$.

Why insist on a nonlinear activation? Two affine maps collapse to one: $W_2(W_1x+b_1)+b_2=(W_2W_1)x+(W_2b_1+b_2)$. Repeating linear or affine layers therefore does not remove the single-separator limitation. Nonlinearity allows a layer to create features that another layer can combine into a more complicated boundary.

Several half-spaces can also define intersections or unions of regions. The required architecture depends on the target and activation. A sketch of a region separated by three lines is not a theorem that every classification problem needs exactly three layers. Width, depth, and approximation accuracy are separate quantities.

24.3 What the perceptron convergence statement assumes L62 · 25:57

For a signed-label formulation, let $t_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ and use augmented features $\widetilde x_i$. Start $\theta_0=0$. Whenever an example has $t_i\theta^\top\widetilde x_i\le0$, update $\theta\leftarrow\theta+t_i\widetilde x_i$. Here zero margin counts as a mistake, fixing the tie convention. This is the perceptron update, not differentiation through a hard threshold.

Assume there exists a unit vector $u$ and a positive margin $\gamma$ such that $t_i u^\top\widetilde x_i\ge\gamma$ for every training example; also assume $\|\widetilde x_i\|\le R$. After $M$ mistakes, taking an inner product with $u$ at each update yields $u^\top\theta_M\ge M\gamma$. At a mistaken example,

$$\|\theta+t_i\widetilde x_i\|^2=\|\theta\|^2+2t_i\theta^\top\widetilde x_i+\|\widetilde x_i\|^2\le\|\theta\|^2+R^2.$$

Induction gives $\|\theta_M\|\le R\sqrt M$. Cauchy–Schwarz now gives $M\gamma\le R\sqrt M$, hence $M\le(R/\gamma)^2$ for $M\gt 0$. Repeated presentation of the finite dataset must eventually stop producing mistakes under these assumptions. The proof is a training-separability result; it gives neither a test-error guarantee nor convergence for XOR. With noisy or inseparable labels, a stopping rule or a different loss is needed.

24.4 Activation, memory, and causality are different notions L62 · 14:04

A sigmoid $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ maps the real line to $(0,1)$ and has derivative $\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$. The hyperbolic tangent maps to $(-1,1)$ and has derivative $1-\tanh^2z$. A ReLU is continuous and has derivative zero for $z\lt 0$ and one for $z\gt 0$, but no ordinary derivative at zero. A hard threshold is constant away from its jump, so its derivative supplies no useful ordinary gradient there.

A static rule $a_t=\phi(w^\top x_t+b)$ is memoryless: its output at time $t$ depends only on the current input. Negative inputs or negative activation values do not create memory. A state equation $h_t=\phi(Ah_{t-1}+Bx_t+b)$ does create memory through $h_{t-1}$. It is still causal when the state is initialized and only present or past inputs are used. An output depending on the actual future input $x_{t+1}$ would be noncausal in this online sense. This distinction resolves an ambiguity in the lecture captions around activation and memory.

A feedforward network has an acyclic computation graph, so a forward evaluation has an unambiguous order. A recurrent connection requires additional semantics, such as the time-indexed state equation above. Drawing an arrow back to an earlier unit does not itself define whether one should solve simultaneous equations, iterate to equilibrium, or advance time; those choices need not have the same solution.

24.5 Count the actual maps and parameters L63 · 15:18

For an $L$-layer feedforward network, put $a^{(0)}=x\in\mathbb R^{d_0}$. For $\ell=1,\ldots,L$, define

$$z^{(\ell)}=W^{(\ell)}a^{(\ell-1)}+b^{(\ell)},\qquad a^{(\ell)}=\phi_\ell(z^{(\ell)}),\qquad W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb R^{d_\ell\times d_{\ell-1}}.$$

The activation acts coordinatewise here. Bias $b^{(\ell)}$ has $d_\ell$ entries. Layer width is $d_\ell$; depth counts successive parameterized maps under this convention, excluding the input holder. A fully connected $3\to3\to1$ network has $9+3=12$ weights and $3+1=4$ biases, totaling 16 parameters. The number of hidden units does not equal the number of layers.

For a batch $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_0}$, the corresponding first-layer calculation is $Z=X(W^{(1)})^\top+\mathbf1(b^{(1)})^\top$, an $n\times d_1$ matrix. With additional axes for time, channels, or image coordinates, arrays have more than two indices. In numerical machine learning these are often called tensors; they are not restricted to three-dimensional arrays.

The network graph is a logical dependency structure, not a literal drawing of processor placement. Its matrix operations can run on CPUs, GPUs, or dedicated accelerators. Google's TPU documentation describes TPUs as its specialized machine-learning chips with matrix units. NVIDIA's matrix-multiplication guide discusses GPU Tensor Cores and how arithmetic dimensions affect their use. Hardware speed depends on precision, shapes, memory movement, and software; no fixed speedup follows from counting graph layers.

24.6 Differentiate a specified loss before updating L64 · 13:38

Begin with the lecture's scalar affine predictor $\widehat y=wx+b$ and target $y$. Use $\ell=\tfrac12(\widehat y-y)^2$ and residual $r=\widehat y-y$. The chain rule gives $\partial_w\ell=rx$ and $\partial_b\ell=r$. Gradient descent updates $w\leftarrow w-\eta rx$ and $b\leftarrow b-\eta r$ for learning rate $\eta\gt 0$. If the loss omits the factor $1/2$, both derivatives double. Either convention works if the derivative and learning rate are interpreted consistently.

For example, take $x=2$, $y=3$, $w=1$, $b=0$. Then $\widehat y=2$, $r=-1$, and $\ell=1/2$. At $\eta=0.1$, the simultaneous update gives $w'=1.2$, $b'=0.1$, hence $\widehat y'=2.5$ and $\ell'=0.125$. Both gradients must be computed from the old parameters; changing $w$ and then computing a new bias derivative would define a different update.

Squared error is convex as a function of the residual, and as a function of affine-model parameters. It need not be convex as a function of all parameters in a nonlinear network. Even the scalar two-factor model $\ell(u,v)=\tfrac12(uv-1)^2$ has an indefinite Hessian at $(0,0)$. Thus a small training loss, global optimality, and accurate unseen predictions are separate questions. Backpropagation does not guarantee any of them by itself.

24.7 Backpropagation is the chain rule with reuse L64 · 20:39

Let a scalar loss $\ell(a^{(L)},y)$ depend on the final activation. Define the column adjoint $\delta^{(\ell)}=\nabla_{z^{(\ell)}}\ell$. At the final layer, $\delta^{(L)}=\nabla_{a^{(L)}}\ell\odot\phi_L'(z^{(L)})$, where $\odot$ means coordinatewise multiplication. Working backward gives

$$\delta^{(\ell)}=\big((W^{(\ell+1)})^\top\delta^{(\ell+1)}\big)\odot\phi_\ell'(z^{(\ell)}),\qquad \nabla_{W^{(\ell)}}\ell=\delta^{(\ell)}(a^{(\ell-1)})^\top,\qquad \nabla_{b^{(\ell)}}\ell=\delta^{(\ell)}.$$

To derive the weight formula rather than memorize it, perturb only $W^{(\ell)}$ by $D$. Holding the already computed input activation fixed gives $dz^{(\ell)}=D a^{(\ell-1)}$. Then $d\ell=(\delta^{(\ell)})^\top D a^{(\ell-1)}=\operatorname{tr}((\delta^{(\ell)}(a^{(\ell-1)})^\top)^\top D)$. This identifies the Frobenius gradient. Its shape is $d_\ell\times d_{\ell-1}$, matching the parameter matrix.

The backward recurrence follows by transposing the forward Jacobian. Reusing the upstream adjoint avoids expanding the complete derivative separately for every parameter. This is the central computational point of Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §5.6, printed pages 159–164, expressed here with column gradients. Reverse-mode differentiation accumulates contributions from all downstream uses of a value; a branching graph requires summation, not an arbitrary single path.

For a scalar loss, a reverse traversal obtains all parameter derivatives using local rules and stored intermediate values. It uses the same elementary derivatives as calculus, evaluated with floating-point arithmetic. It is neither a symbolic simplifier nor a finite-difference approximation. A batch mean loss averages per-example gradients. A sparse connectivity mask fixes selected weights to zero and masks their updates; an accidentally zero but trainable weight is not necessarily a missing connection.

24.8 Derivatives, training schedules, and limitations L64 · 28:39

One epoch is one pass through the training set. With $n$ observations and batch size $m$, an epoch typically contains $\lceil n/m\rceil$ parameter updates. It is not generally synonymous with one update. Training length is a choice determined by the data, optimizer, and stopping criteria; the lecture's large epoch counts are examples rather than mathematical requirements.

A learning rate sets an update scale. Momentum adds state to the optimizer, for example $v_{k+1}=\beta v_k+g_k$ and $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta v_{k+1}$ with $0\le\beta\lt 1$; this is different from merely renaming $\eta$. Validation loss can guide stopping because training loss alone can keep falling while predictive performance deteriorates. A small gradient can indicate a minimum, a saddle, a flat region, or saturation.

Continuity alone does not justify the derivative rules: $|z|$ is continuous but not differentiable at zero. With ReLU networks a derivative convention is chosen at the kink; the usual smooth chain rule applies away from such points. Sigmoid and tanh avoid that kink but their derivatives can be small. Repeated multiplication can then attenuate early-layer gradients. Neither a fixed layer count nor a fixed neuron count defines all deep learning.

Multiple outputs can encode several regression targets or class scores. A ten-class model often has ten output scores, but a probability interpretation requires an appropriate normalization and loss. Bayesian networks describe probability factorizations; they are not obtained simply by changing the name of a feedforward network's activation. The lecture closes by recommending small implementations and Anil Ananthaswamy's Why Machines Learn as historical reading. The calculation below follows that small-implementation principle before any accelerator programming.

24.9 Executable check: an entire input-layer gradient

This original two-hidden-unit network uses tanh and a linear output. A directional check changes all four entries of $W$ together. Agreement tests a composed derivative and its shape, including the output residual and hidden activation derivative. It does not demonstrate training convergence.

import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=6, suppress=True)
x = np.array([0.3, -0.7])
W = np.array([[0.4, -0.2], [-0.1, 0.5]])
b = np.array([0.1, -0.2])
v = np.array([0.6, -0.3])
c = 0.2
target = 0.8


def forward(W):
    a = np.tanh(W @ x + b)
    pred = v @ a + c
    return 0.5 * (pred-target)**2, a, pred


loss, a, pred = forward(W)
delta = (pred-target) * v * (1-a*a)
grad = np.outer(delta, x)
D = np.array([[0.2, -0.1], [0.3, 0.4]])
eps = 1e-5
finite = (forward(W+eps*D)[0]-forward(W-eps*D)[0])/(2*eps)
print(f'prediction={pred:.6f} loss={loss:.6f}')
print('input-layer gradient:')
print(grad)
print(f'directional analytic={np.sum(grad*D):.8f} finite={finite:.8f}')
X = np.array([[0, 0], [0, 1], [1, 0], [1, 1]])
s = X.sum(axis=1)
print('XOR outputs:', np.maximum(s, 0)-2*np.maximum(s-1, 0))

24.10 Six worked problems

Problem 1: rotate the XOR separator?

Problem. A colleague proposes choosing a better bias to fit XOR with one threshold unit. Show why no weights and bias can do so, then give an explicit hidden-feature solution.

Solution

Write the four constraints using the convention positive when the affine score is nonnegative. They are $b\lt 0$, $w_1+b\ge0$, $w_2+b\ge0$, and $w_1+w_2+b\lt 0$. Summing the middle two gives $w_1+w_2+2b\ge0$; adding the first to the last gives the same expression strictly negative. The contradiction rules out every bias and orientation. The features $h_1=\max(0,x_1+x_2)$ and $h_2=\max(0,x_1+x_2-1)$ yield pairs $(0,0),(1,0),(1,0),(2,1)$ on the four inputs. Their combination $h_1-2h_2$ gives $0,1,1,0$. Representability changes because the hidden map is nonlinear.

Problem 2: count parameters without counting arrows twice

Problem. Count weights and biases in a fully connected $4\to5\to3\to2$ network. Does replacing every activation by the identity preserve its expressive advantage?

Solution

The matrices have $5\cdot4=20$, $3\cdot5=15$, and $2\cdot3=6$ entries, totaling 41 weights. Biases contribute $5+3+2=10$, so there are 51 trainable scalars. Identity activations reduce the composition to $Ax+c$ with $A=W^{(3)}W^{(2)}W^{(1)}$ and $c=W^{(3)}W^{(2)}b^{(1)}+W^{(3)}b^{(2)}+b^{(3)}$. Its output is affine despite the parameter count. A nonlinear classification boundary therefore requires something beyond stacking these identity layers.

Problem 3: apply a margin bound correctly

Problem. A finite augmented dataset admits a unit separating vector with signed margin at least $0.4$, and every augmented feature has norm at most 2. What does the perceptron proof guarantee? What happens if every augmented feature is multiplied by 10?

Solution

Starting at zero and updating on nonpositive signed scores, the number of mistakes is at most $(2/0.4)^2=25$. A complete pass with no mistake certifies training separation under the chosen tie rule. Rescaling all augmented features multiplies both $R$ and the margin for the same unit separator by 10, giving $(20/4)^2=25$ again. Scaling only selected measured coordinates is different: it changes the geometry and may change the usable ratio. No test-set bound follows from this calculation.

Problem 4: propagate through two scalar units

Problem. Let $h=\tanh(wx+b)$, $\widehat y=vh+c$, and $\ell=\tfrac12(\widehat y-y)^2$. Derive all four parameter derivatives. Evaluate them at $x=1$, $w=b=c=0$, $v=2$, and $y=1$.

Solution

With $r=\widehat y-y$, output derivatives are $\partial_v\ell=rh$ and $\partial_c\ell=r$. The hidden preactivation adjoint is $rv(1-h^2)$, so $\partial_w\ell=rv(1-h^2)x$ and $\partial_b\ell=rv(1-h^2)$. At the specified point $h=0$, $\widehat y=0$, and $r=-1$. The derivatives in order $(w,b,v,c)$ are $(-2,-2,0,-1)$. Although the output-weight derivative vanishes, other derivatives do not. This also shows why one zero derivative should not be read as evidence of a stationary network.

Problem 5: a shared value has two downstream paths

Problem. In a computation graph, $a=x^2$, $p=3a$, $q=a^2$, and $f=p+q$. Compute $df/dx$ by a reverse traversal. Identify the mistake made by retaining only the path through $q$.

Solution

Seed the output adjoint at one. Both $p$ and $q$ receive adjoint one. Their contributions to $a$ are respectively 3 and $2a$, so the accumulated adjoint is $\overline a=3+2a$. Multiplying by $da/dx=2x$ gives $df/dx=(3+2x^2)2x=6x+4x^3$. The $q$ path alone would give $4x^3$, missing $6x$. For $x=1$ the correct answer is 10 rather than 4. Shared inputs require addition of adjoints before propagating farther.

Problem 6: what does a gradient check establish?

Problem. For $f(w)=\max(0,w)$ at $w=0$, a centered finite difference returns $1/2$. Does this prove that the derivative is $1/2$? Explain how to interpret the smooth-network check above.

Solution

For $\varepsilon\gt 0$, $[f(\varepsilon)-f(-\varepsilon)]/(2\varepsilon)=1/2$. The right derivative is one and the left derivative is zero, so an ordinary derivative does not exist. A centered quotient can average slopes across a kink. In the displayed tanh network all local maps are smooth, and the centered directional quotient approximates the derivative along $D$ with truncation error of order $\varepsilon^2$ under sufficient smoothness. Its agreement with $\langle\nabla_W\ell,D\rangle_F$ is evidence for this implementation at this point, subject to rounding. It neither proves every component globally correct nor guarantees a useful optimizer trajectory.

24.11 Connections to the rest of the course

The hyperplane model uses inner products and affine geometry. The perceptron proof uses margins, norm bounds, and Cauchy–Schwarz. Hidden layers compose functions, and backpropagation organizes Jacobian-transpose products. Gradient descent then uses those derivatives to alter parameters. Keeping these roles distinct makes it possible to diagnose whether a failure comes from an inadequate representation, an incorrect derivative, an unstable update, or a mismatch between training loss and the prediction task.
