26. Linear and logistic regression: a mathematical synthesis

Linear regression models a conditional mean. Logistic regression models a conditional probability through its log odds. Both use affine scores, observation-row design matrices, and optimization, but they fit different statistical models. The final lectures bring the course's geometry, probability, calculus, and algorithms together; the distinction between a model, its fitting criterion, and its prediction rule is the unifying thread.

Lecture spine: Lectures 68–70. The first two lectures motivate the statistical interpretation of regression and the logit link. The likelihood derivatives, existence analysis, stable numerical formulas, and original worked examples below supply explicit mathematical development beyond the lecture sketches. Lecture 70 is a substantive course recap followed by credits, and is included in the synthesis and coverage.

26.1 What a polynomial design matrix actually does L68 · 02:28

Suppose $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2$ has unknown coefficients and is observed exactly at three distinct input values $u,v,w$. The equations $p(u)=y_u$, $p(v)=y_v$, and $p(w)=y_w$ become $Va=y$, where

$$V=\begin{bmatrix}1&u&u^2\\1&v&v^2\\1&w&w^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad a=\begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\\a_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det V=(v-u)(w-u)(w-v).$$

One derivation subtracts the first row from each later row, factors $v-u$ and $w-u$, and evaluates the remaining $2\times2$ determinant $\det\begin{bmatrix}1&v+u\\1&w+u\end{bmatrix}=w-v$. Thus distinct input coordinates make $V$ invertible and determine a unique polynomial of degree at most two. Merely having three distinct ordered pairs is insufficient if two share the same input. The resulting coefficient $a_2$ can be zero, so the polynomial need not have degree exactly two.

For an original example, observations $(0,1),(1,2),(2,5)$ imply $a_0=1$, $a_1+a_2=1$, and $2a_1+4a_2=4$, giving $p(x)=1+x^2$. This is exact interpolation. With many noisy observations, requiring a polynomial to pass through every point is a different problem from estimating a stable relationship. More parameters can force a fit while producing poor behavior between or beyond the observations.

A polynomial model is nonlinear in its input but linear in its coefficients. Its feature row is $(1,x,x^2)$, and its prediction is that row times $a$. The same linear-algebra machinery applies to any fixed feature map. This meaning of linear regression is explained in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §9.2.1, printed page 295. When features themselves contain unknown nonlinear parameters, that simple linear-in-coefficients fitting problem changes.

26.2 The response is random even when the input is fixed L68 · 16:51

The simple regression model is $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\varepsilon$. With fixed input $x$ and zero-mean noise, the conditional mean is $\mathbb E[Y\mid x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x$. If inputs are random, the appropriate assumption is $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$ to conclude $\mathbb E[Y\mid X]=\beta_0+\beta_1X$. An unconditional statement $\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$ alone does not establish the conditional-mean formula.

The term “independent variable” means explanatory input in this usage; it does not assert stochastic independence of input and response. A fitted line predicts a mean under the model, not the exact outcome of every future observation. A regression association also does not by itself identify the effect of an intervention on the input.

For observed pairs $(x_i,y_i)$, noise $\varepsilon_i$ in the population model differs from the fitted residual $e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i$. Noise is defined relative to the unknown true parameters; residuals use estimated parameters. The least-squares procedure chooses $\widehat\beta_0,\widehat\beta_1$ to minimize $S(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_i(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2$. These are vertical errors in the response direction, not perpendicular distances to the line. Treating both coordinates as noisy with symmetric roles would motivate a different fitting criterion.

26.3 Derive the slope and intercept by centering L68 · 37:09

Define means $\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i$ and $\bar y=n^{-1}\sum_i y_i$, centered sums $S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$ and $S_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$. Differentiating $S$ in the intercept yields $\sum_i(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)=0$, so every stationary fit has $\beta_0=\bar y-\beta_1\bar x$. Substituting this gives the one-variable problem

$$S(\bar y-\beta_1\bar x,\beta_1)=S_{yy}-2\beta_1S_{xy}+\beta_1^2S_{xx},\qquad S_{yy}=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2.$$

If $S_{xx}\gt 0$, completing the square shows that its unique minimum occurs at

$$\widehat\beta_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\qquad \widehat\beta_0=\bar y-\widehat\beta_1\bar x.$$

The fitted line passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$. The minimized residual sum is $S_{yy}-S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}$. The condition $S_{xx}\gt 0$ means at least two distinct observed input values. If every input equals $c$, predictions depend only on $\beta_0+c\beta_1$. Any parameters satisfying that combination equal to $\bar y$ minimize the loss; slope and intercept are not separately identifiable.

For points $(0,1),(1,2),(2,2),(3,5)$, the means are $\bar x=1.5$, $\bar y=2.5$, while $S_{xx}=5$, $S_{xy}=6$, and $S_{yy}=9$. Hence the fitted line is $\widehat y=0.7+1.2x$. Predictions are $(0.7,1.9,3.1,4.3)$, and residuals are $(0.3,0.1,-1.1,0.7)$. They sum to zero and their dot product with the input vector is $0+0.1-2.2+2.1=0$. Their squared norm is 1.8, agreeing with $9-36/5$.

26.4 The Gram matrix is the same calculation in another form L68 · 39:44

Let $A=[\mathbf1\;x]\in\mathbb R^{n\times2}$, $\beta=(\beta_0,\beta_1)^\top$, and $y\in\mathbb R^n$. Then $S(\beta)=\|y-A\beta\|^2$, and the normal equations are $A^\top A\widehat\beta=A^\top y$. Here

$$A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}n&\sum_i x_i\\\sum_i x_i&\sum_i x_i^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^\top y=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_i y_i\\\sum_i x_i y_i\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det(A^\top A)=nS_{xx}.$$

The design matrix is generally rectangular; it has full column rank rather than an ordinary inverse. Its Gram matrix is invertible exactly when $S_{xx}\gt 0$. Solving the two normal equations reproduces the centered formulas. The fitted vector $\widehat y=A\widehat\beta$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $A$, because $A^\top(y-\widehat y)=0$. Any candidate fit $Ac$ obeys $\|y-Ac\|^2=\|y-\widehat y\|^2+\|\widehat y-Ac\|^2$, which proves least-squares optimality geometrically.

For interpretation, $n^{-1}\sum_i x_i^2$ is a raw second moment, equal to the square of the root-mean-square value. The centered variance with denominator $n$ is $S_{xx}/n=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i^2-\bar x^2$. Neither quantity should be confused with its square root. The matrix entries contain both a mean and a second moment; centering removes their coupling.

Let $s_x=\sqrt{S_{xx}/n}$ and $s_y=\sqrt{S_{yy}/n}$. When both are positive, define $\rho=S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$. Then $\widehat\beta_1=\rho s_y/s_x$. Using denominator $n-1$ consistently for both standard deviations leaves the ratio unchanged. If $s_y=0$, correlation is undefined although the fitted slope is zero when $S_{xx}\gt 0$. The standardized-response fit is $\widehat y=\bar y+\rho(s_y/s_x)(x-\bar x)$.

With an intercept and $S_{yy}\gt 0$, the coefficient of determination is $R^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}/S_{yy}=\rho^2$. In the example above $R^2=0.8$ and $\rho=\sqrt{0.8}\approx0.894427$. This identity belongs to the in-sample simple least-squares model with an intercept. An out-of-sample $R^2$ can be negative, and a no-intercept model requires care with its reference baseline.

26.5 When least squares is also maximum likelihood

The minimizer of a squared-error objective exists as an algebraic projection without assuming a noise distribution. A Gaussian model gives it an additional statistical interpretation. Assume $Y_i\mid x_i$ are conditionally independent normal variables with mean $a_i^\top\beta$ and common variance $\sigma^2\gt 0$, where $a_i^\top$ is row $i$ of $A$. Their joint negative log likelihood is

$$-\log L(\beta)=\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac1{2\sigma^2}\|y-A\beta\|^2.$$

For fixed $\sigma^2$, maximizing likelihood is exactly minimizing squared error. Full column rank makes the coefficients unique; otherwise the fitted vector remains unique but coefficients can differ by a null-space vector. These assumptions are needed when applying the likelihood calculation in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §9.2, printed pages 292–295. The number of observations alone does not imply full rank.

If zero-mean errors are uncorrelated with common variance $\sigma^2$, conditioning on a full-rank fixed design gives $\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta)=\sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1}$. This follows from $\widehat\beta-\beta=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\varepsilon$ and covariance transformation. Gaussianity is unnecessary for this covariance identity. It is useful for exact finite-sample distributional statements, which are beyond the present fitting derivation.

26.6 Binary outcomes require a probability model L69 · 07:13

For a binary outcome $Y\in\{0,1\}$ with conditional success probability $p(x)$, one has $\mathbb E[Y\mid x]=p(x)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x)=p(x)(1-p(x))$. Independent Bernoulli outcomes have a binomial sum only when their success probabilities are the same; observations with differing feature vectors generally have different probabilities. The response observed in the training set is $y_i$, while $p(x_i)$ is a model quantity to estimate.

A linear probability model $p(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x$ with nonzero slope takes values outside $[0,1]$ as $x$ ranges over the real line. Logistic regression instead models a transformed probability. For $0\lt p\lt 1$, odds are $o=p/(1-p)$; the inverse relation is $p=o/(1+o)$. For an original survey example, 72 positive responses among 120 give sample proportion $\widehat p=0.6$, sample odds $1.5$, and sample log odds $\log1.5$. A sample proportion estimates a population proportion under an appropriate sampling scheme; it is not the population parameter by definition.

The logit transform $\operatorname{logit}(p)=\log[p/(1-p)]$ maps $(0,1)$ bijectively onto $\mathbb R$. Its derivative is $1/[p(1-p)]\gt 0$. Setting this logit equal to an affine score $z=\beta_0+w^\top x$ and solving gives

$$\frac p{1-p}=e^z,\qquad p=e^z(1-p),\qquad p=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}=\sigma(z).$$

The added one is a consequence of solving the odds relation; $e^{-z}$ is already strictly positive for finite real $z$. The model does not introduce the one merely to repair an otherwise zero denominator. A finite score gives a probability strictly between zero and one, approaching the endpoints only as $z$ tends to an infinity.

26.7 Interpret coefficients and decision boundaries L69 · 29:26

In the one-feature model $\operatorname{logit}p=\beta_0+\beta_1x$, increasing $x$ by one multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_1}$. It does not generally add $\beta_1$ to the probability. The probability derivative is $dp/dx=\beta_1p(1-p)$, so its sensitivity depends on the current probability. Holding other encoded features fixed is part of the corresponding multivariable interpretation.

For example, $z=-2+(\log2)x$ doubles the odds per unit of $x$. At $x=0$, odds are $e^{-2}$ and probability is approximately $0.1192$. At $x=1$, odds double but probability rises to approximately $0.2130$, not $0.2384$. The half-probability point is $x=2/\log2$. A categorical feature needs an encoding, such as indicator columns; assigning arbitrary numbers to unordered categories imposes an unintended order and spacing.

A probability threshold $\tau\in(0,1)$ gives the decision rule $\widehat y=\mathbf1\{p(x)\ge\tau\}$. Since the sigmoid is increasing, this is equivalent to $\beta_0+w^\top x\ge\operatorname{logit}(\tau)$. The boundary is therefore an affine hyperplane when $w\ne0$: a point in one input dimension, a line in two, and a plane in three. Its dimension is one less than the number of input coordinates. Applying a sigmoid to an affine score does not by itself produce a curved boundary in those coordinates. Nonlinear feature maps can produce curved boundaries in the original variables.

Threshold one half corresponds to score zero. Other costs or operating requirements can justify another threshold, as derived in Chapter 25. A probability model and a binary decision rule are distinct objects: changing the threshold changes classifications without refitting the probabilities.

26.8 Fit logistic parameters with the Bernoulli likelihood

The lecture's affine-score-plus-sigmoid description specifies the predictor family. It should not be interpreted as an instruction to first fit an ordinary least-squares line to binary labels and then pass that fitted line through a sigmoid. The usual logistic fit directly estimates the score parameters using a Bernoulli likelihood. This section extends the lecture to make the fitting criterion explicit.

Augment each feature vector with an intercept coordinate and write $z_i=a_i^\top\theta$, $p_i=\sigma(z_i)$. Assuming conditional independence, $L(\theta)=\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}$. Its average negative log likelihood, also called binary cross entropy, is

$$J(\theta)=-\frac1n\sum_i[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]=\frac1n\sum_i[\log(1+e^{z_i})-y_i z_i].$$

The equality follows from $\log p_i=z_i-\log(1+e^{z_i})$ and $\log(1-p_i)=-\log(1+e^{z_i})$. For numerical evaluation, $\log(1+e^z)=\max(0,z)+\log(1+e^{-|z|})$ avoids overflow. For extreme scores, the signed-margin form is also useful: with $t_i=2y_i-1$, the loss is $\log(1+e^{-t_i z_i})$. A stable library primitive such as np.logaddexp(0, -t*z) evaluates it without first forming probabilities close to an endpoint.

Differentiating one observation gives $d\ell_i/dz_i=p_i-y_i$ and $d^2\ell_i/dz_i^2=p_i(1-p_i)$. For $A$ with feature rows $a_i^\top$,

$$\nabla J(\theta)=\frac1nA^\top(p-y),\qquad \nabla^2J(\theta)=\frac1nA^\top D A,\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(p_i(1-p_i)).$$

For every $v$, $v^\top\nabla^2Jv=n^{-1}\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(a_i^\top v)^2\ge0$, proving convexity. At finite parameters, all diagonal entries of $D$ are positive, so full column rank makes the Hessian positive definite. These likelihood and convexity claims are consistent with Boyd and Vandenberghe, §7.1.1, printed pages 354–355. The formula resembles a least-squares gradient, but its residual vector and curvature depend on the sigmoid probabilities.

A Newton step solves $(A^\top D A)s=-A^\top(p-y)$ when this system is nonsingular, then uses a safeguarded update $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha s$. One need not explicitly invert the Hessian. A gradient step is simpler but needs a suitable step size. Neither method makes the nonlinear score equations an ordinary linear least-squares solve.

26.9 Convexity does not guarantee a finite fitted parameter

Suppose a vector $v$ strictly separates the signed observations: $t_i a_i^\top v\gt 0$ for every $i$. Along $\theta=cv$ with $c\to\infty$, each loss $\log(1+e^{-c t_i a_i^\top v})$ tends to zero. Every term is strictly positive at every finite $c$, so the unregularized loss has infimum zero without attaining it. The coefficients can grow without bound while the objective approaches a finite lower bound. Positive-definite local curvature at finite parameters does not contradict this failure of attainment.

A particularly transparent dataset has two feature values $-1,+1$ with labels $0,1$ and zero intercept. At slope $c$, both signed margins equal $c$, so the average loss is $\log(1+e^{-c})$. It decreases from about $0.3133$ at $c=1$ to $2.06\times10^{-9}$ at $c=20$, yet has no minimizing finite slope. This is why a training run with very large coefficients is not necessarily evidence of a small numerical error.

Adding $\lambda\|\theta\|^2/2$ with $\lambda\gt 0$ to all parameters makes the objective strictly convex and coercive: it tends to infinity as $\|\theta\|\to\infty$. Continuity then gives a finite minimizer, and strict convexity gives uniqueness. Its gradient adds $\lambda\theta$ and Hessian adds $\lambda I$. Some software leaves the intercept unpenalized; the all-parameter proof does not automatically apply to that different objective. Regularization changes the statistical and geometric problem, rather than merely making the same infinite optimum easier to find.

26.10 Executable synthesis: algebra, derivatives, and nonattainment

This code checks the centered slope identity against a least-squares factorization, tests a regularized logistic gradient in a non-coordinate direction, and exhibits both positive curvature and a separate unregularized separability limit. The examples are constructed and have no application-specific predictive claim.

import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=6, suppress=True)
x = np.array([0., 1., 2., 3.])
y = np.array([1., 2., 2., 5.])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(4), x])
beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
xc = x-x.mean()
yc = y-y.mean()
rho = (xc@yc)/(np.linalg.norm(xc)*np.linalg.norm(yc))
print('OLS intercept, slope:', beta)
print(
    f'rho={
        rho:.6f} slope_from_rho={
            rho *
            np.linalg.norm(yc) /
            np.linalg.norm(xc):.6f}')
A = np.array([[1., -2.], [1., -1.], [1., 0.], [1., 1.], [1., 2.]])
target = np.array([0., 0., 1., 0., 1.])
theta = np.array([-0.2, 0.7])
lam = 0.1


def objective(theta):
    z = A@theta
    return np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, z)-target*z)+0.5*lam*(theta@theta)


z = A@theta
p = 1/(1+np.exp(-z))
g = A.T@(p-target)/len(target)+lam*theta
H = A.T@((p*(1-p))[:, None]*A)/len(target)+lam*np.eye(2)
d = np.array([0.3, -0.4])
eps = 1e-5
fd = (objective(theta+eps*d)-objective(theta-eps*d))/(2*eps)
print(f'logistic direction analytic={g@d:.8f} finite={fd:.8f}')
print('regularized Hessian eigenvalues:', np.linalg.eigvalsh(H))
for c in [1., 5., 20.]:
    print(f'separable scale={c:.0f} loss={np.logaddexp(0, -c):.10f}')
print('stable losses at signed margins -1000,1000:',
      np.logaddexp(0, -np.array([-1000., 1000.])))

26.11 Six worked problems

Problem 1: interpolation and repeated inputs

Problem. Find a polynomial of degree at most two through $(0,2),(1,1),(2,4)$. What changes if the last point is replaced by $(1,4)$?

Solution

Set $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2$. The first point gives $a_0=2$, the second $a_1+a_2=-1$, and the third $2a_1+4a_2=2$. Subtracting twice the second equation gives $2a_2=4$, hence $a_2=2$ and $a_1=-3$. Thus $p(x)=2-3x+2x^2$. The Vandermonde determinant for inputs 0,1,2 is 2, establishing uniqueness. With $(1,4)$ instead, two observations demand $p(1)=1$ and $p(1)=4$, so no function can interpolate both. Distinct data pairs do not replace the requirement of distinct input values for this square interpolation system.

Problem 2: finish a complete statistical line fit

Problem. For the four-point example in §26.3, compute the slope, intercept, residual sum of squares, and in-sample $R^2$. State which quantities need nonzero input or response variance.

Solution

Direct sums give $\bar x=1.5$, $\bar y=2.5$, $S_{xx}=5$, $S_{xy}=6$, and $S_{yy}=9$. Thus the slope is $6/5$, intercept $2.5-(6/5)1.5=0.7$, and squared error $9-36/5=1.8$. The intercept-model baseline error is 9, so $R^2=1-1.8/9=0.8$. A unique slope needs $S_{xx}\gt 0$; the stated $R^2$ and correlation additionally need $S_{yy}\gt 0$. If all targets were equal, the fitted constant could be exact while the ratio defining this $R^2$ would have zero denominator.

Problem 3: translate odds into decisions

Problem. A logistic model has $\operatorname{logit}p=-1+0.5x$. Find the probability at $x=2$, the odds multiplier for a two-unit increase, and the score threshold corresponding to probability $0.8$.

Solution

At $x=2$, the score is zero and $p=1/2$. A two-unit increase adds one to the log odds, so odds multiply by $e$, regardless of the starting input. Probability itself does not multiply by $e$. The logit of $0.8$ is $\log(0.8/0.2)=\log4$. Thus the decision rule $p\ge0.8$ is $-1+0.5x\ge\log4$, or $x\ge2(1+\log4)\approx4.7726$. The threshold remains an affine boundary, shifted from its half-probability location.

Problem 4: fit an intercept-only logistic model

Problem. There are $n$ binary outcomes with $k$ ones, and the model uses a constant probability $p=\sigma(b)$. Find the maximum-likelihood intercept when $0\lt k\lt n$, then examine $k=0$ and $k=n$.

Solution

Average negative log likelihood is $J(b)=\log(1+e^b)-(k/n)b$. Its derivative is $\sigma(b)-k/n$ and its second derivative is $\sigma(b)(1-\sigma(b))\gt 0$ at finite $b$. Setting the derivative to zero gives $p=k/n$ and $b=\log[k/(n-k)]$, the unique minimum. For $k=0$, $J$ tends to zero only as $b\to-\infty$; for $k=n$, it tends to zero only as $b\to+\infty$. The boundary proportions have no finite logit. Convexity does not cure this lack of a finite maximizing parameter.

Problem 5: prove the curvature formula in a chosen direction

Problem. Let $A$ be any design matrix and $D=\operatorname{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))$ at finite logistic parameters. Show why $A^\top D A$ is positive semidefinite, and determine when it is positive definite. Does that guarantee existence of an unregularized minimum?

Solution

For any vector $v$, $v^\top A^\top DAv=\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(a_i^\top v)^2$. Every weight is strictly positive at finite scores, so this is nonnegative and equals zero precisely when $Av=0$. It is positive for all nonzero $v$ exactly when $A$ has full column rank. The Hessian condition gives strict convexity and at most one finite minimizer. It does not ensure attainment. The two-point separable slope example has positive curvature at every finite slope but loss decreasing toward zero at an infinite slope. Adding a positive penalty to every parameter provides coercivity as well as strict convexity.

Problem 6: distinguish a fit from a probability transformation

Problem. Two identical feature rows have binary labels 0 and 1. Compare an intercept-only ordinary least-squares fit followed by a sigmoid with the intercept-only logistic fit. What does this reveal about the caption shorthand “linear regression plus sigmoid”?

Solution

The least-squares intercept minimizes $b^2+(1-b)^2$, so $b=1/2$. Applying a sigmoid afterward produces $\sigma(1/2)\approx0.622459$. The logistic likelihood instead has one success in two trials, so the preceding calculation gives fitted probability $1/2$ and logit intercept zero. The results differ even in this smallest mixed-label example. Logistic regression uses an affine score followed by a sigmoid as its model, but usually estimates that score by Bernoulli likelihood rather than least squares on the original labels. A final decision threshold is yet another choice.

26.12 Read the course backward through the regression models L70 · 00:19

Spaces, bases, and transformations, L70 01:19–07:57. A vector space supplies linear combinations; a basis makes their coordinates unique. Polynomials, matrices, and coordinate arrays can all be vectors in suitable spaces. A linear map is determined by its action on a basis because $T(\sum_j c_jv_j)=\sum_j c_jT(v_j)$. Its kernel records directions that disappear, and its range records attainable outputs. These ideas explain why a rank-deficient regression design cannot identify all coefficient directions. Subspaces contain zero and are closed under linear combinations. Intersections of subspaces remain subspaces, while a union of two is a subspace only when one contains the other. An orthogonal complement is a subspace; a set-theoretic complement is a different construction.

Eigenvectors, orthogonality, and projections, L70 08:13–15:51. For an endomorphism, an eigenvector satisfies $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$. A diagonalization $A=P\Lambda P^{-1}$ requires a full eigenbasis and is not available for every square matrix. Real symmetric matrices do admit an orthonormal eigenbasis. In finite-dimensional inner-product spaces, every vector decomposes uniquely into a component in a subspace and one in its orthogonal complement. Their squared norms add by Pythagoras. Orthogonal projection matrices are symmetric and idempotent; a general oblique projection need only be idempotent. Least squares applies the orthogonal version to the column space of its design.

The four subspaces and SVD, L70 13:21–20:52. For $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$, $\ker A$ is orthogonal to the row space in $\mathbb R^d$, and $\ker A^\top$ is orthogonal to the column space in $\mathbb R^m$. The ranks of $A$, $A^\top$, $A^\top A$, and $AA^\top$ agree. An SVD $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ exists for rectangular matrices, with nonnegative singular values in $\Sigma$. The pseudoinverse is $A^+=V\Sigma^+U^\top$, where positive singular values are reciprocated; $\Sigma$ itself is not the pseudoinverse. The sum $A=\sum_{j=1}^r\sigma_j u_jv_j^\top$ displays the rank-one outer-product components of a rank-$r$ matrix. It is an expansion of this matrix, not by itself a complete basis of the entire matrix space.

PCA and separating geometry, L70 20:58–21:43. A centered covariance matrix has leading eigenvectors that maximize projected variance under norm constraints. Its eigenvalues are nonnegative, so the leading eigenspaces rank variation. Support-vector classification uses separating geometry with a different objective: margin and, when needed, penalties for violations. PCA does not use class labels and need not preserve the directions most useful for classification. Chapter 23 connects PCA's stationarity equations to its global variance comparison.

Probability spaces and conditioning, L70 21:50–25:25. A probability model consists of a sample space, a sigma-algebra of measurable events, and a countably additive probability measure with total mass one. Independence means $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$, while conditional probability $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$ requires $P(B)\gt 0$ in this elementary form. Mutually exclusive positive-probability events are not independent. The law of total probability decomposes over a measurable partition, and Bayes' rule updates a prior through a likelihood and evidence normalization. These distinctions support conditional-response modeling without turning every conditional probability into a causal statement.

Random variables and joint distributions, L70 25:25–28:27. A random variable is a measurable numerical function on the sample space. A mass function assigns atom probabilities; a density integrates to interval probabilities when an absolutely continuous model applies. A cumulative distribution function describes every real-valued random variable, including mixtures of discrete and continuous components. Means, variances, joint distributions, and marginals summarize different aspects. Covariance changes with measurement units; correlation standardizes linear association when both variances are positive. Zero correlation is weaker than independence in general. The logistic Bernoulli response has mean equal to its success probability, explaining why the regression link targets that probability.

Sample geometry, bounds, and covariance, L70 28:33–30:30. The projection of a data vector onto the all-ones direction is its mean times that vector. The remaining centered vector yields variance after choosing the denominator. Markov's inequality bounds $P(Z\ge a)$ by $\mathbb EZ/a$ for a nonnegative random variable and $a\gt 0$; Chebyshev applies it to a squared centered deviation with finite variance. Under suitable assumptions, a central limit theorem describes scaled averages, not arbitrary raw data. A covariance matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite because $v^\top\Sigma v=\operatorname{Var}(v^\top X)\ge0$. Nonnegative eigenvalues imply a nonnegative determinant; the determinant's sign alone does not certify positive semidefiniteness. These are the same quadratic-form tests used for the regression Hessians.

Calculus, optimization, and networks, L70 30:30–32:48. A derivative is a local linear map; a scalar gradient represents it under an inner product. Jacobians handle vector outputs, and Hessians describe second-order scalar curvature. Matrix differentials keep tensor shapes manageable. Stationarity, constraint qualification, and second-order checks determine what an optimization equation actually proves. Gradient descent and stochastic gradients turn derivatives into algorithms; backpropagation efficiently organizes the chain rule through a network. Classification, clustering, linear regression, and logistic regression then make different uses of these shared tools. No one tool supplies model validity, finite-sample accuracy, or global success without its own assumptions.
