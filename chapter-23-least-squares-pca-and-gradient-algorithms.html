23. Least squares, PCA, and gradient algorithms

A small regression problem can be solved by a factorization, by repeated gradient updates, or by a Newton step. They can target the same mathematical optimum while having different costs and failure modes. Principal component analysis provides a second test: its stationary equations also contain eigenvectors, but only a comparison of their objective values identifies the desired direction.

Lecture spine: Lectures 59–61. The examples, algorithm checks, and worked problems are original. Column gradients and observation-row data matrices are used consistently. Sum and average objectives are distinguished explicitly.

23.1 Differentiate the residual objective L59 · 05:17

Let $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ contain fixed feature rows, $y\in\mathbb R^n$ contain targets, and $w\in\mathbb R^d$ be the coefficients. The average squared-error objective is $J(w)=\|Xw-y\|_2^2/(2n)$. Write $r=Xw-y$ for the residual. Expanding a parameter perturbation $h$ gives

$$J(w+h)-J(w)=\frac1n r^\top Xh+\frac1{2n}\|Xh\|_2^2.$$

The gradient is therefore $g(w)=X^\top(Xw-y)/n$, and the Hessian is $H=X^\top X/n$. For every $h$, $h^\top Hh=\|Xh\|_2^2/n\ge0$, so the objective is convex. Setting the gradient to zero gives $X^\top Xw=X^\top y$, the normal equations.

To prove their sufficiency, let $w_*$ satisfy them. The expansion at $w_*$ loses its linear term and leaves $J(w_*+h)-J(w_*)=\|Xh\|^2/(2n)\ge0$. Thus every stationary point is a global minimizer. Coefficients are unique precisely when $X$ has full column rank. Otherwise adding a vector in $\ker X$ changes no fitted value. More observations than coefficients do not guarantee independent columns.

The familiar inverse expression $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ requires full column rank. For computation, a QR or singular-value factorization avoids explicitly forming that inverse. The rank assumption is essential in applying the derivation in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §9.2, printed pages 292–294. Least-squares geometry itself does not require Gaussian noise; that assumption supplies an additional likelihood interpretation.

23.2 A centered design separates the coefficient directions

Use three constructed observations with feature values $-1,0,1$ and responses $1,2,4$. Include an intercept, so $X$ has rows $(1,-1),(1,0),(1,1)$. Its normal equations are diagonal: $X^\top X=\operatorname{diag}(3,2)$ and $X^\top y=(7,3)^\top$. The minimizing coefficients are $w_*=(7/3,3/2)^\top$.

The predictions are $(5/6,7/3,23/6)^\top$ and residuals are $(-1/6,1/3,-1/6)^\top$. Their sum is zero and their dot product with the feature column is zero, exactly the two normal equations. The average objective is $(1/36+1/9+1/36)/6=1/36$. This is an imperfect fit that is nevertheless optimal within the stated affine class.

The Hessian eigenvalues are $1$ and $2/3$. Centering makes the intercept and slope orthogonal on these observations, so each coefficient direction can be analyzed independently. This improves the numerical geometry without changing the underlying prediction class. Centering constants used for deployment must come from the fitting data, not from the held-out evaluation sample.

23.3 Principal directions require a unit-length constraint L59 · 17:28

Let $Z\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ be centered observations with $n\gt1$, and let $S=Z^\top Z/(n-1)$ be their sample covariance matrix. For a unit vector $v\in\mathbb R^d$, the projected coordinate vector is $Zv$. Its sample variance is $v^\top Sv$. Without the constraint $v^\top v=1$, scaling $v$ scales that variance quadratically, so the maximization would usually be unbounded.

Use the Lagrangian $\mathcal L(v,\lambda)=v^\top Sv-\lambda(v^\top v-1)$. Symmetry gives stationarity $2Sv-2\lambda v=0$, hence $Sv=\lambda v$. Multiplying by $v^\top$ shows $v^\top Sv=\lambda$. This identifies the variance at an eigenvector. It still leaves the task of choosing among eigenvalues.

Because $S$ is real symmetric, take an orthonormal eigenbasis $q_j$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d$. Writing a unit vector as $v=\sum_j a_jq_j$ gives $\sum_ja_j^2=1$ and $v^\top Sv=\sum_j\lambda_ja_j^2\le\lambda_1$. Equality is achieved in the top eigenspace, proving the global maximum. A repeated largest eigenvalue makes the first principal direction nonunique. The multiplier argument and variance comparison are checked against §10.2, printed pages 320–322.

The covariance denominator $n$ used for an empirical-population convention rescales every eigenvalue by $(n-1)/n$ but leaves eigenvectors unchanged. The convention must still be stated when reporting variance magnitudes. Also, translating all projected coordinates by a constant does not change variance. Therefore maximizing variance cannot identify a free offset; choosing the sample mean as a reconstruction center answers an additional approximation question.

23.4 Gradient descent turns a derivative into an iteration L60 · 11:41

Gradient descent uses $w_{k+1}=w_k-\eta g(w_k)$ with positive step size $\eta$. All updated coordinates use the same old vector. A hierarchical nonlinear model can make this gradient expensive, but the update still refers to a scalar objective and a vector of fitted parameters. Its successful reduction on training observations is a numerical claim, separate from predictive performance.

For a quadratic with minimizer $w_*$, define error $e_k=w_k-w_*$. The normal equations imply $g(w_k)=He_k$, so $e_{k+1}=(I-\eta H)e_k$. In a Hessian eigenvector direction with positive eigenvalue $\lambda$, the scalar error is multiplied by $1-\eta\lambda$ each iteration. It contracts exactly when $0\lt\eta\lt2/\lambda$. Thus a constant step works in every positive-curvature direction when $0\lt\eta\lt2/\lambda_{\max}$.

At the upper boundary, the largest-curvature component can oscillate without shrinking. Above it, that component grows. For the running design, $\lambda_{\max}=1$, so $\eta=1/2$ is stable. A zero Hessian eigenvalue has multiplier one: an initial null-space coefficient component persists. Starting at zero keeps least-squares iterates in the row space and, when they converge, selects the minimum-norm solution. Different initial null components select different coefficients with the same fitted values.

For a smooth nonquadratic objective, a useful extension assumes the gradient is $L$-Lipschitz along the update region: $\|g(x)-g(y)\|\le L\|x-y\|$. Integrating the derivative along the step gives the descent bound $J(w-\eta g)\le J(w)-\eta(1-L\eta/2)\|g\|^2$. This guarantees decrease for $0\lt\eta\lt2/L$ when the assumptions hold and the gradient is nonzero. It does not by itself guarantee arrival at a global minimizer of a nonconvex objective.

23.5 Small updates do not identify the reason for stopping L61 · 01:34

A small update can result from a small gradient, an extremely small learning rate, numerical rounding, or a stopping rule. These cases have different meanings. A small gradient establishes approximate stationarity under the chosen scaling. It can also occur near a maximum or saddle. If a quadratic Hessian is positive definite with smallest eigenvalue $\mu\gt0$, $g=He$ implies $\|e\|\le\|g\|/\mu$, providing a coefficient-error bound. Without positive curvature, that conclusion fails.

Random restarts explore different basins in a nonconvex problem and compare the attained objective values. If an initialization independently lands in a successful basin with probability $p$, then $r$ restarts hit it with probability $1-(1-p)^r$. This conditional calculation explains a possible benefit. It is not a distribution-free promise of finding a global optimum: the relevant basin may have zero probability under the chosen initialization, and each run may fail to converge.

23.6 Stochastic gradients change the sampling and cost L61 · 04:47

Write the average objective as $J(w)=n^{-1}\sum_i\ell_i(w)$. For a fixed current $w$, draw an index $I$ uniformly with replacement. Then $g_I(w)=\nabla\ell_I(w)$ satisfies $E[g_I(w)\mid w]=\nabla J(w)$. This follows by multiplying each possible gradient by probability $1/n$ and summing. If the objective is a sum rather than an average, an unbiased estimator of its gradient is $n g_I$; changing normalization changes the correct scaling.

A batch average of $b$ independent sampled gradients has covariance $\Sigma(w)/b$, where $\Sigma(w)$ is the covariance of one sample. The cross-covariance terms vanish by independence. Sampling without replacement or sequentially reshuffling the dataset has different conditional properties. An unbiased update direction in expectation need not decrease the full objective at every realized step.

At zero in our running case, the three one-example gradients are $(-1,1),(-2,0),(-4,-4)$. Their average is $(-7/3,-1)$, agreeing with the full gradient. At the least-squares optimum the individual residuals remain nonzero, so individual gradients still disagree. Constant-step stochastic iterates can fluctuate even near the optimum. Diminishing-step convergence theorems need additional assumptions on smoothness, gradient noise, and step schedules; unbiasedness alone is insufficient.

Noise can sometimes help leave a stationary region, but it is not a universal escape mechanism. If every individual example has zero gradient at the same suboptimal point, stochastic sampling leaves that point unchanged too. The cost and sampling motivation is consistent with §7.1.3, printed pages 231–232; the assumptions above delimit the claims used here.

23.7 Newton minimizes a local quadratic model L61 · 16:22

At the current point, approximate the objective change by $g^\top s+s^\top Hs/2$. If $H$ is positive definite, differentiating with respect to the proposed step $s$ gives $g+Hs=0$. The Newton step solves $Hs=-g$, so the formal expression is $s=-H^{-1}g$. The factor of one half cancels when differentiating the symmetric quadratic; there is no additional factor of two in the step. For a positive-definite quadratic this reaches its exact minimizer in one exact-arithmetic step.

For a general nonlinear objective, the quadratic model is local. An indefinite Hessian can make its stationary solution unsuitable as a minimizing step, and even a descent direction may need damping or a line search before a finite update is accepted. In implementation one normally solves the linear system instead of explicitly forming the inverse. The derivation and conditions match Boyd and Vandenberghe, §9.5.1, printed pages 484–486.

Quasi-Newton methods build curvature approximations from changes in iterates and gradients. For $s_k=w_{k+1}-w_k$ and $y_k=g_{k+1}-g_k$, a Hessian approximation can enforce the secant relation $B_{k+1}s_k=y_k$. This imitates the gradient-change equation of a quadratic along an observed direction. BFGS is a particular symmetric update strategy; positive curvature safeguards matter. The official SciPy BFGS documentation describes skipping or damping updates when its curvature condition fails. Limited-memory variants retain selected correction information rather than a full dense matrix, as documented for L-BFGS-B. These implementation descriptions are not global convergence claims for arbitrary objectives.

23.8 Compare the algorithms on constructed data

This NumPy program makes no file or network changes. It compares gradient descent and one Newton step against an independent least-squares factorization, checks the sampled-gradient mean, and verifies a principal variance for four centered points. It uses exact small models whose mathematical targets are known; it is not a performance benchmark.

import numpy as np

X = np.column_stack((np.ones(3), [-1., 0., 1.]))
y = np.array([1., 2., 4.])
H = X.T @ X / len(X)
reference = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
w = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(100):
    w -= 0.5 * (X.T @ (X @ w - y) / len(X))
assert np.allclose(w, reference)
grad0 = -X.T @ y / len(X)
newton = -np.linalg.solve(H, grad0)
assert np.allclose(newton, reference)
individual = -y[:, None] * X
assert np.allclose(individual.mean(axis=0), grad0)
Z = np.array([[2., 0.], [-2., 0.], [0., 1.], [0., -1.]])
S = Z.T @ Z / (len(Z) - 1)
eigenvalues, directions = np.linalg.eigh(S)
assert np.isclose(eigenvalues[-1], 8/3)
print("least-squares coefficients:", np.round(reference, 6))
print("gradient eigenvalues:", np.round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(H), 6))
print(f"GD error={np.linalg.norm(w-reference):.6f}")
print(f"Newton error={np.linalg.norm(newton-reference):.6f}")
print("one-example mean gradient:", np.round(individual.mean(axis=0), 6))
print("PCA sample variances:", np.round(eigenvalues, 6))

23.9 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · A stable-step boundary

For $J(w)=3w^2/2$, classify constant-step gradient descent for positive $\eta$, including the boundary.

Solution

The derivative is $3w$, so $w_{k+1}=(1-3\eta)w_k$. It converges to zero from every start exactly when $|1-3\eta|\lt1$, or $0\lt\eta\lt2/3$. At $2/3$ a nonzero start alternates sign forever; above it the magnitude grows. A zero start remains stationary in every case, so a single successful initialization would not establish convergence from all starts.

Problem 2 · Newton's normalization

For a positive-definite quadratic with minimizer $w_*$, derive the effect of using $s=-2H^{-1}g$ instead of the Newton step.

Solution

For the quadratic, $g=H(w-w_*)$. The correct step gives $w+s=w_*$. The doubled step gives new error $e_{k+1}=e_k-2e_k=-e_k$. A nonzero error therefore oscillates without shrinking, and its quadratic objective gap remains unchanged. The missing cancellation of the Taylor factor one half is a consequential algorithmic error, not a harmless normalization.

Problem 3 · Which eigenvector maximizes variance?

For $S=\operatorname{diag}(5,2,0)$, characterize every unit direction achieving maximum variance. What changes if the second eigenvalue is also five?

Solution

The variance is $5v_1^2+2v_2^2$ with $v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2=1$. It equals $5-3v_2^2-5v_3^2$, so the maximizers are $v=(\pm1,0,0)$. If both first eigenvalues are five, the variance is $5(1-v_3^2)$, and every unit vector in the first-two-coordinate plane maximizes it. A repeated top eigenvalue identifies a subspace rather than one privileged axis.

Problem 4 · Nonuniform sampling

If example $i$ is sampled with probability $q_i\gt0$, find an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the average objective.

Solution

Use $g_I/(nq_I)$. Its expectation is $\sum_iq_i g_i/(nq_i)=n^{-1}\sum_ig_i=\nabla J$. Without the correction, it targets a probability-weighted objective instead. Very small $q_i$ produce large weights and can increase variance, so unbiasedness does not by itself make an estimator computationally useful.

Problem 5 · Noise without escape

Construct a nonconvex finite-sum objective for which every sampled gradient is zero at a nonoptimal stationary point.

Solution

Let every example loss be $\ell_i(w)=(w^2-1)^2$. At $w=0$, every gradient $4w(w^2-1)$ is zero. Yet the objective is one there and zero at $w=\pm1$. Both full-gradient and stochastic-gradient updates remain at zero from that exact initialization. Sampling introduces no update noise when all per-example gradients agree at zero. General statements that stochasticity necessarily escapes suboptimal stationary points are therefore false.

Problem 6 · Compare resources fairly

Design a comparison between full-gradient, minibatch, and Newton methods for the running regression, and explain what it can and cannot establish.

Solution

Use the same fixed data, objective normalization, starting coefficients, numerical precision, and accuracy target. Record objective gap, coefficient error against the factorization, gradient evaluations, linear-system work, and elapsed time separately. Tune any step parameters using a stated development procedure. For tiny matrices, setup and interpreter overhead can dominate time, so the result mainly checks correctness rather than large-scale throughput. A lower training objective or a faster solve does not establish lower future prediction error; that requires a separate held-out evaluation and statistical assumptions.
