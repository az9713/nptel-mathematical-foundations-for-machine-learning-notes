10. Margins, support vectors, and the linear algebra synthesis

A monitor must distinguish ordinary measurements near zero from unusually large positive or negative measurements. A threshold on the signed measurement cannot accept both extremes while rejecting the middle. A nonlinear representation can make this task linearly separable, but it also changes what a large safety margin means. That distinction connects classification with the linear algebra developed so far.

Recording spine: Lecture 27 and the substantive questions in Lecture 28. The monitoring example, proofs, code, and exercises are original teaching constructions. A nonlinear feature lift is distinct from an orthogonal projection.

10.1 A separating score is not yet a distance Lecture 27 · 01:17

Let $x_i\in\mathbb R^d$ be observation $i$'s column feature vector and $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ its supplied label, for $i=1,\ldots,n$. These observations are supervised because training receives the desired labels. Choose a nonzero normal vector $w\in\mathbb R^d$ and an offset $b\in\mathbb R$. The score $f(x)=w^\top x+b$ defines the hyperplane $H=\{x:f(x)=0\}$. Its positive and negative sides define two classes; ties need an explicit convention.

A hyperplane is affine: it need not pass through zero. For two points $z_1,z_2\in H$, subtraction gives $w^\top(z_1-z_2)=0$, so $w$ is perpendicular to every displacement within it. Multiplying both $w$ and $b$ by a positive constant preserves the classifier while multiplying every score. A raw score therefore cannot be a geometric distance.

Consider the point $z=x-tw$ and require $z\in H$. Substitution gives $t=(w^\top x+b)/\|w\|_2^2$, where $\|w\|_2=\sqrt{w^\top w}$. Every other point of $H$ differs from $z$ in a direction perpendicular to $w$. Pythagoras proves that $z$ is nearest, giving

$$\operatorname{dist}(x,H)=\frac{|w^\top x+b|}{\|w\|_2}.$$

The signed geometric margin of a labeled point is $y_if(x_i)/\|w\|_2$. It is positive for correct strict classification and negative for a point on the wrong side. Unlike a score, it is invariant to positive parameter rescaling. The reference derivation appears in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, Sections 12.1–12.2, printed pages 372–377.

10.2 Maximize the smallest gap Lecture 27 · 04:24

Assume that the finite training set contains at least one observation of each label, $-1$ and $+1$. A finite dataset is strictly linearly separable if some $(w,b)$ makes every signed score positive. Let $m=\min_i y_if(x_i)\gt0$. Dividing $w$ and $b$ by $m$ gives an equivalent classifier whose smallest signed score is one. This normalizes parameters, not data coordinates or physical units. Under that convention, the smallest geometric distance is $1/\|w\|_2$.

The hard-margin support vector machine maximizes this distance by solving

$$\min_{w,b}\frac12\|w\|_2^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1\quad(i=1,\ldots,n).$$

The supporting hyperplanes with scores $+1$ and $-1$ are separated by $2/\|w\|_2$. Here “margin” means the one-sided nearest-point distance; another convention calls that full width the margin. A support vector is a training observation attaining equality in a margin constraint.

The perturbation interpretation is deterministic and metric-specific. A feature perturbation $\delta$ changes the score by $w^\top\delta$, whose magnitude is at most $\|w\|_2\|\delta\|_2$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. A correctly classified point of margin $r$ keeps its sign for every perturbation of norm strictly below $r$. This does not guarantee semantic robustness or success on an arbitrary future population.

Overlapping labels can make the hard constraints infeasible. A soft-margin extension permits nonnegative slack variables $\xi_i$ with $y_if(x_i)\ge1-\xi_i$, and penalizes $\|w\|_2^2/2+C\sum_i\xi_i$ for $C\gt0$. Slack between zero and one can describe a correctly signed point inside the margin, rather than a classification error. The penalty constant and feature scaling jointly determine the trade-off.

10.3 A higher-dimensional representation can change separability Lecture 27 · 07:52

Use scalar measurements $x=-3,-1,1,3$. Give the inner points $\pm1$ label $-1$ and the outer points $\pm3$ label $+1$. No affine scalar score $ax+b$ realizes those strict signs. Positivity at both extremes requires $b\gt3|a|$, while negativity at both inner points requires $b\lt-|a|$. The conditions contradict one another.

Define $\phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^2$ by $\phi(x)=(x,x^2)^\top$. Inner points have second coordinate one; outer points have second coordinate nine. The horizontal line at second coordinate five separates them. The classifier is affine in transformed features and nonlinear in the original measurement.

Its hard-margin optimum can be proved without a solver. Write the transformed normal as $w=(a,c)^\top$. Averaging the two positive constraints gives $9c+b\ge1$. Averaging the negative constraints gives $c+b\le-1$. Hence $c\ge1/4$, so every feasible normal has length at least $1/4$. The choice $a=0$, $c=1/4$, $b=-5/4$ attains that lower bound and all four constraints. It is therefore globally optimal.

All signed scores equal one, giving transformed one-sided margin four. In the original variable the boundary is $x^2=5$, or the two thresholds $\pm\sqrt5$. The nearest original-coordinate distance is different. A robustness radius in the transformed Euclidean metric cannot silently become a radius in the sensor's physical units.

Lifting does not guarantee separability for every labeling. Identical inputs with opposite labels remain identical after any deterministic feature map. Increasing dimension can expose useful structure, but neither dimensionality nor separability establishes generalization. PCA's variance criterion and SVM's separation criterion also answer different questions; a low-variance coordinate may be essential for the labels.

10.4 Kernels compute feature inner products indirectly Lecture 27 · 18:55

A kernel associated with a real feature map is $k(x,z)=\phi(x)^\top\phi(z)$. Here $k(x,z)=xz+x^2z^2$. An algorithm expressed entirely through feature inner products can substitute this formula without constructing transformed vectors. That substitution is the kernel trick; an explicit feature lift alone is not yet an implicit-kernel implementation.

For finite inputs $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, let the Gram matrix have entries $K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)$. For every real coefficient vector $\alpha$,

$$\alpha^\top K\alpha=\left\|\sum_i\alpha_i\phi(x_i)\right\|_2^2\ge0.$$

A feature inner-product kernel therefore produces symmetric positive semidefinite Gram matrices. An arbitrary similarity formula need not. Kernel choice specifies a geometry and its assumptions; it does not provide a universally correct similarity measure. The finite-feature argument establishes the mechanism without requiring a dual optimization derivation.

10.5 Rank and nullity separate retained and lost directions Lecture 28 · 08:34

Let $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$ represent a linear map. Its kernel is $\ker A=\{v:Av=0\}$; its image is $\{Av:v\in\mathbb R^d\}$. Their dimensions are nullity and rank. Choose a kernel basis $v_1,\ldots,v_q$ and extend it to a domain basis $v_1,\ldots,v_d$. The images $Av_{q+1},\ldots,Av_d$ span the image because the first $q$ basis elements disappear.

Those images are independent. If their linear combination vanishes, the corresponding combination of added basis vectors belongs to the kernel and can also be expressed using the first $q$ vectors. Independence of the full domain basis forces all coefficients to vanish. Counting image basis vectors proves

$$\operatorname{rank}A+\dim\ker A=d.$$

For $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&0\\0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, rank is two and the kernel is spanned by $(-2,1,0)^\top$. Inputs differing along that line have identical outputs. Row reduction expresses the same accounting as pivot variables plus free variables. Rank counts independent linear directions; it does not count every statistically or semantically useful feature.

The row space is $\operatorname{im}A^\top$. A vector is orthogonal to every row exactly when $Av=0$, so $(\operatorname{im}A^\top)^\perp=\ker A$. Taking dimensions gives $\operatorname{rank}A^\top=d-\dim\ker A=\operatorname{rank}A$. The left kernel $\ker A^\top$ is the orthogonal complement of the column space in $\mathbb R^m$. These complements occupy different ambient spaces when $m\ne d$.

10.6 Which transformations preserve which quantities? Lecture 28 · 31:47

An invertible coordinate transformation preserves recoverability of every original vector, but need not preserve lengths, angles, or Euclidean margins. An orthogonal square matrix $Q$, defined by $Q^\top Q=I$, preserves inner products because $(Qx)^\top(Qz)=x^\top z$. Its inverse is its transpose. A projection onto a proper subspace is linear but loses components in its nonzero kernel. Linearity alone does not imply information preservation.

A basis must span as well as be independent. An independent list that omits a direction cannot represent every vector in the space. For nonzero orthogonal basis vectors $a_j$, coefficient $j$ of $x$ is $a_j^\top x/\|a_j\|^2$. The denominator disappears only for unit vectors. Likewise, orthogonality of raw sample columns differs from zero covariance, which concerns centered columns. Independence in probability is stronger than zero linear correlation.

Diagonalization $A=PDP^{-1}$ gives $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$. Computing the diagonal power is inexpensive after obtaining the basis change, but storing and applying dense $P$ and $P^{-1}$ still costs work. Some real matrices lack real eigenbases; symmetric real matrices have orthonormal ones. A diagonal covariance matrix means pairwise zero covariance, not general statistical independence.

Truncating an SVD gives a low-rank approximation under a specified norm. It may discard small-energy directions important for labels. Full-rank data can still admit a useful approximate low-rank representation; exact rank is not a measure of prediction importance. PCA, SVD, and SVM can be useful on nonlinear datasets, but their subspaces or chosen kernels limit what they represent. Performance must be assessed for the particular task.

10.7 An executable geometry check

The following NumPy program checks the exact lifted solution, the feature-kernel identity, and rank–nullity for the constructed matrix. Its finite toy inputs are fixed, and it has no file or network effects. Assertions compare the implementation with independently derived results. They check these constructions, not a production classifier.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([-3.0, -1.0, 1.0, 3.0])
y = np.array([1.0, -1.0, -1.0, 1.0])
features = np.column_stack((x, x**2))
w = np.array([0.0, 0.25])
b = -1.25
signed = y * (features @ w + b)
assert np.allclose(signed, 1.0)
assert np.isclose(np.min(signed) / np.linalg.norm(w), 4.0)
gram = np.outer(x, x) + np.outer(x**2, x**2)
assert np.allclose(gram, features @ features.T)
assert np.linalg.eigvalsh(gram).min() > -1e-10
A = np.array([[1.0, 2.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]])
assert np.allclose(A @ np.array([-2.0, 1.0, 0.0]), 0.0)
rank = int(np.linalg.matrix_rank(A))
assert rank + 1 == A.shape[1]
print("lifted signed scores:", signed.tolist())
print("one-sided lifted margin:", min(signed) / np.linalg.norm(w))
print("rank, nullity:", rank, A.shape[1] - rank)

lifted signed scores: [1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0]
one-sided lifted margin: 4.0
rank, nullity: 2 1

10.8 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Scale and distance

For $f(x_1,x_2)=3x_1+4x_2-10$, find the distance from $(2,2)$ to the zero hyperplane. Explain the effect of doubling all coefficients.

Solution

The score is four and the normal length is five, giving distance $4/5$. Doubling coefficients doubles both quantities, leaving the ratio unchanged. The projected point is $(2,2)-(4/25)(3,4)=(38/25,34/25)$; direct substitution gives hyperplane score zero. This checks both distance and projection formulas.

Problem 2 · An impossible labeling

Why can no deterministic feature map separate two identical inputs with opposite labels by a positive margin?

Solution

Both map to $\phi(x)$. Their two constraints require $w^\top\phi(x)+b\ge1$ and its negative to be at least one. Adding gives $0\ge2$, a contradiction. Additional feature dimensions cannot repair an input collision. Label-noise treatment, additional measured information, or a changed task is required.

Problem 3 · A family of exact solutions

Keep inner measurements $\pm1$ but replace outer measurements by $\pm r$, where $r\gt1$. Derive the optimal lifted normal, offset, and margin.

Solution

For normal $(a,c)$, averaged outer constraints give $r^2c+b\ge1$, while inner constraints give $c+b\le-1$. Thus $c\ge2/(r^2-1)$. The norm lower bound is attained by $a=0$, $c=2/(r^2-1)$, $b=-(r^2+1)/(r^2-1)$. Every signed score is one, so the one-sided lifted margin is $(r^2-1)/2$. It tends to zero as the two classes collide at $r=1$.

Problem 4 · Three preservation claims

Give a linear map that preserves recoverability but changes Euclidean distance, and another that loses recoverability.

Solution

$D=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$ is invertible, with inverse $\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1)$. It doubles distances along the first axis and preserves distances along the second. The projector $P=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ maps both $(1,0)$ and $(1,5)$ to $(1,0)$, so no inverse recovers every input. These examples separate invertibility, isometry, and linearity.

Problem 5 · Reject an invalid kernel

A proposed two-point Gram matrix is $K=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Can it arise from real feature inner products?

Solution

For $\alpha=(1,-1)^\top$, its quadratic form is $1+1-4=-2$. A squared length cannot be negative, so this matrix cannot be a real inner-product Gram matrix. Nonnegative diagonal entries were insufficient. The chapter's polynomial kernel passes because its quadratic form is explicitly the squared norm of a feature-vector sum for every coefficient vector.

Problem 6 · Compression can erase the labels

Four centered points are $(10,\epsilon),(10,-\epsilon),(-10,\epsilon),(-10,-\epsilon)$, with $0\lt\epsilon\lt10$. Label by the sign of the second coordinate. What does one-component PCA retain?

Solution

The cross-product sum is zero by cancellation. Diagonal sums of squares are $400$ and $4\epsilon^2$, so sample covariance is diagonal with entries $400/3$ and $4\epsilon^2/3$. Its leading direction is the first axis. Keeping that coordinate merges each pair with opposite labels at $+10$ and $-10$. Every deterministic classifier using only this coordinate must misclassify at least one point in each pair, giving error at least one half under equal weights. Preserving almost all variance can discard the information needed for the target.
