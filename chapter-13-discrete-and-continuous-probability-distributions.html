13. Discrete and continuous probability distributions

A service records whether a request succeeds, how many of four requests succeed, how long it waits for the first success, and the elapsed time until a response. These quantities describe the same system but require different probability laws. Choosing a distribution means specifying a sampling mechanism and the quantity being measured, rather than selecting a familiar curve by its appearance.

Recording spine: Lectures 35–37. All service examples and executable checks are original teaching constructions. Parameters below are specified model inputs, not measured service statistics.

13.1 A mass function adds all outcomes mapped to the same value Lecture 35 · 02:05

Let $X$ be a real random variable whose law is concentrated on a finite or countable set $S$. Its probability mass function, abbreviated PMF, is $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$. It is nonnegative and satisfies $\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)=1$. For a numerical event $E$, $P(X\in E)=\sum_{x\in E\cap S}p_X(x)$. The set $S$ need not be equally spaced, and points outside it have zero mass.

For a discrete underlying experiment, $p_X(x)$ is the sum of the probabilities of all elementary outcomes mapped to $x$. Counting successes in a sequence forgets their order. For example, the sequences $001,010,100$ all map to count one. If their probabilities differ, one must add their actual weights; counting three sequences and dividing by eight assumes all eight sequences are equally likely.

A many-to-one map does not necessarily preserve the information needed for another question. A count of one success does not identify which request succeeded. Conversely, forgetting order is harmless when the task needs only the count. The law of a count captures that numerical quantity, not every property of the original process.

13.2 One trial and a fixed number of trials Lecture 35 · 23:40

A Bernoulli random variable $B$ takes value one for success and zero for failure. Its parameter $p\in[0,1]$ is $P(B=1)$; therefore $P(B=0)=1-p$. The labels one and zero encode the modeled outcome and need not imply positive or negative value. A defect indicator and a success indicator can both be Bernoulli variables.

Now let $B_1,\ldots,B_n$ be independent Bernoulli variables with the same parameter $p$, where $n$ is a fixed nonnegative integer. The count $S_n=\sum_iB_i$ has a binomial distribution. For $0\lt p\lt1$, every specified sequence with $k$ successes has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ by independence. There are $\binom nk=n!/[k!(n-k)!]$ choices of success positions, where factorial $j!$ is the product of positive integers up to $j$ and $0!=1$. Thus

$$P(S_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n.$$

The probabilities sum to $(p+1-p)^n=1$ by the binomial expansion. At $p=0$ the count is always zero, and at $p=1$ it is always $n$; these degenerate cases can be defined directly. For four requests with success probability $0.2$, the chance of exactly two successes is $6(0.2)^2(0.8)^2=0.1536$.

The iid assumption means independent and identically distributed. Equal marginal success probabilities alone do not imply independence: four perfectly synchronized copies of one Bernoulli outcome also each have success probability $p$, but their count is only zero or four. Different independent success probabilities instead yield products that depend on the selected positions and generally do not reduce to one binomial formula.

13.3 Replacement changes the law Lecture 36 · 10:51

Suppose a finite batch has $N$ objects, $M$ of which are marked success. Draw $n$ objects uniformly without replacement. Success in one draw changes the composition available for later draws, so the binomial model is generally incorrect. Counting unordered samples gives the hypergeometric law

$$P(S=k)=\frac{\binom Mk\binom{N-M}{n-k}}{\binom Nn},$$

for feasible integers $k$, with zero probability otherwise. The numerator chooses successful and unsuccessful objects separately; the denominator counts all equally likely size-$n$ samples. For $N=5$, $M=2$, $n=2$, both successes occur with probability $\binom22\binom30/\binom52=1/10$. With independent replacement the probability would be $(2/5)^2=4/25$. The distinction concerns the sampling mechanism, not a correction to the arithmetic.

Unconditionally, each draw without replacement still has success probability $M/N$. It is the conditional probability after earlier draws that changes. Identical marginal distributions and independence are separate requirements. This distinction is essential when modeling examples drawn from a finite dataset.

13.4 Waiting for the first success Lecture 36 · 26:52

Let repeated trials be independent with unchanged success probability $p\in(0,1]$. Let $T$ count the trial on which the first success occurs, including that successful trial. The event $T=k$ requires $k-1$ failures followed by a success. Consequently,

$$P(T=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots.$$

This is the geometric distribution under the trials-including-success convention. A convention counting failures uses $T-1$ and starts at zero. The masses sum to one by the geometric series. The tail has a direct interpretation: $P(T>k)=(1-p)^k$, because the first $k$ trials must all fail. Thus eventual success has probability one when $p\gt0$, although no finite deadline is guaranteed. At $p=0$, first success never occurs and a finite-valued geometric law is unavailable.

For $p=0.2$, first success at trial three has probability $0.8^2(0.2)=0.128$. After ten failures, the next-trial success probability is still $0.2$ under this model. This is memorylessness, not a claim that a real organization or learning system cannot improve or deteriorate. If trial conditions change, the fixed-$p$ model may be inappropriate.

13.5 Uniform choices and arrival counts Lecture 36 · 38:12

A discrete uniform variable on $m$ distinct values assigns mass $1/m$ to each. The values need not be consecutive integers. Equal likelihood is the modeling property; the number of values determines the common mass through normalization.

A Poisson variable $N$ with parameter $\lambda\ge0$ models a nonnegative integer count with mass $P(N=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. For $\lambda=0$, it is identically zero. For positive $\lambda$, normalization follows from $\sum_{k\ge0}\lambda^k/k!=e^\lambda$. The parameter is the expected count over the specified exposure interval, not automatically a rate per unit time. If a homogeneous Poisson process has rate $r$ arrivals per hour, its count over $t$ hours has parameter $rt$.

The Poisson mass can arise as a rare-event limit. For fixed $k$ and $\lambda$, substitute $p=\lambda/n$ into the binomial mass. The factor $\binom nk(\lambda/n)^k$ tends to $\lambda^k/k!$, and $(1-\lambda/n)^{n-k}$ tends to $e^{-\lambda}$. Their product gives the Poisson mass. This limiting construction uses many independent opportunities, each with a small event probability. Bursty or dependent arrivals need not follow it merely because they are counts.

13.6 A density has units of inverse measurement Lecture 37 · 01:33

A real distribution is absolutely continuous when it has a nonnegative density $f_X$ with total integral one and

$$P(a\lt X\le b)=\int_a^bf_X(x)\,dx.$$

Such a variable has zero probability at each individual point because an integral over a singleton vanishes. The density value $f_X(x)$ is not that point probability. Where the density is continuous, a short interval of width $h$ near $x$ has probability approximately $f_X(x)h$. Density therefore has units reciprocal to the units of $x$.

A uniform variable on $[a,b]$, with $a\lt b$, has density $1/(b-a)$ inside the interval and zero outside. Equal-length subintervals have equal probabilities. For $[2,2.25]$, the density is four; a subinterval of length $0.05$ has probability $4(0.05)=0.2$. Densities may exceed one. Their integrals, not their heights, are probabilities. Sections 6.2.2–6.2.3, printed pages 181–183 distinguish mass, density, and cumulative probability.

An exponential variable of rate $r\gt0$ has density $re^{-rt}$ for $t\ge0$ and zero otherwise. Integration gives survival probability $P(T>t)=e^{-rt}$. It is a constant-hazard waiting-time model, with $r$ measured in inverse time. It does not model every product lifetime: aging, wear, and changing operating conditions can violate its assumptions. Its memorylessness follows from $e^{-r(s+t)}/e^{-rs}=e^{-rt}$.

A normal, or Gaussian, variable with location $\mu\in\mathbb R$ and scale $\sigma\gt0$ has density

$$f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\qquad x\in\mathbb R.$$

The standardized coordinate $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$ is dimensionless. The factor $1/\sigma$ compensates the change of variable $dx=\sigma\,dz$, preserving total probability. The normalization $\sqrt{2\pi}$ follows by squaring $I=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-z^2/2}\,dz$ and using polar coordinates: $I^2=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi$. The bell shape is a model property, not a reason to impose normality on every set of scores.

13.7 The cumulative distribution describes every real law Lecture 37 · 27:17

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, is $F_X(t)=P(X\le t)$. It exists for every real random variable, whether its law is discrete, has a density, or is mixed. It is not merely a fallback when an exact distribution is unknown; a known CDF specifies that distribution.

Event inclusion proves that $F_X$ is nondecreasing. Continuity of probability for decreasing events proves right continuity: if $t_j\downarrow t$, then $\{X\le t_j\}\downarrow\{X\le t\}$, so $F_X(t_j)\to F_X(t)$. As $t\to-\infty$, the probabilities tend to zero; as $t\to+\infty$, they tend to one. Infinity here denotes a limit, not an ordinary real argument.

Subtracting nested events gives $P(a\lt X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a)$. The left limit $F_X(x^-)=P(X\lt x)$ yields atom mass $P(X=x)=F_X(x)-F_X(x^-)$. For an absolutely continuous law, $F_X(t)=\int_{-\infty}^tf_X(x)\,dx$, and its derivative equals the density almost everywhere. Continuity of a CDF alone does not guarantee that it has an ordinary density.

A continuous CDF can have flat intervals, for example outside a bounded support or inside a gap in the density. It need not increase strictly everywhere. A mixed law can combine a point mass at zero with continuous positive waiting times. Its CDF has both a jump and a continuous part; neither a PMF alone nor a density alone captures the whole law.

13.8 Compare model mechanisms in code

The standard-library code computes probabilities for the constructed service and verifies normalization for the finite binomial law. The Poisson normalization check truncates an infinite series, so it uses a stated numerical tolerance. There are no file or network effects, and no fitted parameters or random estimates.

from math import comb, exp, factorial, fsum, isclose

n, p = 4, 0.2
binomial = [comb(n, k) * p**k * (1-p)**(n-k)
            for k in range(n+1)]
assert isclose(fsum(binomial), 1.0)
geometric_at_three = (1-p)**2 * p
poisson = [exp(-2.0) * 2.0**k / factorial(k) for k in range(30)]
assert abs(fsum(poisson) - 1.0) < 1e-12
without_replacement = comb(2, 2) * comb(3, 0) / comb(5, 2)
assert isclose(without_replacement, 0.1)
assert isclose((1 / 0.25) * 0.05, 0.2)
print("two successes in four:", f"{binomial[2]:.6f}")
print("first success at three:", f"{geometric_at_three:.6f}")
print("zero arrivals at parameter two:", f"{poisson[0]:.6f}")
print("two marked objects without replacement:", without_replacement)

two successes in four: 0.153600
first success at three: 0.128000
zero arrivals at parameter two: 0.135335
two marked objects without replacement: 0.1

13.9 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Equal marginals are insufficient

Let $B$ be Bernoulli with parameter $1/2$ and set all four trial indicators equal to $B$. Find their count law and compare it with a binomial count.

Solution

Every marginal is Bernoulli with parameter $1/2$, but the count is $4B$, so masses are $1/2$ at zero and $1/2$ at four. A binomial $(4,1/2)$ law has masses $(1,4,6,4,1)/16$. Independence fails in the synchronized construction. Equal marginal success probabilities cannot justify multiplying probabilities of specified sequences.

Problem 2 · Derive geometric memorylessness

For the trials-including-success geometric variable, prove $P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=P(T>t)$ for nonnegative integers $s,t$.

Solution

The event in the numerator is already contained in $\{T>s\}$, so the conditional probability is $(1-p)^{s+t}/(1-p)^s=(1-p)^t$ when $0\lt p\lt1$. This equals the original survival probability. For $p=1$, conditioning on any positive number of failures has probability-zero conditioning and is undefined. The identity applies where the denominator is positive; it is not an argument that a real retry probability cannot change with history.

Problem 3 · Separate a count parameter from a rate

A homogeneous Poisson arrival model has rate three per hour. Write the count law for a twenty-minute interval and the chance of no arrival. What changes if the observation interval doubles?

Solution

Twenty minutes is one third of an hour, so the Poisson count parameter is $3(1/3)=1$. The mass is $e^{-1}/k!$, and zero-arrival probability is $e^{-1}$. Doubling exposure gives parameter two, mass $e^{-2}2^k/k!$, and no-arrival probability $e^{-2}$. Treating the numerical rate three as the parameter for every interval would ignore exposure units.

Problem 4 · A mixed CDF

With probability $1/4$, set $X=0$; otherwise draw $X$ uniformly on $[0,1]$. Derive its CDF and find $P(X=0)$ and $P(0\lt X\le1/2)$.

Solution

For $x\lt0$, $F(x)=0$. For $0\le x\le1$, $F(x)=1/4+(3/4)x$. For $x\gt1$, it is one. The jump at zero is $1/4$, which is the atom mass. The interval probability is $F(1/2)-F(0)=3/8$. A density $3/4$ on $(0,1)$ accounts for only three quarters of total mass; it must be supplemented by the atom.

Problem 5 · A unit change alters density height

Let $T$ be uniform over a quarter-hour interval $[0,1/4]$, measured in hours. Set $M=60T$, measured in minutes. Derive both densities and verify that the probability of waiting at most three minutes agrees.

Solution

The hour density is four on $[0,1/4]$. The minute variable is uniform on $[0,15]$, with density $1/15$. Its height differs because the horizontal unit changed by sixty. The event is $T\le3/60=1/20$ or $M\le3$. The two integrals are $4(1/20)=1/5$ and $3/15=1/5$. Density values are coordinate-dependent; event probabilities are invariant under a consistent change of units.

Problem 6 · Recover discrete mass from a CDF

A CDF is zero below zero, $0.2$ on $[0,1)$, $0.7$ on $[1,3)$, and one on $[3,\infty)$. Find every point mass and $P(0\lt X\le3)$.

Solution

The jumps are $0.2$ at zero, $0.7-0.2=0.5$ at one, and $1-0.7=0.3$ at three. There is no mass in the flat intervals. Their sum is one. The requested event excludes zero but includes three, so it has probability $F(3)-F(0)=1-0.2=0.8$. Replacing the half-open interval by one that includes zero changes its probability, which is why CDF endpoint conventions matter.
