22. Optimization, constraints, and Lagrange multipliers

A model may minimize its loss only by violating an allowed parameter budget. Adding a constraint changes which perturbations are available, so a nonzero ordinary gradient can coexist with an optimum. Before choosing an algorithm, we must determine whether an optimum exists, whether it is local or global, and which first-order equations are justified.

Lecture spine: Lectures 56–58. The examples, proofs, code, and exercises are original constructions. Caption expressions are interpreted under explicit mathematical assumptions; a stationary multiplier solution is not automatically a maximum or minimum.

22.1 State the objective, variables, and feasible set L56 · 02:27

An optimization problem specifies a variable $x\in\mathbb R^d$, a real objective $f(x)$, and a feasible set $\mathcal C$ of permitted inputs. Minimization seeks an element of $\operatorname*{argmin}_{x\in\mathcal C}f(x)$, the set of inputs attaining the lowest objective. The minimum value and the minimizing input are different objects. Replacing $f$ by $-f$ changes maximization into minimization without changing the feasible set.

A global minimizer $x_*$ satisfies $f(x_*)\le f(x)$ for every feasible $x$. A local minimizer satisfies that inequality only for feasible points within some positive-radius neighborhood. A strict local minimum requires strict inequality for every other sufficiently nearby feasible point. These definitions use the feasible set; a boundary point must not be tested against unavailable perturbations.

A problem may be infeasible, unbounded, or have a finite infimum that is not attained. The function $e^x$ on $\mathbb R$ has infimum zero but is positive at every finite input. No equation solver can produce a minimizer that does not exist. A continuous function on a nonempty compact set does attain its extremes; unbounded domains need other existence arguments. These distinctions follow the terminology in Boyd and Vandenberghe, §4.1, printed pages 127–130.

For our running example, choose $f(u,v)=u^2+4v^2$. Without constraints the minimum is zero at the origin. Impose $u+v=3$: the origin is now unavailable. The two squared coefficients penalize the coordinates differently, so the best feasible split need not be equal.

22.2 Interior optima require stationarity L56 · 12:40

Suppose a differentiable function has an unconstrained local minimum at an interior point $x_*$. For any direction $s$, the scalar function $t\mapsto f(x_*+ts)$ has a local minimum at zero. Its derivative must vanish: a positive derivative would allow improvement with negative $t$, and a negative derivative would allow improvement with positive $t$. Hence $\nabla f(x_*)^\top s=0$ for every $s$, so $\nabla f(x_*)=0$.

This is necessary, not sufficient. The scalar functions $x^2$, $-x^2$, and $x^3$ all have zero derivative at zero; only the first has a minimum there. The second has a maximum, and the third has neither. A stationary point is where the derivative vanishes. The term critical point is sometimes broader and includes derivative failures, so we will state the derivative condition explicitly.

In several variables, $f(u,v)=u^2-v^2$ has zero gradient at the origin. Its restriction to one axis increases and its restriction to the other decreases. Such a point is a saddle point: arbitrarily nearby values occur on both sides of its value. Solving the stationary equations finds a candidate, not a completed optimization result.

22.3 Second-order tests distinguish some candidates L57 · 15:56

For a twice continuously differentiable function at a stationary point, Taylor expansion gives $f(x_*+h)-f(x_*)=h^\top Hh/2+o(\|h\|^2)$, where $H=\nabla^2f(x_*)$ is symmetric. If its smallest eigenvalue is positive, the quadratic term bounds the increase below by a positive multiple of $\|h\|^2$. The remainder is smaller in a sufficiently small neighborhood, proving a strict local minimum. A negative-definite Hessian proves a strict local maximum. Eigenvalues of both signs prove a saddle by choosing their eigenvector directions.

In two variables write $H=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $\Delta=ac-b^2$. If $a\gt0$ and $\Delta\gt0$, completing the square gives $h^\top Hh=a(h_1+bh_2/a)^2+(\Delta/a)h_2^2$, so the matrix is positive definite. If $a\lt0$ and $\Delta\gt0$, it is negative definite. If $\Delta\lt0$, the eigenvalues have opposite signs. A zero determinant is inconclusive.

For example, $u^4+v^4$ has a strict minimum at zero with zero Hessian, while $u^4-v^4$ has a saddle with the same Hessian there. Positive semidefiniteness is necessary at a smooth local minimum but is not generally sufficient. Also, determinant sign alone is not a general high-dimensional curvature test: a diagonal matrix with entries $1,-1,-1$ has positive determinant and remains indefinite.

The running objective has Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(2,8)$, so it is strictly convex. For a differentiable convex function on a convex domain, the supporting-plane inequality $f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top(y-x)$ makes a zero gradient sufficient for global optimality. Convexity gives this additional conclusion; local curvature at one point does not imply it for an arbitrary nonlinear function.

22.4 Eliminating a constraint changes the derivative being tested L56 · 30:51

The constraint $u+v=3$ lets us write $v=3-u$. Substitution gives $\widetilde f(u)=u^2+4(3-u)^2=5u^2-24u+36$. Its derivative is $10u-24$, so its minimum is at $u=12/5$ and $v=3/5$. Completing the square gives $\widetilde f(u)=5(u-12/5)^2+36/5$, proving a unique global constrained minimum of $36/5=7.2$.

At this point the ordinary gradient is $(24/5,24/5)^\top$, which is nonzero. Every feasible perturbation has the form $(t,-t)$, and its inner product with that gradient is zero. The constrained first-order test uses feasible tangent directions, not every vector in the ambient space. Direct expansion shows the finite feasible loss change is $5t^2$, so this candidate really is a minimum.

Elimination is effective for simple constraints, but solving one variable in terms of all the others can be inconvenient or only locally possible. Lagrange multipliers express the same tangent-space condition without choosing that variable in advance.

22.5 A multiplier expresses normality at a regular constraint L58 · 06:17

Suppose $f,g$ have continuous first partial derivatives near a feasible point $x_*$ with $g(x_*)=0$, and $\nabla g(x_*)\ne0$. The latter is a regularity condition: the constraint has a well-defined tangent hyperplane there. For a local constrained extremum, the Lagrange-multiplier theorem supplies a scalar $\lambda$ satisfying

$$\nabla f(x_*)+\lambda\nabla g(x_*)=0,\qquad g(x_*)=0.$$

The geometric reason is that every tangent direction $s$ satisfies $\nabla g^\top s=0$, and constrained stationarity requires $\nabla f^\top s=0$ as well. Thus $\nabla f$ lies in the one-dimensional normal span of $\nabla g$. For an affine constraint, every tangent direction generates an exactly feasible line, so this argument is immediate. For smooth nonlinear regular constraints, the implicit-function theorem supplies feasible curves realizing those tangent directions. Without regularity, that conclusion need not hold.

We use the Lagrangian $\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda g(x)$. Other sign conventions replace $\lambda$ by its negative. Stationarity in $x$ gives the normality equation; stationarity in $\lambda$ reproduces feasibility. This is not, in general, unconstrained minimization jointly over $x$ and $\lambda$. At an infeasible $x$, varying $\lambda$ sends the term $\lambda g(x)$ arbitrarily far in either direction. The multiplier is enforcing a condition, not supplying an ordinary extra minimization variable. The distinction is formalized in Boyd and Vandenberghe, §5.5.3, printed pages 243–244.

For the running example, $g(u,v)=u+v-3$ and the equations are $2u+\lambda=0$, $8v+\lambda=0$, and $u+v=3$. Thus $u=4v$, yielding the same minimizer $(12/5,3/5)$ and multiplier $\lambda=-24/5$. Feasibility and stationarity identify a candidate; the completed-square argument already proves its global minimum.

22.6 A stationary multiplier solution still needs classification L58 · 25:25

Consider $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2$ on the plane $x+y+z=1$. Completing the sum of squares gives

$$x^2+y^2+z^2=\frac13+ (x-\tfrac13)^2+(y-\tfrac13)^2+(z-\tfrac13)^2.$$

The identity uses the constraint to cancel the linear centered terms. It proves that the equal-coordinate point is the unique minimum, with value $1/3$. It is not a maximum. The feasible family $(t,-t,1)$ has squared norm $2t^2+1$, which grows without bound. The same multiplier equations must not be interpreted as whichever extremum one happened to request.

A compact constraint supplies a different situation. On the unit circle $u^2+v^2=1$, the objective $u^2+4v^2$ lies between one and four, because it equals $1+3v^2$. Its minima are $(\pm1,0)$ and maxima are $(0,\pm1)$. All four satisfy multiplier stationarity. Comparing their values, or using the displayed identity, finishes the classification.

Regularity can also fail. Minimize $f(x)=x$ subject to $g(x)=x^2=0$. The only feasible point is zero, so it is a constrained minimum. But $f'(0)=1$ and $g'(0)=0$, and no multiplier solves $1+\lambda0=0$. The theorem's nonzero-gradient condition is substantive.

22.7 Several constraints produce a span of normals L58 · 29:55

For equalities $g_1(x)=\cdots=g_p(x)=0$ with linearly independent constraint gradients, stationarity becomes $\nabla f(x)+\sum_j\lambda_j\nabla g_j(x)=0$. The objective gradient lies in the span of the normals. With affine constraints $Cx=b$, the tangent space is $\ker C$, and its orthogonal complement is the row space of $C$.

For a quadratic $f(x)=x^\top Hx/2+q^\top x$ with symmetric $H$, the equations form a block linear system:

$$\begin{bmatrix}H&C^\top\\C&0\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\\lambda\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}-q\\b\end{bmatrix}.$$

If $C$ has full row rank and $H$ is positive definite on $\ker C$, the feasible quadratic has a unique minimizer. To verify this, let $x_*$ solve the block equations and write another feasible point as $x_*+h$, where $Ch=0$. Its objective difference is $(Hx_*+q)^\top h+h^\top Hh/2$. The linear term is $-\lambda^\top Ch=0$ and the remaining term is positive for nonzero feasible $h$. This is a sufficient global certificate for the quadratic.

Inequality constraints require additional signs and complementary-slackness conditions. For minimization with constraints $g_j(x)\le0$, their multipliers are nonnegative, and a multiplier can be nonzero only on an active boundary. These are parts of the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions. The convex sufficiency statement requires convex objectives and inequalities, affine equalities, and the stated feasibility and stationarity conditions; general nonconvex problems need further analysis.

22.8 Execute the stationarity and feasibility checks

The following NumPy construction solves the running quadratic's block system and independently checks both equation groups. It also evaluates a feasible sequence for the unbounded maximization example. The program has no file or network effects and does not claim to be a general constrained optimizer.

import numpy as np

H = np.diag([2.0, 8.0])
C = np.array([[1.0, 1.0]])
K = np.block([[H, C.T], [C, np.zeros((1, 1))]])
solution = np.linalg.solve(K, [0.0, 0.0, 3.0])
x, multiplier = solution[:2], solution[2]
assert np.allclose(x, [2.4, 0.6])
assert np.allclose(H @ x + C.T[:, 0]*multiplier, 0)
assert np.allclose(C @ x, 3)
print("constrained minimizer:", x)
print(f"objective={x[0]**2 + 4*x[1]**2:.6f} lambda={multiplier:.6f}")
for t in [0.0, 1.0, 10.0]:
    z = np.array([t, -t, 1.0])
    assert np.isclose(z.sum(), 1)
    print(f"feasible t={t:.0f} squared_norm={z @ z:.0f}")

22.9 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · A local optimum without a global optimum

Give a smooth real function with a local minimum but no global minimum, and justify both assertions.

Solution

Take $f(x)=x^3-3x$. Its derivative is $3x^2-3$, and at $x=1$ it vanishes while the second derivative is $6\gt0$, giving a strict local minimum. As $x\to-\infty$, the cubic term drives the function to $-\infty$. Therefore it is unbounded below and has no global minimizer. A local second-derivative test cannot settle the global problem.

Problem 2 · A changing budget

Minimize $u^2+4v^2$ subject to $u+v=b$ for real $b$. Compute the optimal value and relate its derivative to the multiplier.

Solution

Stationarity gives $u=4v$, so $v=b/5$ and $u=4b/5$. The value is $16b^2/25+4b^2/25=4b^2/5$. Its derivative is $8b/5$. With Lagrangian $f+\lambda(u+v-b)$, the multiplier is $\lambda=-2u=-8b/5$. Thus the value derivative is $-\lambda$. Changing the sign convention changes the multiplier's sign, not the sensitivity. Here the formula is exact for all $b$.

Problem 3 · Tangent-space curvature

The Hessian of $f(u,v)=u^2-v^2$ is indefinite. Is the function minimized at the origin under the equality $v=0$?

Solution

The feasible set is the $u$ axis. On it the objective is $u^2$, so zero is its unique global constrained minimum. The only feasible directions have second component zero, and the Hessian quadratic form on those directions is $2h_1^2\gt0$ for nonzero $h$. Full-space indefinite curvature does not prevent positive curvature on the feasible tangent space. The constraint removes the decreasing direction.

Problem 4 · A failed regularity condition

Explain why rewriting the constraint $x=0$ as $x^2=0$ preserves the feasible set but can change the validity of ordinary multiplier equations.

Solution

The feasible sets are identical, but the first representation has gradient one at zero while the second has gradient zero. For objective $f(x)=x$, the first representation permits multiplier $-1$ in $f'+\lambda g'=0$. The second gives $1=0$ for every multiplier. Constraint representations can therefore change regularity even when they describe the same feasible points. A constraint qualification belongs to the mathematical formulation, not only to the set.

Problem 5 · Multiple affine constraints

Minimize $x^2+y^2+z^2$ subject to $x+y=1$ and $y+z=0$ without forming an inverse matrix.

Solution

Eliminate $x=1-y$ and $z=-y$. The objective becomes $(1-y)^2+2y^2=3(y-1/3)^2+2/3$. Thus the unique minimizer is $(2/3,1/3,-1/3)$ and the minimum is $2/3$. The completed square is a global certificate along the entire feasible line. A block multiplier solve would yield the same result, but the reduced one-dimensional expression is enough to expose the mechanism.

Problem 6 · Audit an optimizer's claim

A numerical solver reports small stationarity residual and small equality residual. What additional checks are needed before calling its output a global minimizer?

Solution

First verify that its equations correspond to the intended objective and feasible set, with the correct sign and derivative convention. Then determine whether a global sufficiency condition applies, such as convexity or a positive quadratic form on all feasible directions. For a nonconvex problem, stationarity can identify maxima or saddles; local second-order checks do not usually prove global optimality. Check regularity, compare objective values of other candidates, and account for boundaries and unbounded directions. Finally, numerical residual tolerances must be interpreted relative to scaling and conditioning. They certify approximate equations, not a missing existence or sufficiency theorem.

They do not turn all constrained problems into unconstrained minimization, and they do not certify a global optimum for every nonlinear model.
