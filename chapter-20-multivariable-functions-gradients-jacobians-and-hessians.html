20. Multivariable functions, gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians

Two parameters of a model can compensate for one another. Changing either alone may increase a loss even though changing both together decreases it. To choose an update, we need to describe changes in arbitrary directions, not just along coordinate axes. The same local description must also survive composition through several stages of a model.

Lecture spine: Lectures 52–54. This edition uses column gradients for scalar functions and Jacobians with outputs as rows. Thus the scalar Jacobian is the transpose of the gradient. Row-gradient conventions are equivalent when transposed consistently. Examples, code, and exercises are original constructions.

20.1 Graphs and level sets describe different geometries L52 · 04:16

A scalar function $f:D\subseteq\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R$ takes a vector of $d$ real coordinates and returns one number. For two inputs, its graph consists of triples $(u,v,f(u,v))$ in three-dimensional space. A level set at value $c$ is instead the set of inputs satisfying $f(u,v)=c$. It lies in the input plane. A contour plot represents several such level sets together; it is not the graph itself.

For $f(u,v)=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}$, the graph is the upper circular cone. For $c\gt0$, its level set is the circle $u^2+v^2=c^2$. At $c=0$ it is one point, and for negative $c$ it is empty. A level set need not be a regular curve. For $g(u,v)=u+2v$, the graph is a plane while the level sets are parallel lines $u+2v=c$.

Our running loss is $L(u,v)=(u+v-1)^2+4(u-v)^2$. The first term asks the two parameters to sum to one; the second penalizes their disagreement. Its graph is a quadratic bowl with unequal curvature in two directions. The loss is zero only at $u=v=1/2$. Defining $a=(u+v-1)/\sqrt2$ and $b=(u-v)/\sqrt2$ gives $L=2a^2+8b^2$. Hence its positive level sets are ellipses centered at $(1/2,1/2)$, with faster growth along the disagreement direction.

20.2 Partial derivatives examine coordinate slices L52 · 22:34

The partial derivative with respect to coordinate $j$ is

$$\partial_jf(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+he_j)-f(x)}{h},$$

where $e_j$ has one in coordinate $j$ and zero elsewhere. Other coordinates are held fixed. This is an ordinary one-variable derivative along one slice of the domain. For the loss, differentiation gives $\partial_uL=2(u+v-1)+8(u-v)=10u-6v-2$ and $\partial_vL=2(u+v-1)-8(u-v)=-6u+10v-2$.

Coordinate slices do not determine all limiting behavior. Define $q(0,0)=0$ and $q(u,v)=u^3/(u^2+v^2)$ elsewhere. Along the $u$ axis its derivative at the origin is one; along the $v$ axis it is zero. Both partial derivatives exist. Along the line $(u,v)=t(a,b)$, however, the difference quotient is $a^3/(a^2+b^2)$ for nonzero $(a,b)$. These directional rates do not form a linear function of $(a,b)$. For $(1,1)$ the rate is $1/2$, whereas the partial derivatives would predict one.

The missing condition is a single linear approximation that works for perturbations approaching from all directions. Merely checking the existence of partial derivatives is insufficient. Continuous partial derivatives in a neighborhood are a useful sufficient condition for differentiability, but continuity of the original function alone is weaker.

20.3 The gradient represents one linear differential L53 · 07:03

A scalar function is differentiable at $x$ if there is a linear map of perturbations with

$$f(x+h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top h+r(h),\qquad \frac{r(h)}{\|h\|_2}\to0.$$

Here the gradient $\nabla f(x)\in\mathbb R^d$ is a column vector whose entries are the partial derivatives. The superscript $\top$ transposes it into a row so the displayed product is scalar. The derivative is the linear map $h\mapsto\nabla f(x)^\top h$; the gradient is the vector representing that map in Euclidean coordinates.

For the loss, the gradient is $(10u-6v-2,-6u+10v-2)^\top$. At $(1,-1/2)$ it is $(11,-13)^\top$. A small positive change in $u$ alone increases the loss, while the same change in $v$ alone decreases it. These signs refer to the specified point. They can change at another parameter value.

For a unit vector $s$, the directional derivative is $D_sf(x)=\lim_{t\to0}[f(x+ts)-f(x)]/t$. Substituting the differentiability expansion proves $D_sf(x)=\nabla f(x)^\top s$. Normalization gives rate per unit Euclidean distance. Without normalization, scaling the direction also scales the rate. The derivative conventions and their alternative layouts are described in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §§5.2–5.3, printed pages 146–150.

20.4 Steepest change and level-set tangents L53 · 34:58

Cauchy–Schwarz gives $-\|\nabla f\|_2\le\nabla f^\top s\le\|\nabla f\|_2$ for unit $s$. At a nonzero gradient, the upper endpoint occurs for $s=\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|_2$ and the lower endpoint for its negative. Thus the gradient points toward steepest first-order ascent in Euclidean geometry. Its negative points toward steepest first-order descent. At a zero gradient every first-order directional rate vanishes, so no unique first-order steepest direction is defined.

Suppose a differentiable path $x(t)$ stays on a level set, so $f(x(t))=c$. Differentiating gives $\nabla f(x(t))^\top x'(t)=0$. The gradient is perpendicular to the tangent wherever it is nonzero. The directional derivative along the tangent is zero; the gradient itself need not be zero. For $f(u,v)=u+2v$, its constant gradient $(1,2)^\top$ is perpendicular to each level line despite never vanishing.

The word steepest also depends on how step length is measured. If permitted unit steps satisfy $s^\top Ms=1$ for a positive-definite matrix $M$, the optimizing direction is proportional to $M^{-1}\nabla f$, not generally to $\nabla f$. To see this, set $z=M^{1/2}s$ and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to $(M^{-1/2}\nabla f)^\top z$. Feature scaling changes geometry, so raw Euclidean steepest descent is not coordinate-invariant.

20.5 A chain rule composes local maps L54 · 05:01

Let $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ be differentiable scalar paths. Assume that $f$ is differentiable at their current point $(u(t),v(t))$, in the sense of the single linear approximation defined above. Then the composite derivative is

$$\frac{d}{dt}f(u(t),v(t))= \partial_uf\,u'(t)+\partial_vf\,v'(t).$$

Both partial derivatives are evaluated at the current point $(u(t),v(t))$. The formula sums contributions from every route by which the input affects the output. It is not the product of all partial derivatives.

For an original check, take $f(u,v)=u^2v$ and the path $u=t$, $v=1+t^2$. Direct substitution gives $f=t^2+t^4$ and derivative $2t+4t^3$. The chain rule gives $(2uv)(1)+(u^2)(2t)$, which becomes the same expression. If the second route through $v$ were omitted, the cubic contribution would be too small. This mechanism reappears when a parameter influences several branches of a neural network.

20.6 A Jacobian has one row per output L54 · 14:53

For a differentiable vector function $F:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^m$, define its Jacobian $J_F(x)\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$ by $(J_F)_{ij}=\partial F_i/\partial x_j$. Its local action is $F(x+h)=F(x)+J_F(x)h+o(\|h\|)$. The $i$th row is the transpose of the column gradient of output $F_i$. A map between Euclidean spaces need not be linear; its derivative at a point is a linear map.

For $F(u,v)=(uv,u+v^2)^\top$,

$$J_F(u,v)=\begin{bmatrix}v&u\\1&2v\end{bmatrix}.$$

For the scalar function $g(a,b)=a+3b$, the composite is $g(F(u,v))=uv+3u+3v^2$. Multiplication gives $J_{g\circ F}=[1\;3]J_F=[v+3\;u+6v]$, agreeing with direct differentiation. More generally $J_{G\circ F}(x)=J_G(F(x))J_F(x)$. The shapes are $p\times m$ times $m\times d$, yielding $p\times d$. Matrix order follows the direction in which perturbations travel.

For scalar $g$, the same identity in column-gradient form is $\nabla(g\circ F)=J_F^\top\nabla g$. A denominator-layout derivative table transposes these Jacobians. Either convention is valid, but mixing formulas from different conventions without transposing produces shape or meaning errors. In particular, a square Jacobian can hide such an error because both orders remain dimensionally possible.

20.7 The Hessian describes directional curvature L54 · 31:31

For twice continuously differentiable scalar $f$, its Hessian $H_f$ has entries $(H_f)_{ij}=\partial_j\partial_if$. Its mixed derivatives are equal, so it is symmetric. The continuity condition concerns the second partial derivatives in a neighborhood; continuity of $f$ alone does not establish the equality.

One way to see the equality is to take the rectangular increment $f(x+s,y+t)-f(x+s,y)-f(x,y+t)+f(x,y)$. Integrating first in one coordinate and then the other expresses it both as an integral of $f_{xy}$ and as an integral of $f_{yx}$. Dividing by $st$ and shrinking the rectangle, continuity makes the limits equal. The hypothesis and resulting symmetry agree with §5.7, printed page 165.

The second-order Taylor model is $f(x+h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top h+h^\top H_f(x)h/2+o(\|h\|^2)$. This follows by applying the one-variable Taylor formula to $t\mapsto f(x+th)$: its first derivative at zero is $\nabla f^\top h$, and its second is $h^\top H_fh$. Thus the Hessian's quadratic form gives curvature along a chosen direction.

The running loss has constant Hessian $H=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}10&-6\\-6&10\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Its unit eigenvectors in the shared and disagreement directions have eigenvalues $4$ and $16$. Those positive curvatures prove a unique global minimum for this quadratic. Solving $H(u,v)^\top=(2,2)^\top$ gives $(1/2,1/2)$. A stationary point with one negative Hessian eigenvalue has a direction of local decrease and cannot be a local minimum.

Positive semidefinite curvature at one stationary point remains inconclusive. The functions $u^4+v^4$ and $u^4-v^4$ have zero Hessians at the origin; one has a strict minimum and the other a saddle. A local minimum of a twice differentiable function has a positive-semidefinite Hessian, but this necessary condition is not generally sufficient. Positive definiteness, with the stated smoothness, is sufficient for a strict local minimum.

20.8 Check a derivative along a direction

The following NumPy program implements only the constructed quadratic. It performs no file or network operations. It compares the analytic directional derivative against an independent central difference, checks the Hessian eigenvalues, and solves the stationary equations. The fixed direction has unit length, so its rate is per unit parameter distance.

import numpy as np


def loss(x):
    u, v = x
    return (u + v - 1)**2 + 4*(u - v)**2


x = np.array([1.0, -0.5])
grad = np.array([10*x[0] - 6*x[1] - 2,
                 -6*x[0] + 10*x[1] - 2])
H = np.array([[10.0, -6.0], [-6.0, 10.0]])
u = np.array([3.0, 4.0]) / 5
h = 1e-5
finite = (loss(x + h*u) - loss(x - h*u)) / (2*h)
assert np.isclose(finite, grad @ u)
assert np.allclose(np.linalg.eigvalsh(H), [4, 16])
x_star = np.linalg.solve(H, [2.0, 2.0])
assert np.allclose(x_star, [0.5, 0.5])
print("gradient:", grad)
print(f"directional analytic={grad @ u:.6f} finite={finite:.6f}")
print("curvatures:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(H))
print("minimizer:", x_star, f"loss={loss(x_star):.6f}")

gradient: [ 11. -13.]
directional analytic=-3.800000 finite=-3.800000
curvatures: [ 4. 16.]
minimizer: [0.5 0.5] loss=0.000000

A finite-difference match is evidence for this implementation at these inputs, not a proof for every input or for nonsmooth points. Independent directions and step-size sweeps make such checks more informative.

20.9 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · A cone's missing gradient

For $f(u,v)=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}$, find its gradient away from zero and decide whether it is differentiable at the origin.

Solution

Away from zero, the gradient is $(u,v)^\top/\sqrt{u^2+v^2}$ by the chain rule. At zero, the restriction to the $u$ axis is $|u|$, whose two-sided derivative does not exist. Hence the full function cannot be differentiable there. Its zero level set is a single point, so the regular-curve normal argument also does not apply at the vertex.

Problem 2 · All directional derivatives can still be insufficient

Verify the directional derivatives of $q(u,v)=u^3/(u^2+v^2)$, extended by zero, and explain the failure of differentiability.

Solution

For nonzero direction $(a,b)$, $q(ta,tb)=t a^3/(a^2+b^2)$, so every two-sided directional derivative exists and equals $a^3/(a^2+b^2)$. The coordinate derivatives imply the candidate linear differential $(a,b)\mapsto a$. At $(1,1)$ this predicts one, while the true directional derivative is one half. A differentiable function must have a linear directional-rate map. Existence in each fixed direction alone does not meet that requirement.

Problem 3 · A nonzero gradient along a contour

For $f(u,v)=u+2v$, parametrize a level line and compute the rate along its tangent.

Solution

At level $c$, choose $x(t)=(c-2t,t)$. Its tangent is $(-2,1)$, while the gradient is $(1,2)$. Their dot product is $-2+2=0$, so $f(x(t))$ has zero derivative. The gradient remains nonzero. The vanishing object is the directional change along the level line, not the entire gradient.

Problem 4 · A rectangular Jacobian

Let $F(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(x_1x_3,x_2+x_3^2)$ and $g(a,b)=a^2+b$. Derive the gradient of $g\circ F$.

Solution

The Jacobian of $F$ is $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x_3&0&x_1\\0&1&2x_3\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. The outer gradient is $(2a,1)^\top$. Multiplication by $J_F^\top$ and substitution of $a=x_1x_3$ gives $(2x_1x_3^2,1,2x_1^2x_3+2x_3)^\top$. Direct expansion of $g\circ F=x_1^2x_3^2+x_2+x_3^2$ gives the same result. The rectangular shape makes the required transpose visible.

Problem 5 · Degenerate curvature

Compare the origin for $f(u,v)=u^4+v^4$ and $g(u,v)=u^4-v^4$. What does the Hessian test establish?

Solution

Both gradients and Hessians vanish at zero. For $f$, every nonzero nearby input has positive value, so zero is a strict local and global minimum. For $g$, moving along the $u$ axis gives positive values and moving along the $v$ axis gives negative values, so zero is a saddle. The zero Hessian is positive semidefinite in both cases and does not settle the question. Higher-order terms distinguish them.

Problem 6 · Compare a family of directions

At $(1,-1/2)$ in the running loss, compare the directional rates along the positive coordinate axes, $(3,4)/5$, and the steepest-descent unit direction.

Solution

The gradient is $(11,-13)$. Coordinate rates are $11$ and $-13$. Along $(3,4)/5$, the rate is $(33-52)/5=-19/5=-3.8$, agreeing with the program. The gradient has Euclidean norm $\sqrt{290}$. The steepest-descent unit direction is $(-11,13)/\sqrt{290}$ and gives rate $-\sqrt{290}$. This is a first-order comparison at one point. A finite step still requires curvature control; it does not inherit the directional derivative's ordering for arbitrary step lengths.
