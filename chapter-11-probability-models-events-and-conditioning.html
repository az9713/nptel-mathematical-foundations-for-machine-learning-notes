11. Probability models, events, and conditioning

Two document checkers each report “pass” or “fail.” Before deciding whether to trust a document, an assistant needs the probability that both checks pass and how one result changes the chance of the other. Knowing the possible reports is insufficient. The same four possible outcomes can describe independent checks, perfectly synchronized checks, or checks that never pass together. A probability model must specify how uncertainty is allocated among those outcomes.

Recording spine: Lectures 29–31, in their original order. The document-checker model, tables, code, and exercises are original constructions. Probability statements below concern their stated models; they are not measurements of deployed checkers.

11.1 Specify the experiment before assigning numbers Lecture 29 · 09:53

A random experiment is a modeled procedure whose outcome is not known before it is observed. Its sample space $\Omega$ is the set of outcomes admitted by the model. An individual outcome is denoted $\omega\in\Omega$. For two ordered binary checker reports, take $\Omega=\{00,01,10,11\}$, with one denoting pass. The order matters: $01$ and $10$ are distinct reports.

A model can be useful without listing every microscopic physical possibility. Modeling a coin by heads and tails abstracts away landings on its edge; such exclusions must match the intended experiment closely enough for the decision. A sample space with one outcome is also mathematically legitimate: its uncertainty is degenerate. Randomness does not require all listed outcomes to be physically unpredictable or to have equal probabilities.

Sample spaces can be finite, countably infinite, or uncountable. The number of retries until success ranges over positive integers. A waiting time measured as an ideal real number can range over $[0,\infty)$. Rounding a waiting time to integer milliseconds produces another model with a countable outcome set. Choosing a representation and choosing the probability law on it are separate decisions.

An event is a measurable subset of the sample space. It occurs when the realized outcome belongs to that subset. Let $A=\{10,11\}$ mean that checker one passes and $B=\{01,11\}$ mean that checker two passes. Outcomes are elements; events are sets of outcomes. In a finite model it is customary to allow every subset as an event. With $m$ outcomes this gives $2^m$ subsets, because each outcome can be independently included or excluded when constructing a subset.

11.2 Event operations encode questions about the reports Lecture 30 · 00:59

The intersection $A\cap B$ means both checkers pass; the union $A\cup B$ means at least one passes. The complement $A^c=\Omega\setminus A$ means checker one fails. Here $A\cap B=\{11\}$ and $A\cup B=\{01,10,11\}$. “Or” in a union is inclusive: it includes the case in which both events occur.

Events are disjoint, or mutually exclusive, when their intersection is empty. A collection is collectively exhaustive when its union is $\Omega$. A partition is a collection of nonempty, pairwise disjoint events whose union is $\Omega$. The events $A$ and $A^c$ form a partition when both are nonempty. The events $A$ and $B$ generally do not: they overlap at $11$ and omit $00$.

For an infinite space, we must also specify a collection $\mathcal F$ of measurable events. It contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions; such a collection is a sigma algebra. Those closure rules ensure that ordinary questions such as “not $A$” and “at least one of $A_1,A_2,\ldots$” remain meaningful events. Chapter 12 develops this construction. Interest in a few events does not license assigning unrelated numbers to them: the full model must respect these set relations.

11.3 Probability is a consistent measure on events Lecture 30 · 22:19

A probability measure $P$ maps events in $\mathcal F$ to real numbers. It satisfies nonnegativity $P(E)\ge0$, normalization $P(\Omega)=1$, and countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint events $E_j$,

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}E_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}P(E_j).$$

The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$ is a probability space. For finite $\Omega$, assigning nonnegative atom weights $p_\omega$ summing to one determines $P(E)=\sum_{\omega\in E}p_\omega$. A finite uniform model has $p_\omega=1/|\Omega|$, so $P(E)=|E|/|\Omega|$. The favorable-outcomes rule follows from uniformity. It is invalid for a biased die merely because the die has six faces.

Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong distinguish the sample space, event space, and probability measure in Section 6.1.2, printed page 175.

A relative frequency is an observed ratio, such as 37 passes in 100 trials. A probability is a parameter of the model. Repeated frequencies can provide evidence for that parameter under an appropriate sampling model. A limit of arbitrary observed frequencies need not exist, so it cannot by itself define probability for every possible experiment. Later limit theorems state precise conditions under which repeated averages approach model probabilities.

11.4 Consequences of the axioms Lecture 30 · 33:21

The empty event has probability zero. Since $\Omega$ and $\varnothing$ are disjoint with union $\Omega$, additivity gives $1=1+P(\varnothing)$. The decomposition $\Omega=E\cup E^c$ then gives $P(E^c)=1-P(E)$. Nonnegativity of the complement proves $P(E)\le1$.

If $E\subseteq F$, decompose $F=E\cup(F\setminus E)$ into disjoint parts. Therefore $P(F)=P(E)+P(F\setminus E)\ge P(E)$. This is monotonicity. Applying the same decomposition to overlapping events yields the addition rule

$$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).$$

One way to see the correction is to split the union into $A\setminus B$, $A\cap B$, and $B\setminus A$. Adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ counts the middle piece twice. In our model the union probability is $0.4+0.7-0.28=0.82$, agreeing with direct addition of the three nonzero-report outcomes.

Zero probability is weaker than emptiness. In the ideal uniform model on $[0,1]$, the probability of an interval equals its length. Every singleton is a nonempty event of probability zero. An observed real-valued outcome lies in some singleton, but any particular singleton fixed in advance still has probability zero. There is no contradiction: the union of all such singletons is uncountable, while the additivity axiom applies to countable disjoint unions. A physical instrument with finite resolution requires its own discrete or interval-valued observation model.

11.5 Conditioning restricts and renormalizes Lecture 31 · 30:23

Suppose the second checker is known to have passed. Outcomes outside $B$ are excluded, and probabilities within $B$ must sum to one. For $P(B)\gt0$, define

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.$$

The vertical bar means “conditional on.” More generally, $P_B(E)=P(E\cap B)/P(B)$ is a probability measure on events: nonnegativity and additivity are inherited from $P$, and $P_B(\Omega)=1$. Conditioning changes the weights attached to questions; it does not change which event was originally called $A$.

For the table, $P(A\mid B)=0.28/0.7=0.4$. Let $C=\{00,11\}$ mean that the reports agree. Then $P(C)=0.46$, and $P(A\mid C)=0.28/0.46=14/23$, approximately $0.608696$. Learning that checker two passed gives no update about checker one in this model, but learning agreement does. The information being conditioned on matters.

Rearranging the definition gives the multiplication rule $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$. When both event probabilities are positive, one can also write $P(A\cap B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)$. The conditional probabilities generally differ because their denominators differ. The ratio formula does not define conditioning on a zero-probability event; later density-based conditioning needs additional structure.

Unconditional probabilities may be called prior probabilities when they describe information before an observation, and updated hypothesis probabilities may be called posterior probabilities. Neither is determined by the sample space and event definitions alone. A probability law and an information context are also required. Not every conditional quantity in an arbitrary calculation needs to be called a posterior.

11.6 Independence is an exact factorization Lecture 31 · 41:00

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. If $P(B)\gt0$, this is equivalent to $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. This definition also handles zero-probability events without division. It says that learning whether $B$ occurs does not alter the probability of $A$, with the complement case following by subtraction.

The checker table satisfies $0.28=0.4\cdot0.7$, so its pass events are independent. They are not mutually exclusive: both can pass. Conversely, disjoint events of positive probabilities cannot be independent, because their intersection probability is zero while the product is positive. Knowing that one occurred excludes the other entirely.

Failure to infer another outcome with certainty is not enough to establish independence. A signal might change a probability from $0.1$ to $0.8$ without making the outcome certain. Independence requires no probability change, not merely incomplete knowledge. Repeated coin tosses are independent only under the specified toss model; this is not a logical consequence of calling them separate trials.

For more than two events, pairwise independence is weaker than mutual independence. Take two independent fair bits $U,V$. The events $E=\{U=1\}$, $F=\{V=1\}$, and $G=\{U=V\}$ each have probability $1/2$, and each pair has intersection probability $1/4$. Yet $E\cap F\cap G=\{11\}$ has probability $1/4$, not $1/8$. Knowing two reports can reveal a third even when no pair alone reveals dependence.

11.7 Enumerate the finite model exactly

This standard-library program uses rational arithmetic, so the finite event probabilities are exact before decimal formatting. It has no file or network effects. It checks the addition rule, conditional probability, and the pairwise-versus-mutual counterexample. A changed or invalid probability table can break its assertions; enumeration does not estimate the behavior of real checkers.

from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product

omega = list(product([0, 1], repeat=2))
weights = dict(zip(omega, [F(18, 100), F(42, 100),
                           F(12, 100), F(28, 100)]))
A = {s for s in omega if s[0] == 1}
B = {s for s in omega if s[1] == 1}
C = {s for s in omega if s[0] == s[1]}
pA = sum(weights[s] for s in A)
pB = sum(weights[s] for s in B)
pAB = sum(weights[s] for s in A & B)
pC = sum(weights[s] for s in C)
assert pAB == pA * pB
assert sum(weights[s] for s in A | B) == pA + pB - pAB
assert pAB / pB == pA
assert sum(weights[s] for s in A & C) / pC == F(14, 23)
events = [A, B, C]
for i in range(3):
    for j in range(i):
        assert F(len(events[i] & events[j]), 4) == F(1, 4)
assert F(len(A & B & C), 4) != F(1, 8)
print("P(A), P(B), P(A and B):", pA, pB, pAB)
print("P(A given agreement):", F(14, 23))
print("fair-bit triple probability:", F(len(A & B & C), 4))

11.8 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Counting needs a model

A six-face die has probabilities proportional to the face number. Find the probability of an even result and compare it with counting three favorable faces out of six.

Solution

The sum of weights is $1+2+3+4+5+6=21$. The even-face weights sum to $2+4+6=12$, so the probability is $12/21=4/7$. Counting faces gives $1/2$, which assumes equal atom weights and is incorrect for this model. The sample space is identical in both models; the probability measure differs.

Problem 2 · Bound an unknown overlap

If $P(A)=0.7$ and $P(B)=0.6$, determine every possible value of $P(A\cap B)$.

Solution

Monotonicity bounds the intersection above by $0.6$. The union cannot exceed one, so the addition rule gives $0.7+0.6-P(A\cap B)\le1$, or overlap at least $0.3$. Every value $q\in[0.3,0.6]$ is realizable with atom masses $q$, $0.7-q$, $0.6-q$, and $q-0.3$ on the both, A-only, B-only, and neither cells. They are nonnegative and sum to one.

Problem 3 · Sampling without replacement

A bag contains two red and two blue tokens. Draw two ordered tokens uniformly without replacement. Find the chance that the second is red, and its chance conditional on a red first draw. Are the red events independent?

Solution

By symmetry each original token is equally likely to be second, so the unconditional probability is $2/4=1/2$. After a red first draw, one red and two blue tokens remain, giving conditional probability $1/3$. The joint red probability is $(1/2)(1/3)=1/6$, different from $(1/2)^2$. Distinct draws do not imply independence; the sampling mechanism determines it.

Problem 4 · Disjoint and independent

Prove that disjoint events can be independent only if at least one has probability zero. Does a zero-probability event have to be empty?

Solution

Disjointness gives $P(A\cap B)=0$. Independence then requires $P(A)P(B)=0$. Since both factors are nonnegative real numbers, at least one is zero. Conversely that zero factor makes the equality hold for disjoint events. A singleton in the uniform $[0,1]$ model is nonempty but has probability zero, so zero mass does not establish emptiness.

Problem 5 · A union bound

Prove $P(\bigcup_{j=1}^mE_j)\le\sum_{j=1}^mP(E_j)$ without assuming independence. When is the bound useful for several checker failure modes?

Solution

Define disjoint increments $D_1=E_1$ and $D_j=E_j\setminus\bigcup_{i<j}E_i$. Their union equals the original union and each $D_j\subseteq E_j$. Additivity and monotonicity give $P(\bigcup_jE_j)=\sum_jP(D_j)\le\sum_jP(E_j)$. The bound gives a conservative chance of any failure when marginal failure probabilities are known but their dependence is not. Values above one may be replaced by one; overlap can make the bound loose.

Problem 6 · Conditioning on the report mechanism

For two fair independent bits, compare information “the first bit is one” with “at least one bit is one.” Find the probability of two ones in each case and explain the difference.

Solution

The first event retains outcomes $10,11$, each with conditional probability $1/2$, so the answer is $1/2$. The second retains $01,10,11$, each with conditional probability $1/3$, so the answer is $1/3$. Both statements mention a one, but select different subsets of the original sample space. A report saying “I observed a one” is insufficient until the reporting rule specifies which event was learned.
