15. Joint distributions, marginals, and independence

Two binary monitors each raise an alert on half their observations. That statement leaves a decisive operational question unanswered: do they alert together, on complementary observations, or independently? The same individual behavior permits all three possibilities. A model for a vector must specify how its coordinates occur together, not just a separate distribution for each coordinate.

Let $X$ and $Y$ be real random variables on the same probability space. Their ordered pair $(X,Y)$ is a random vector. Its law assigns probabilities to regions of the plane. Neither variable has to cause the other; the pair simply records two numerical functions of the same underlying outcome.

A joint mass table contains information absent from its margins Lecture 40, 09:40

For discrete variables, define $p_{XY}(x_i,y_j)=P(X=x_i,Y=y_j)$. These are probabilities of intersections of events. Every entry is nonnegative and the sum of every entry is one. Values absent from the support have mass zero; a displayed table must not silently omit an outcome with positive mass.

For the two monitors, consider the following family, with $0\leq q\leq1/2$:

$p_{XY}$$Y=0$$Y=1$$p_X$
$X=0$$q$$1/2-q$$1/2$
$X=1$$1/2-q$$q$$1/2$
$p_Y$$1/2$$1/2$$1$

At $q=0$, exactly one monitor alerts. At $q=1/2$, the monitors always agree. At $q=1/4$, every pair has probability $1/4$. In every case each monitor separately is Bernoulli with probability $1/2$. Thus the marginals cannot determine the probability of a simultaneous alert, which is $q$.

Lecture 40, 16:23. A marginal law is obtained by forgetting one coordinate. The event $X=x_i$ is the disjoint union over $j$ of events $(X,Y)=(x_i,y_j)$, so countable additivity gives

$$p_X(x_i)=\sum_jp_{XY}(x_i,y_j),\qquad p_Y(y_j)=\sum_ip_{XY}(x_i,y_j).$$

The summed coordinate disappears; the coordinate held fixed remains the argument of the marginal. Summing along a row or column is merely the table implementation of this event identity. The same operation extends to any number of coordinates, summing over all coordinates one wants to remove. This is the sum rule in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, §6.3, printed p. 184.

A joint density weights area, including its support Lecture 40, 23:59

When the vector has a joint density $f_{XY}$ with respect to plane area, for a measurable region $A$ its probability is $\iint_A f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx\,dy$. The density must be nonnegative and integrate to one over the plane. Its height is not a point probability and need not be at most one. Its units are the inverse product of the units of $X$ and $Y$.

A small rectangle of side lengths $\Delta x,\Delta y$ around a point where the density is continuous has probability approximately $f_{XY}(x,y)\Delta x\Delta y$. Integrating the height over an area gives the volume under the density surface. A limiting local ratio can recover a density at regular points, but the integrated probability identity is the definition needed for calculations.

As an original example, choose a point uniformly in the triangular region $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$. Its area is $1/2$, so its density is two within the triangle and zero elsewhere. For fixed $x\in(0,1)$, the allowable values of $y$ run from zero to $x$. Therefore

$$f_X(x)=\int_0^x2\,dy=2x,\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_y^1 2\,dx=2(1-y),\quad 0\lt x,y\lt1.$$

Each marginal integrates to one. A uniform density over a region need not have uniform coordinate marginals: near $x=1$ there is more allowable vertical length than near zero. Ignoring the triangular support and integrating over the whole square would produce the wrong marginal and the wrong normalization.

The general marginal identities are $f_X(x)=\int_{\mathbb R}f_{XY}(x,y)\,dy$ and $f_Y(y)=\int_{\mathbb R}f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx$, valid almost everywhere. Nonnegativity permits these integrations and their interchange. These are probability-density versions of the same sum rule, rather than a new physical principle.

Continuous coordinate marginals do not guarantee a plane density. If $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$ and $X=Y=U$, both marginals are uniform, but the pair lies on the diagonal with probability one. A plane density would assign probability zero to that zero-area diagonal. This singular joint law still has a joint distribution and a joint CDF; it simply lacks the particular density representation.

A joint CDF works for discrete, dense, and singular laws Lecture 40, 33:12

Define $F_{XY}(a,b)=P(X\leq a,Y\leq b)$. It measures the lower-left rectangle ending at $(a,b)$, including its upper and right boundaries. It lies in $[0,1]$, is nondecreasing in each coordinate, and is right-continuous. Letting one coordinate tend to positive infinity gives the other marginal CDF: $F_X(a)=\lim_{b\to\infty}F_{XY}(a,b)$. Taking both thresholds to positive infinity gives one, while sending either threshold to negative infinity with the other fixed gives zero.

To extract a bounded rectangle, start with the large lower-left rectangle, remove its two unwanted strips, and restore their overlap:

$$P(a\lt X\leq b,c\lt Y\leq d)=F_{XY}(b,d)-F_{XY}(a,d)-F_{XY}(b,c)+F_{XY}(a,c).$$

The half-open boundary convention makes this true even at atoms. Every such alternating increment must be nonnegative. Coordinatewise monotonicity alone does not ensure this crucial property. When a joint density exists, the mixed derivative of the CDF recovers it almost everywhere under the corresponding integration result; an arbitrary joint law need not have such a derivative representation.

For the diagonal example $X=Y=U$, the CDF is $\max(0,\min(1,a,b))$. It completely describes a law that has no plane density. For the monitor table with $0\leq a,b\lt1$, the CDF equals $q$. CDFs can thus expose dependence even when all marginal CDFs agree.

Independence is an entire-law factorization Lecture 40, 20:54

Random variables are independent when, for all measurable sets $A,B$,

$$P(X\in A,Y\in B)=P(X\in A)P(Y\in B).$$

For discrete laws this is equivalent to $p_{XY}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ for every pair of possible values. Summing pointwise factorization over $A$ and $B$ proves the event criterion; choosing singleton sets proves the converse. For a joint density the analogous equality holds almost everywhere, and for CDFs the equivalent criterion is $F_{XY}(a,b)=F_X(a)F_Y(b)$ for every real $a,b$. Equality at a few tested thresholds is insufficient.

In the binary monitor family the product of the marginals is $1/4$ in every cell. Independence therefore holds exactly at $q=1/4$. In the triangular example, both marginals are positive throughout $(0,1)$, yet the joint density vanishes throughout the positive-area region $y\gt x$. It cannot equal their product, so the coordinates are dependent.

Independence means that observing one coordinate leaves the law of the other unchanged, wherever ordinary conditioning is defined. It does not mean complete ignorance of the other variable: its marginal law may already be highly concentrated. Nor does dependence require each possible observed value to change every event probability. Dependence says the complete factorization fails somewhere.

Joint expectations: linearity survives, products need a condition Lecture 41, 03:04

A real function $g(X,Y)$ has expectation

$$E[g(X,Y)]=\sum_{i,j}g(x_i,y_j)p_{XY}(x_i,y_j)$$

in the discrete case, or $\iint g(x,y)f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ in the density case, provided it is integrable, or as an extended nonnegative integral. There is no requirement to derive the law of $g(X,Y)$ first. For integrable $X,Y$, distribute the sum for $X+Y$ and sum out the other coordinate to obtain $E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]$. This proof explains why dependence cannot invalidate linearity.

Lecture 41, 12:12. Products behave differently. If $X,Y$ are independent and integrable, factorization gives $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$; absolute integrability of the product follows by applying the same nonnegative calculation to $|X||Y|$. More generally independent variables give $E[g(X)h(Y)]=E[g(X)]E[h(Y)]$ for integrable transformed variables. This entire collection of identities is much stronger than the one identity with $g(x)=x$ and $h(y)=y$.

A raw joint moment of orders $r,s$ is $E[X^rY^s]$ when it exists. Orders $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ recover the means; order $(1,1)$ is $E[XY]$. The lectures call this last quantity “correlation” in a signal-processing convention. To avoid ambiguity, these notes call it the raw cross moment and reserve Pearson correlation for the centered, normalized quantity introduced below.

Centering isolates joint fluctuations, not every kind of dependence Lecture 41, 23:44

Assume finite second moments and write $\mu_X=E[X],\mu_Y=E[Y]$. The $(1,1)$ central joint moment is

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y.$$

Expanding the square of the centered sum now gives $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$. If covariance is zero, the variables are uncorrelated. Independence implies zero covariance under these moment assumptions, but the converse fails.

For our monitors, the means are $1/2$, the raw cross moment is $q$, and covariance is $q-1/4$. Consequently $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=2q$. At $q=0$ the sum is exactly one, explaining its zero variance. At $q=1/2$ both coordinates fluctuate together and the sum has variance one. Treating the two equal marginal variances as sufficient to add variances would miss this full range of behavior.

Lecture 42, 04:57. For a counterexample to the converse, let $U$ take $-2,0,2$ with probabilities $1/3$ each and put $V=U^2$. Then $E[U]=0$, $E[V]=8/3$, and $E[UV]=E[U^3]=0$, giving zero covariance. Yet $V$ is completely determined by $U$. In particular $P(U=0,V=0)=1/3$, whereas $P(U=0)P(V=0)=1/9$. Positive and negative contributions cancel in one cross moment; they cannot cancel an entire-law dependence criterion.

Covariance has the product of the variables' units. With positive standard deviations, Pearson correlation is $\rho=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$, removing those units. It still measures only centered linear association. Chapter 16 proves its range, equality cases, and precise prediction interpretation. Zero raw cross moment, zero covariance, and independence are three distinct statements; centering is the bridge between the first two.

Executable check: coupling changes an aggregate while preserving marginals

from fractions import Fraction as F
for q in (F(0), F(1, 4), F(1, 2)):
    law = {(0, 0): q, (0, 1): F(1, 2)-q,
           (1, 0): F(1, 2)-q, (1, 1): q}
    ex = sum(x*p for (x, y), p in law.items())
    ey = sum(y*p for (x, y), p in law.items())
    cov = sum((x-ex)*(y-ey)*p for (x, y), p in law.items())
    varsum = sum((x+y-ex-ey)**2*p for (x, y), p in law.items())
    assert ex == ey == F(1, 2)
    assert varsum == 2*q
    print(f"q={q}: means=({ex},{ey}), covariance={cov}, "
          f"variance of sum={varsum}")

q=0: means=(1/2,1/2), covariance=-1/4, variance of sum=0
q=1/4: means=(1/2,1/2), covariance=0, variance of sum=1/2
q=1/2: means=(1/2,1/2), covariance=1/4, variance of sum=1

Six worked problems

1. Unequal marginal alert rates

If $P(X=1)=0.3$ and $P(Y=1)=0.6$, find every possible value of $q=P(X=1,Y=1)$ and identify independence.

Solution

The four cell masses are $q$, $0.3-q$, $0.6-q$, and $0.1+q$. Nonnegativity requires $0\leq q\leq0.3$, and every such value gives a normalized table with the desired margins. Independence occurs at $q=(0.3)(0.6)=0.18$. Thus marginal rates alone do not determine either simultaneous or exclusive alerts. The covariance is $q-0.18$.

2. A probability inside a triangular support

For density two on $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$, compute $P(X\gt3/4,Y\lt1/4)$ and compare with the product of marginal probabilities.

Solution

The specified rectangle lies entirely inside the triangular support, so its probability is $2(1/4)(1/4)=1/8$. The marginal probabilities are $P(X\gt3/4)=1-(3/4)^2=7/16$ and $P(Y\lt1/4)=2(1/4)-(1/4)^2=7/16$. Their product is $49/256$, different from $1/8$. This is an explicit event-level witness of dependence.

3. Why coordinate monotonicity of a CDF is insufficient

Could a bivariate CDF have values $F(0,0)=0$, $F(1,0)=0.6$, $F(0,1)=0.6$, and $F(1,1)=0.7$?

Solution

These four values respect coordinatewise monotonicity, but the rectangle $(0,1]\times(0,1]$ would have probability $0.7-0.6-0.6+0=-0.5$. No probability measure can assign negative mass. A joint CDF must also have nonnegative alternating increments for every rectangle, as well as boundary and continuity properties.

4. Recover dependence by changing the features

For the uncorrelated pair $U,V=U^2$ above, show that their squared-feature covariance is not zero.

Solution

Since $V=U^2$, $\operatorname{Cov}(U^2,V)=\operatorname{Var}(V)$. Here $E[V^2]=E[U^4]=32/3$, so the variance is $32/3-(8/3)^2=32/9\gt0$. A feature transformation can expose dependence canceled by the raw linear features. This does not make any single chosen transformed covariance a universal independence test.

5. Independence survives deterministic coordinate transformations

Prove that independent $X,Y$ give independent measurable transforms $g(X),h(Y)$, without assuming moments.

Solution

For measurable sets $A,B$, the relevant event is $\{X\in g^{-1}(A),Y\in h^{-1}(B)\}$. The preimages are measurable, so independence factors its probability into $P(g(X)\in A)P(h(Y)\in B)$. This proves the claim. It uses event probabilities, so neither a density nor a mean is needed. Moment identities are consequences only when the required expectations exist.

6. Orthogonal need not mean uncorrelated

Let $(X,Y)$ be $(1,0)$ or $(0,1)$ with probability $1/2$ each. Compute the raw cross moment and covariance, and interpret the result.

Solution

The product $XY$ is zero on both outcomes, so $E[XY]=0$: the variables are orthogonal in the uncentered second-moment geometry. Both means are $1/2$, hence covariance is $-1/4$. They are dependent because $Y=1-X$. Their centered fluctuations point in opposite directions. This illustrates why orthogonality of raw measurements and orthogonality of centered fluctuations must not be interchanged.
