21. Matrix derivatives and the geometry of differentials

A model can predict several quantities at once. Its coefficients then form a matrix, and a proposed update is another matrix of the same shape. The derivative must tell us the first-order loss change caused by that whole update. Writing each coefficient separately is possible, but a differential exposes the structure and makes incorrect transposes easier to detect.

21.1 A linear map is its own local derivative L55 · 04:10

Let $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$ be fixed and let $x\in\mathbb R^d$ be a column vector. The output $F(x)=Ax\in\mathbb R^m$ is a vector-valued function. The Jacobian has entry $\partial F_i/\partial x_j=A_{ij}$ because $F_i=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j$. Therefore $J_F=A$. The same conclusion follows without component derivatives: $F(x+h)-F(x)=Ah$ exactly, with zero approximation remainder.

For example, $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&-1\\3&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ maps two input coordinates to three outputs. Its Jacobian is $3\times2$, not a square matrix. A denominator-layout derivative table would display its transpose. The transformation itself has not changed; only the derivative's storage convention has. Matching the perturbation equation $dF=A\,dx$ is a reliable way to choose the intended orientation.

When the matrix is variable too, the differential is $d(Ax)=(dA)x+A\,dx$. The omitted finite-change term is $(\Delta A)(\Delta x)$, which is second order when both changes are small. Keeping track of which quantities are fixed prevents a common error: treating $A$ as a constant in one step and as a fitted parameter in the next without changing the derivative.

21.2 Squared length has a gradient, not a matrix Jacobian ambiguity L55 · 14:48

For $f(x)=x^\top x=\sum_jx_j^2$, expand $(x+h)^\top(x+h)=x^\top x+2x^\top h+h^\top h$. The first-order differential is $df=2x^\top dx$, so the column gradient is $\nabla_xf=2x$. Its scalar Jacobian is the row $2x^\top$. These are related representations of the same linear functional of perturbations.

The Hessian is $2I_d$, where $I_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity. It is positive definite: $h^\top(2I_d)h=2\|h\|_2^2\gt0$ for nonzero $h$. This establishes a unique minimum at zero. The factor of two changes if the objective is defined with a factor of one half. Objective normalizations leave minimizers unchanged but change gradient magnitudes and the meaning of an unchanged numerical step size.

21.3 Bilinear forms distinguish the two arguments L55 · 17:13

Let $y\in\mathbb R^m$, $x\in\mathbb R^d$, and $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$. The scalar $b(y,x)=y^\top Ax$ is linear in either vector when the other is fixed, and is therefore called a bilinear form in its two vector arguments. Its differential is

$$db=(dy)^\top Ax+y^\top A\,dx+y^\top(dA)x.$$

Holding $y,A$ fixed identifies $\nabla_xb=A^\top y\in\mathbb R^d$. Holding $x,A$ fixed gives $\nabla_yb=Ax\in\mathbb R^m$. The different shapes clarify why the transpose is necessary. A square matrix can conceal an incorrect transpose; a rectangular one cannot.

The form need not be a quadratic form in either vector separately. A quadratic form results when the two vector arguments are the same and the matrix is square. That substitution makes both appearances of the vector variable, so both routes contribute to the derivative. Substituting $y=x$ into a derivative calculated while holding $y$ fixed would omit one route.

21.4 A quadratic form sees only the symmetric matrix part L55 · 27:07

For $A\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$, let $q(x)=x^\top Ax$. Expanding a perturbation gives

$$q(x+h)-q(x)=h^\top Ax+x^\top Ah+h^\top Ah. $$

Transposing the scalar first term yields $h^\top Ax=x^\top A^\top h$. The linear coefficient is therefore $x^\top(A+A^\top)h$, giving $\nabla q=(A+A^\top)x$. If $A$ is symmetric, this reduces to $2Ax$. Without symmetry the simpler formula is generally wrong. The corresponding Hessian is $A+A^\top$.

Decompose $A=S+K$, where $S=(A+A^\top)/2$ is symmetric and $K=(A-A^\top)/2$ is skew-symmetric. Since the scalar $x^\top Kx$ equals its transpose $x^\top K^\top x=-x^\top Kx$, it must be zero. Thus $q(x)=x^\top Sx$: the skew-symmetric part contributes neither a quadratic value nor a derivative.

For $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\-2&4\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $x=(2,-1)^\top$, the correct gradient is $(3,-6)^\top$. Direct expansion gives $q=x_1^2+x_1x_2+4x_2^2$ and the same gradient $(2x_1+x_2,x_1+8x_2)^\top$. By contrast $2Ax=(-2,-16)^\top$, exposing the missing symmetry assumption. The general quadratic identity is recorded in Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong, §5.5, printed page 158.

21.5 A scalar function of a matrix has a same-shaped gradient

For real matrices $U,V$ of the same shape, their Frobenius inner product is $\langle U,V\rangle_F=\sum_{ij}U_{ij}V_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(U^\top V)$. The trace is the sum of a square matrix's diagonal entries. The associated norm is $\|U\|_F=\sqrt{\langle U,U\rangle_F}$.

For a differentiable scalar function $f(W)$ with $W\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$, define the gradient $G=\nabla_Wf$ by $df=\langle G,dW\rangle_F=\operatorname{tr}(G^\top dW)$. Each entry of $G$ is the partial derivative with respect to the matching entry of $W$. This definition is a local linear map on a vector space of matrices. It avoids forming an unnecessary large Jacobian when the output is scalar.

A matrix-valued function requires more indices. If $B(W)$ has entries $B_{ij}$, its full derivative contains $\partial B_{ij}/\partial W_{kl}$. It is a four-index array or, after choosing an ordering of entries, an ordinary matrix between flattened vectors. Flattening is a representation choice, not a proof that every derivative naturally has only two indices. This distinction is developed in the inspected §5.4, printed pages 155–158.

The trace identity $\operatorname{tr}(UV)=\operatorname{tr}(VU)$ for compatible rectangular products follows by summing $\sum_{ij}U_{ij}V_{ji}$ in the opposite order. It permits cyclic rearrangements of a product whose dimensions match. It does not permit arbitrary swaps of factors: matrix multiplication remains noncommutative.

21.6 Multioutput least squares follows the same perturbation argument

Let $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ contain one observation per row, let $W\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$ contain coefficients for $r$ outputs, and let $Y\in\mathbb R^{n\times r}$ contain targets. Define residual matrix $R=XW-Y$ and objective $L(W)=\|R\|_F^2/2$. For an update $D$ of the same shape as $W$,

$$L(W+D)-L(W)=\langle R,XD\rangle_F+\frac12\|XD\|_F^2. $$

Now $\langle R,XD\rangle_F=\operatorname{tr}(R^\top XD)=\operatorname{tr}((X^\top R)^\top D)$. Comparing with the gradient definition gives $\nabla_WL=X^\top(XW-Y)$. The product has shape $d\times r$, matching $W$. Setting this to zero gives one common normal-equation matrix for all output columns.

If $X$ has full column rank, each output's coefficient vector is unique. If not, adding any matrix $N$ with $XN=0$ leaves every fitted output unchanged. The derivative identifies the ambiguity but does not choose among those coefficients. A penalty such as $\lambda\|W\|_F^2/2$ with $\lambda\gt0$ adds gradient $\lambda W$ and selects a unique solution, because $X^\top X+\lambda I_d$ is positive definite. The penalty is an extra modeling preference, not information recovered from missing data directions.

21.7 Test a matrix gradient with a directional perturbation

The following NumPy program uses a rectangular design and two outputs. It changes no files and makes no network requests. It compares the directional derivative from the complete matrix gradient with an independent central difference. A separate nonsymmetric quadratic check prevents an accidental symmetry from hiding a transpose error.

import numpy as np


def objective(W):
    return np.sum((X @ W - Y)**2) / 2


X = np.array([[1., 0.], [1., 1.], [1., 2.]])
Y = np.array([[1., 0.], [2., 1.], [2., 3.]])
W = np.array([[0.5, -1.], [1., 0.5]])
D = np.array([[0.2, -0.3], [0.4, 0.1]])
G = X.T @ (X @ W - Y)
h = 1e-5
finite = (objective(W + h*D) - objective(W - h*D)) / (2*h)
analytic = np.sum(G*D)
assert np.isclose(finite, analytic)
A = np.array([[1., 3.], [-2., 4.]])
x = np.array([2., -1.])
quadratic_grad = (A + A.T) @ x
assert np.allclose(quadratic_grad, [3., -6.])
print("matrix gradient:\n", G, sep="")
print(f"directional analytic={analytic:.6f} finite={finite:.6f}")
print("nonsymmetric quadratic gradient:", quadratic_grad)

matrix gradient:
[[-0.5 -5.5]
 [ 0.5 -7.5]]
directional analytic=1.000000 finite=1.000000
nonsymmetric quadratic gradient: [ 3. -6.]

The directional check tests the contraction with one perturbation. A wrong gradient could accidentally have the correct contraction in that direction, so a fuller check tests a basis of directions or several independent directions. Agreement within a finite-difference tolerance is implementation evidence; the symbolic perturbation identity supplies the general proof.

21.8 Exercises and worked solutions

Problem 1 · Derivative with respect to the matrix

For $f(A)=y^\top Ax$ with fixed vectors, derive the Frobenius gradient with respect to $A$.

Solution

The differential is $df=y^\top(dA)x=\sum_{ij}y_i x_j\,dA_{ij}$. The coefficient matrix has entries $y_ix_j$, so $\nabla_Af=yx^\top$. It has the same $m\times d$ shape as $A$. Its transpose inside the trace gives $\operatorname{tr}(xy^\top dA)=y^\top(dA)x$, confirming the convention.

Problem 2 · Recover a quadratic's hidden part

Suppose $x^\top Ax=x^\top Bx$ for every real vector $x$. Prove that $A$ and $B$ have the same symmetric part, and explain the remaining freedom.

Solution

Let $S$ be the symmetric part of $A-B$. Then $x^\top Sx=0$ for every $x$. Taking coordinate vectors shows $S_{ii}=0$. Taking $e_i+e_j$ gives $S_{ii}+2S_{ij}+S_{jj}=0$, hence $S_{ij}=0$. Thus $S=0$. Any skew-symmetric difference is invisible to the quadratic form, because its scalar contribution changes sign under transposition and therefore vanishes.

Problem 3 · Weighted residuals

For symmetric positive-definite $C\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$, derive the gradient of $L(W)=\operatorname{tr}((XW-Y)^\top C(XW-Y))/2$.

Solution

Write $R=XW-Y$ and $dR=X\,dW$. The differential is $[\operatorname{tr}((dR)^\top CR)+\operatorname{tr}(R^\top C\,dR)]/2$. Symmetry makes the two scalar terms equal. Substituting $dR$ yields $dL=\operatorname{tr}(R^\top CX\,dW)$, so the gradient is $X^\top CR$. If $C$ were not symmetric, the gradient would instead use its symmetric part; the positivity assumption also makes the objective a weighted squared norm.

Problem 4 · A matrix-valued derivative

Let $B(W)=W^\top W$. Derive its differential and show why replacing it by $2W$ is not a complete derivative formula.

Solution

Expansion gives $B(W+D)-B(W)=D^\top W+W^\top D+D^\top D$. Thus $dB=(dW)^\top W+W^\top dW$. The output is a matrix, and the derivative maps a matrix perturbation to another matrix. The expression $2W$ alone does not specify that action or its four-index entries. Only after contracting with a scalar objective, such as $\operatorname{tr}(B)$, do we obtain the same-shaped scalar gradient $2W$.

Problem 5 · Diagnose a broadcast error

A one-output implementation uses a prediction array of shape $(n,)$ and a target array of shape $(n,1)$. Why can subtraction return the wrong objective without raising an exception?

Solution

Array broadcasting can align the flat prediction as a row and the target as a column, producing an $n\times n$ array of pairwise differences. The intended residual is one matched difference per observation. Summing the broadcast square optimizes comparisons between every prediction and every target instead. Require identical intended target and prediction shapes, either both flat or both explicit columns, before subtracting. A running program is not evidence that its mathematical dimensions are correct.

Problem 6 · How many gradient checks?

Can one directional finite-difference agreement certify a matrix gradient? Construct a counterexample and propose a stronger test.

Solution

For an update direction $D$, any proposed gradient $G+E$ with $\langle E,D\rangle_F=0$ gives the same directional prediction as $G$. In a matrix space with more than one degree of freedom, many nonzero such $E$ exist. Testing every coordinate matrix isolates every gradient entry and detects an error up to numerical tolerance. Random directions are cheaper and make accidental orthogonality less likely, but finitely many numerical tests remain evidence for selected inputs rather than a universal proof.

The scalar differential method represents first-order changes in a specified real inner product. Complex parameters require conjugation conventions. Nonsmooth operations need a separate treatment, and finite-difference tests need an appropriate numerical scale. These derivative formulas describe the training objective, not the reliability of its future predictions.
