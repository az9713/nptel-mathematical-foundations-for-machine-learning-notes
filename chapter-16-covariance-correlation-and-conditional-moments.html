16. Covariance, correlation, and conditional moments

A prediction system observes one measurement $X$ before predicting another measurement $Y$. Correlation can tell us how much a best affine predictor improves on a constant prediction. A conditional distribution can answer a richer question: after the particular value $X=x$ is observed, what outcomes of $Y$ remain plausible? Confusing these two objects hides nonlinear information and can turn a useful summary into a false independence claim.

Unless stated otherwise, moment calculations below assume finite second moments. Write $\mu_X=E[X]$, $\sigma_X^2=\operatorname{Var}(X)$, and similarly for $Y$. Pearson correlation additionally requires $\sigma_X,\sigma_Y\gt0$.

Correlation is the angle between centered fluctuations Lecture 43, 07:24

Standardize the two variables as $Z_X=(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $Z_Y=(Y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$. Each has mean zero and second moment one. Standardization does not make an arbitrary law normal: a standardized Bernoulli variable still has only two possible values.

The Pearson correlation coefficient is

$$\rho_{XY}=E[Z_XZ_Y]=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}.$$

It is dimensionless because numerator and denominator have the same product of units. A covariance numerical value changes when measurement units change; this is mathematically appropriate, but complicates comparisons of linear association across scales. For nonzero constants $a,c$, covariance transforms as $\operatorname{Cov}(aX+b,cY+d)=ac\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$, while correlation transforms as $\operatorname{sign}(ac)\rho_{XY}$. Positive changes of units preserve its value; reversing one axis reverses its sign.

In the space of square-integrable real random variables, identify variables equal almost surely and define $\langle U,V\rangle=E[UV]$. This is an inner product: bilinearity and symmetry follow from expectation, and $E[U^2]=0$ implies $U=0$ almost surely. For centered variables the inner product is covariance, and the corresponding length is standard deviation. Thus correlation is the cosine of the angle between centered variables. The almost-sure identification is necessary for a positive-definite inner product; changing a function on a null set does not change its length. See Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, §6.4.6, printed pp. 195–196.

Lecture 43, 09:42. Nonnegativity alone proves the correlation range: $E[(Z_X-Z_Y)^2]=2-2\rho\geq0$ and $E[(Z_X+Z_Y)^2]=2+2\rho\geq0$. Therefore $-1\leq\rho\leq1$, the Cauchy–Schwarz bound in normalized form. Equality has a precise meaning: $\rho=1$ exactly when $Z_Y=Z_X$ almost surely, and $\rho=-1$ exactly when $Z_Y=-Z_X$ almost surely. Consequently perfect correlation requires an affine relation with positive slope, while perfect negative correlation requires one with negative slope. Merely tending to increase together does not imply $\rho=1$.

Uncorrelated centered variables are orthogonal, and the Pythagorean identity gives $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2$ when covariance is zero. Independence is sufficient but stronger than needed. If either variable is almost surely constant, its covariance with every finite-variance variable is zero, but Pearson correlation is undefined because its denominator vanishes.

The exact prediction meaning of a linear association

Consider predictors $\widehat Y=a+bX$. For a fixed slope $b$, Chapter 14's mean argument sets the best intercept to $a=\mu_Y-b\mu_X$. Centering then reduces the expected squared loss to

$$E[(Y-\widehat Y)^2]=\sigma_Y^2-2b\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+b^2\sigma_X^2.$$

Completing the square gives $b_*={\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}/{\sigma_X^2}$ and minimum loss $\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)$. The residual $Y-\mu_Y-b_*(X-\mu_X)$ has zero covariance with $X$, exactly the normal equation for an orthogonal projection. This is an original derivation explaining the lectures' phrase “linear relationship.” At zero correlation, an affine predictor cannot improve on the best constant. A nonlinear predictor may still improve dramatically.

For instance, take $X$ uniform on $[-1,1]$ and $Y=X^2$. Symmetry gives $E[X]=E[X^3]=0$, so covariance is zero. Nevertheless $X$ predicts $Y$ exactly by squaring it. The best affine predictor is the constant $E[Y]=1/3$, with error $E[X^4]-1/9=1/5-1/9=4/45$. Correlation misses this dependence by cancellation, not because every nonlinear relationship must have zero correlation.

The Gaussian exception requires a joint Gaussian law Lecture 43, 22:14

A jointly Gaussian vector is one for which every real linear combination of the coordinates is a univariate Gaussian, allowing degenerate constants. For a nonsingular two-dimensional Gaussian with positive standard deviations and $|\rho|\lt1$, let $u=(x-\mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $v=(y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$. Its density is

$$f_{XY}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_X\sigma_Y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{u^2-2\rho uv+v^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\right].$$

The means set its center, the marginal variances set its scales, and correlation sets the cross term. This is the two-dimensional form of the Gaussian density inspected in §6.5, printed pp. 198–199. Setting $\rho=0$ separates both normalization and exponential into the product of two marginal normal densities. Hence jointly Gaussian uncorrelated coordinates are independent. The density formula excludes $|\rho|=1$, where the law lies on an affine line; it cannot be evaluated there by division by zero.

Two individually normal variables need not be jointly normal. Let $Z$ be standard normal, let $S$ be an independent random sign with equal probabilities, and put $X=Z$, $Y=SZ$. Symmetry makes both marginals standard normal and $E[XY]=E[S]E[Z^2]=0$. But $|X|=|Y|$ surely, and the joint law lies on two lines. The Gaussian independence conclusion is therefore unavailable. Normal-looking marginal histograms cannot establish its joint hypothesis.

Lecture 43, 36:15. Correlation also does not identify a causal direction. As a mathematical illustration, suppose $H,\epsilon,\eta$ are independent random signs and define $A=H+\epsilon$, $B=H+\eta$. Their covariance is one and both variances are two, so correlation is $1/2$. Both respond to the shared input $H$. Under the stipulated structural model, replacing the equation for $A$ by a fixed assigned value changes no term in the equation for $B$. The observed correlation alone could not establish an effect of intervening on $A$.

A conditional distribution is a normalized slice Lecture 44, 29:54

For discrete variables and a value $x$ with $p_X(x)\gt0$,

$$p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{p_{XY}(x,y)}{p_X(x)}.$$

Hold the observed coordinate $x$ fixed, take its row of joint masses, and divide by their sum. All entries are nonnegative and summing over $y$ gives one. Varying $x$ produces a family of distributions, not one probability table normalized over both arguments. Summing over the conditioning values without weighting by $p_X(x)$ generally gives no normalization identity. Values $x$ with zero marginal mass do not define this ratio.

Lecture 44, 35:54. For a vector with joint density, a conditional density version is $f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=f_{XY}(x,y)/f_X(x)$ for almost every $x$ with $f_X(x)\gt0$. Its integral over $y$ is one. This is not the elementary event ratio $P(Y\in B,X=x)/P(X=x)$, whose denominator is zero for a continuous coordinate. Rather, the density ratio supplies conditional laws consistent with integration over positive-probability sets of $X$. Versions at exceptional conditioning values need not be unique.

Return to the triangular density two on $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$. Since $f_X(x)=2x$, the conditional density is $1/x$ on $0\lt y\lt x$ for $0\lt x\lt1$. Thus, after observing $X=x$, $Y$ is uniform on an interval whose endpoint is the observation. Its conditional mean is $x/2$ and variance $x^2/12$. Marginally $Y$ has decreasing density $2(1-y)$ on $(0,1)$; a conditional slice and a marginal projection are different operations.

Conditioning on an event $B$ with $P(B)\gt0$ is also legitimate: $P(Y\in A\mid B)=P(\{Y\in A\}\cap B)/P(B)$. Observing a coarse event such as $X\gt1/2$ averages the appropriate slices with their conditional weights. It does not select the slice at $x=1/2$ or make all remaining $x$ values equally likely.

A latent choice produces a mixture, and Bayes reverses it Lecture 44, 11:31

Choose one of two devices with equal probabilities. Device L succeeds with probability $1/4$ per test; device H succeeds with probability $3/4$. Conditional on the chosen device, run three independent tests and let $K$ count successes. The conditional laws are binomial, but the unconditional law is their mixture:

$$P(K=k)=\tfrac12\binom3k(1/4)^k(3/4)^{3-k}+\tfrac12\binom3k(3/4)^k(1/4)^{3-k},\quad k=0,1,2,3.$$

For L, the probability of at least two successes is $5/32$; for H it is $27/32$. Averaging gives $P(K\geq2)=1/2$. Bayes' rule therefore gives $P(H\mid K\geq2)=(\tfrac12\cdot27/32)/(1/2)=27/32$. The threshold and number of trials stay fixed throughout this calculation; changing “more than” to “at least” changes an event in a discrete model.

Lecture 44, 39:44. In general, a discrete hidden class $C$ and observation $x$ obey $P(C=c\mid X=x)=p_{X\mid C}(x\mid c)P(C=c)/\sum_jp_{X\mid C}(x\mid j)P(C=j)$, with positive denominator. Replace mass likelihoods by densities for a continuous observation; the class probabilities still sum, because the hidden class is discrete. With a continuous hidden parameter the corresponding denominator is an integral. This sum-versus-integral choice follows the variable being marginalized, not the notation used for the observation.

Conditional means separate explained and remaining variation

The conditional laws permit $m(x)=E[Y\mid X=x]$ and $v(x)=E[(Y-m(x))^2\mid X=x]$. In the discrete case, expand $\sum_xp_X(x)\sum_y y p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)$ to recover $E[Y]$. The density case uses the same integrable joint calculation. Thus $E[m(X)]=E[Y]$, the law of total expectation.

Write $Y-E[Y]=(Y-m(X))+(m(X)-E[Y])$. Conditional on $X$, the first summand has mean zero, making the expected cross term zero. Squaring and averaging proves

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y)=E[v(X)]+\operatorname{Var}(m(X)).$$

This reference-supported extension is the total-variance identity discussed in §6.5.3, printed p. 203. The lectures introduce conditional laws but do not develop a general conditional-expectation theorem. Here the law-based derivation suffices. Similarly, for any square-integrable predictor $g(X)$, $E[(Y-g(X))^2]=E[v(X)]+E[(m(X)-g(X))^2]$. Consequently the conditional mean is the best squared-loss predictor among functions of $X$, uniquely up to almost-sure equality.

In the triangle, $E[X^2]=\int_0^1 2x^3dx=1/2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=1/18$. Hence $E[v(X)]=1/24$ and $\operatorname{Var}(m(X))=1/72$, summing to $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1/18$. Conditional means explain part of the variation, but knowing $X$ does not eliminate the remaining uniform uncertainty.

Executable check: conditional independence can disappear in a mixture

from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb
ps = [F(1, 4), F(3, 4)]
laws = [{k: F(comb(3, k))*p**k*(1-p)**(3-k)
         for k in range(4)} for p in ps]
tails = [sum(law[k] for k in (2, 3)) for law in laws]
mixture = {k: sum(law[k] for law in laws)/2 for k in range(4)}
mean = sum(k*p for k, p in mixture.items())
variance = sum((k-mean)**2*p for k, p in mixture.items())
posterior = tails[1]/sum(tails)
assert sum(mixture.values()) == 1
assert variance == F(9, 8)
print("conditional tail probabilities:", ", ".join(map(str, tails)))
print("posterior high-success device:", posterior)
print("mixture count mean and variance:", mean, variance)
print("single-binomial variance would be:", 3*F(1, 2)*F(1, 2))

conditional tail probabilities: 5/32, 27/32
posterior high-success device: 27/32
mixture count mean and variance: 3/2 9/8
single-binomial variance would be: 3/4

Six worked problems

1. Perfect negative correlation

If $\sigma_X=2$, $\sigma_Y=5$, $\mu_X=1$, $\mu_Y=7$, and $\rho=-1$, determine $Y$ as a function of $X$.

Solution

Equality implies $(Y-7)/5=-(X-1)/2$ almost surely. Therefore $Y=19/2-(5/2)X$. Covariance is $-10$. The relation is exact almost surely, not merely a tendency for large $X$ to accompany small $Y$.

2. A conditional triangle calculation

For the triangular law, compute $P(Y\lt1/4\mid X=3/4)$ using its conditional density version, and compare with the marginal probability.

Solution

The conditional density is uniform on $(0,3/4)$, so the probability is $(1/4)/(3/4)=1/3$. The marginal probability is $\int_0^{1/4}2(1-y)dy=7/16$. This is a conditional-density calculation at an interior regular point, not division by $P(X=3/4)$.

3. Dependence from a shared device

For the device mixture, let $I_1,I_2$ indicate success on the first two tests. Compute their unconditional covariance and the count variance by total variance.

Solution

Conditional independence gives $E[I_1I_2\mid C]=p_C^2$, hence $E[I_1I_2]=(1/16+9/16)/2=5/16$. Each marginal mean is $1/2$, so covariance is $1/16$ and correlation is $1/4$. Also $E[\operatorname{Var}(K\mid C)]=3(3/16)=9/16$, while $\operatorname{Var}(E[K\mid C])=\operatorname{Var}(3p_C)=9/16$. Their sum is $9/8$, larger than the variance of a binomial with parameter $1/2$. Conditional independence does not imply unconditional independence.

4. Gaussian conditional prediction

A jointly Gaussian pair has means $(2,-1)$, standard deviations $(2,3)$ and correlation $1/2$. Find the conditional mean and variance of $Y$ given $X=x$.

Solution

Complete the square $u^2-2\rho uv+v^2=(v-\rho u)^2+(1-\rho^2)u^2$ in the joint density and divide by the marginal density of $X$. The resulting normal law has mean $-1+\rho(3/2)(x-2)=-1+(3/4)(x-2)$ and variance $9(1-1/4)=27/4$. Its conditional mean equals the best affine predictor; that equality is a property of the jointly Gaussian law, not of all variables with the same covariance.

5. A conditional table is not a joint table

Suppose $P(Y=1\mid X=0)=0.2$ and $P(Y=1\mid X=1)=0.7$. Is $P(Y=1)$ determined? What further information is needed?

Solution

If $r=P(X=1)$, total probability gives $P(Y=1)=0.2(1-r)+0.7r=0.2+0.5r$. Without $r$ the answer can range from $0.2$ to $0.7$, with endpoint conditional versions assigned on null values if needed. The two entries sum to $0.9$, but no rule requires a sum of one across conditioning values. A marginal weighting is needed to construct the joint law.

6. Diagnose a normality shortcut

A report finds normal marginal distributions and zero correlation, and therefore removes one feature as independent of the other. Is the conclusion justified?

Solution

No. The random-sign construction $X=Z$, $Y=SZ$ has both properties yet $Y^2=X^2$ surely. For example the events $|X|\leq1$ and $|Y|\leq1$ are identical and have a probability strictly between zero and one; their intersection probability cannot equal the square of that probability. Joint Gaussianity would make zero covariance sufficient, but normal marginals alone do not establish it. Even independence would not by itself show that a feature has no information about a separate prediction target.
