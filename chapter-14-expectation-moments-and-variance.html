14. Expectation, moments, and variance

A service promises an average response time of two seconds. One implementation always takes two seconds; another takes either zero or four seconds with equal probability. Their promises agree, but a user facing a three-second deadline experiences different services. A probability law answers every measurable question about response time. An expectation compresses that law into one number, and variance restores one particular aspect of the information lost.

Throughout this chapter $X$ is a real random variable. We distinguish a population expectation, computed from its law, from the arithmetic average of observed values. They are related by sampling theorems, not by definition.

Expectation is a weighted sum, with an existence condition Lecture 38, 07:19

If $X$ takes distinct values $x_i$ with probabilities $p_i$, its expectation is $E[X]=\sum_i x_i p_i$. If it has density $f_X$, its expectation is $E[X]=\int_{\mathbb R}x f_X(x)\,dx$. These expressions average values using probability weights; an unweighted average of possible values is justified only when those values have equal mass.

For a finite, unambiguous real expectation we require integrability: $E[|X|]\lt\infty$. More generally the expectation of a nonnegative variable can be $+\infty$. For a signed variable write $X=X^+-X^-$, where $X^+=\max(X,0)$ and $X^-=\max(-X,0)$. The expression $E[X^+]-E[X^-]$ is undefined when both terms are infinite. An arrangement of positive and negative terms cannot repair that failure.

The same rule computes an expectation of a measurable transformation without first deriving its distribution:

$$E[g(X)]=\sum_i g(x_i)p_i\quad\text{or}\quad E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx,$$

provided $g(X)$ is integrable, or with an extended nonnegative expectation when appropriate. Several outcomes can give the same transformed value. Their probability weights are simply added. This is the expectation rule presented in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, §6.4.1, printed pp. 187–189; the existence conditions are made explicit here because infinite supports require them.

Consider a law with $P(X=2^k)=2^{-k}$ for integers $k\geq1$. Its masses sum to one, but $E[X]=\sum_{k\geq1}1=\infty$. Having a valid distribution does not guarantee a finite mean. A more subtle failure occurs for density $1/[\pi(1+x^2)]$: each of the positive and negative first-moment integrals diverges logarithmically. Symmetric truncation gives zero, but that principal value is not an expectation. Symmetry alone therefore does not establish a finite mean.

Linearity does not require independence Lecture 38, 29:40

For integrable $X,Y$ and real constants $a,b,c$, linearity of sums or integrals gives $E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c$. Both variables may depend on exactly the same outcome. Independence is irrelevant to this identity. It will matter when we try to replace an expectation of a product by a product of expectations.

An especially useful variable is the indicator $1_A$, equal to one on event $A$ and zero otherwise. Directly, $E[1_A]=P(A)$. If $N=\sum_{i=1}^n1_{A_i}$ counts occurrences, then $E[N]=\sum_iP(A_i)$, even when the events overlap or influence one another. Thus a binomial count has mean $np$, but the same mean holds for any sum of $n$ Bernoulli variables with common success probability $p$. The binomial law requires more structure than its mean does.

For the two service implementations, $E[X]=2$ in either case. The mean need not be a possible outcome: the second service never takes two seconds. It also need not be the most probable outcome or a median. Those are different summaries, each answering a different question. Interpreting expectation as a long-run sample average requires assumptions on the sequence sampled; Chapter 17 supplies a finite-variance iid result.

There is also a finite-dimensional geometric analogy. For observations $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^T$, the least-squares projection onto the line spanned by the all-ones vector $\mathbf1$ is $\bar x\mathbf1$, since its coefficient is $(\mathbf1^Tx)/(\mathbf1^T\mathbf1)=\bar x$. The scalar average is the projection coefficient, not the projected vector. Population expectation has a related weighted geometry, developed further in Chapter 16.

Why the mean is the best constant under squared loss Lecture 38, 33:55

Suppose a predictor must output the same constant $c$ before seeing the service response. Specify its objective: minimize the expected squared error $E[(X-c)^2]$. Assume $E[X^2]\lt\infty$, which also ensures a finite mean $\mu=E[X]$. Write $X-c=(X-\mu)+(\mu-c)$, expand, and use $E[X-\mu]=0$:

$$E[(X-c)^2]=E[(X-\mu)^2]+(\mu-c)^2.$$

The first term does not depend on $c$, while the second is nonnegative and vanishes only at $c=\mu$. This proves optimality and uniqueness without differentiation. It explains why a conditional mean will later be optimal when a predictor can use observed information.

The conclusion depends on the loss. Absolute error favors medians; a deadline penalty depends on a tail probability. For a law that is zero with probability $0.9$ and ten with probability $0.1$, the mean is one. Squared error at one is nine, compared with ten at zero. Absolute error at one is $1.8$, compared with one at zero. Calling the mean the universally “best prediction” would hide the decision being optimized.

Raw and central moments measure different things Lecture 39, 02:06

For a positive integer $k$, the $k$th moment about a fixed real number $c$ is $E[(X-c)^k]$, when defined. The raw moment uses $c=0$; a central moment uses $c=\mu$. The first raw moment is the mean. The first central moment is always zero for an integrable variable, so it cannot quantify spread: positive and negative deviations cancel.

The second central moment avoids cancellation because it squares every deviation. It is the variance, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]$. Its nonnegative square root $\sigma$ is the standard deviation. If response time is measured in seconds, the variance has units seconds squared and $\sigma$ has units seconds. Higher central moments describe additional shape, but can fail to exist even when variance exists; a finite list does not recover an arbitrary law.

A shift $Y=X+b$ changes the mean to $\mu+b$ but leaves every defined central moment unchanged, because $Y-E[Y]=X-\mu$. Raw moments generally change. Variance is therefore a measure of fluctuations about a location, not a measure of that location itself.

For the constant service, variance is zero. For the zero-or-four service, each squared deviation from two is four, giving variance four. Zero variance means more than a zero average deviation: it means $X=\mu$ almost surely. To prove this, for every $\epsilon\gt0$, nonnegativity gives $E[(X-\mu)^2]\geq\epsilon^2P(|X-\mu|\geq\epsilon)$. If variance is zero, every such probability is zero; the union over $\epsilon=1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$ shows $P(X\ne\mu)=0$. Values on probability-zero sets can still differ.

Variance identities, distribution formulas, and numerical limits Lecture 39, 22:09

With a finite second moment, expanding the square yields

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-\mu^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X).$$

The second identity follows more transparently by centering first: $aX+b-E[aX+b]=a(X-\mu)$. It includes $a=0$. Variance is not a linear operator: $\operatorname{Var}(X+X)=4\operatorname{Var}(X)$, not $2\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Variances of independent sums do add, as shown using cross terms in Chapter 16.

The distinction between raw second moment and variance is also the distinction between total mean-square signal amplitude and fluctuation power. The identity $E[X^2]=\mu^2+\sigma^2$ includes the contribution of a nonzero constant component. Identifying variance with total power requires a zero-mean signal and the relevant probabilistic interpretation.

These formulas concern the stated models, not generic variables with the same name. For Bernoulli, $X^2=X$, immediately giving $p-p^2$. For an exponential, integration by parts gives $E[X]=\int_0^\infty xr e^{-rx}dx=1/r$, and a second application gives $E[X^2]=2/r^2$. Rate has inverse-time units, so the mean must have time units. The geometric mean $1/p$ is an average over repetitions, not a minimum number of trials or a guaranteed waiting time. The first trial can succeed for every $p\gt0$.

For a standard normal $Z$, odd symmetry gives $E[Z]=0$, and integration by parts using $\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)$ gives $E[Z^2]=[-z\phi(z)]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int\phi(z)dz=1$. Substituting $X=\mu+\sigma Z$ proves the displayed normal moments. The remaining scalar formulas are derived in the problems or follow from the independent-sum identity in Chapter 16.

The identity $E[X^2]-E[X]^2$ is exact mathematics, but subtracting nearly equal large floating-point numbers can lose most significant digits. Centered accumulation is generally preferable for computing a small spread around a large offset. A finite data file also always has finite computed moments; that fact cannot prove that its generating population has finite moments.

Executable check: the same moments can hide a deadline risk

Two exact rational laws below both have mean two and variance one. The first places some mass above three seconds; the second places none. The program checks normalization, moment equality, and the deadline probabilities without sampling error.

from fractions import Fraction as F


def summarize(law):
    assert sum(law.values()) == 1
    mean = sum(F(x)*p for x, p in law.items())
    variance = sum((F(x)-mean)**2*p for x, p in law.items())
    late = sum(p for x, p in law.items() if x > 3)
    return mean, variance, late


laws = [{0: F(1, 8), 2: F(3, 4), 4: F(1, 8)},
        {1: F(1, 2), 3: F(1, 2)}]
for name, law in zip("AB", laws):
    mean, variance, late = summarize(law)
    print(f"{name}: mean={mean}, variance={variance}, P(X>3)={late}")
assert summarize(laws[0])[:2] == summarize(laws[1])[:2]

A: mean=2, variance=1, P(X>3)=1/8
B: mean=2, variance=1, P(X>3)=0

Six worked problems

1. Geometric moments without a remembered formula

For $P(G=k)=p(1-p)^{k-1}$ on $k\geq1$, derive its first two moments and variance, including $p=1$.

Solution

Write $q=1-p$. For $0\leq q\lt1$, differentiate the convergent geometric series to obtain $\sum_{k\geq1}kq^{k-1}=(1-q)^{-2}$. Hence $E[G]=p/p^2=1/p$. Differentiating again gives $\sum k(k-1)q^{k-2}=2/(1-q)^3$, so $E[G(G-1)]=2q/p^2$. Thus $E[G^2]=(2-p)/p^2$ and variance is $(1-p)/p^2$. For $p=1$, $G=1$ surely and the same formulas hold. For $p=0$, a finite-valued geometric waiting time is not normalized; the formulas are not applicable.

2. Uniform variance by centering

Derive the uniform-interval variance without subtracting two raw moments.

Solution

Let $m=(a+b)/2$ and $h=(b-a)/2$. For $U$ uniform on $[a,b]$, symmetry gives mean $m$, and $U-m$ is uniform on $[-h,h]$. Thus $\operatorname{Var}(U)=(2h)^{-1}\int_{-h}^{h}u^2du=h^2/3=(b-a)^2/12$. If the interval shrinks to a point, the limiting law is a point mass with variance zero, rather than an ordinary interval density with a zero denominator.

3. Poisson factorial moments

Use the Poisson mass function to derive its mean and variance.

Solution

For $\lambda\gt0$, $E[N]=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k\geq1}k\lambda^k/k!=\lambda e^{-\lambda}\sum_{j\geq0}\lambda^j/j!=\lambda$. Similarly $E[N(N-1)]=\lambda^2$. Since $N^2=N(N-1)+N$, its second raw moment is $\lambda^2+\lambda$ and its variance is $\lambda$. At $\lambda=0$ the law is concentrated at zero and the results remain valid.

4. A finite mean and infinite variance

Let $X$ have density $2/x^3$ for $x\geq1$, zero elsewhere. Verify normalization and diagnose which squared-loss claims apply.

Solution

The density integrates to $[-x^{-2}]_1^\infty=1$. Its mean is $2\int_1^\infty x^{-2}dx=2$, but $E[X^2]=2\int_1^\infty x^{-1}dx=\infty$. For any fixed $c$, beyond $x\geq\max(1,2|c|)$, $(x-c)^2\geq x^2/4$, so expected squared error is infinite. The finite-loss minimization theorem therefore does not select a uniquely best constant here. A finite mean alone is insufficient.

5. Unit conversion and a misleading comparison

A response has mean two seconds and variance $0.25$ seconds squared. Convert to milliseconds. Does the larger numerical variance mean the service became less stable?

Solution

For $Y=1000X$, mean is $2000$ milliseconds, variance is $10^6(0.25)=250000$ milliseconds squared, and standard deviation is $500$ milliseconds. No physical property changed. A dimensionless coefficient of variation, when mean is positive, is $\sigma/\mu=0.25$ in either unit system. This ratio is a chosen summary and can be inappropriate near zero or for signed quantities.

6. Design a deadline monitor

Using the two laws in the executable check, determine the best constant squared-loss prediction and propose a statistic that distinguishes their deadline performance.

Solution

Both have $\mu=2$ and variance one, so both are best predicted by two under squared loss and both attain minimum expected squared loss one. The event $X\gt3$ has probability $1/8$ for A and zero for B. Monitoring that indicator, or estimating the corresponding tail probability, distinguishes this deadline risk. Neither mean nor variance alone determines it. The construction also disproves a general claim that two moments completely specify a distribution, although within an assumed normal family the two parameters do specify its law.
